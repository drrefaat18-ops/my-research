## 4.1. Antidiabetic Drugs

Several drugs for type 2 diabetes also act on pathways that drive inflammation in RA, such as AMPK and NF-κB (Section 3). Insulin resistance is also common in RA and is linked to higher disease activity [Ormseth2013]. This section first covers metformin and pioglitazone, followed by glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors.

## 4.1.1. Metformin
> 📖 الإملاء: [[4.1a-Metformin]] · 🎯 ≥400 كلمة (حد أدنى)
> ✏️ اكتب تحت السطر ده: 
Metformin is an inexpensive first-line drug for type 2 diabetes and is generally safe [Gharib2021]. The drug appears to act mainly by activating AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which in hepatocytes lowers acetyl-CoA carboxylase activity, increases fatty acid oxidation and suppresses lipogenic enzyme expression [Zhou2001]. Activated AMPK phosphorylates raptor, a step needed to inhibit mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) complex 1 (mTORC1) under energy stress [Gwinn2008]. Both mTOR complexes, mTORC1 and mTORC2, promote the differentiation of T helper 17 (Th17) cells [Nagai2013].

Animal studies consistently show that metformin reduces arthritis and protects the joint. In the collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model (Table 1), immunized mice were given 5 mg/mouse of metformin orally three times per week for 9 weeks, starting 7 days after the first immunization. This treatment significantly reduced arthritis severity and shifted the Th17 to regulatory T (Treg) cell ratio, with fewer Th17 cells and more Treg cells. Phosphorylation of signal transducers and activators of transcription 3 and 5 (STAT3 and STAT5) fell and rose, respectively, and osteoclast activity was reduced [Son2014]. Similarly, in rats with CIA, intraperitoneal (i.p.) metformin (100 mg/kg every 3 days for 3 weeks) protected the knee joints on micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) and on histopathology. Micro-CT showed preserved trabecular bone, histology showed less osteoclast formation and synovitis, and metformin also lowered markers of systemic inflammation, chondrocyte apoptosis and cartilage matrix degradation [Fan2020]. After body-surface-area conversion for a 60-kg adult [NairJacob2016], the oral dose of 5 mg/mouse three times weekly [Son2014] equals approximately 970–1,220 mg per dose, assuming a 20–25-g mouse because body weight was not reported. The intraperitoneal dose of 100 mg/kg every 3 days in rats [Fan2020] equals approximately 970 mg per dose, and both values lie within the usual clinical range of 500–2,550 mg/day [Naffaa2020].

Data from human joint tissue also suggest a link between AMPK and RA activity. In synovium from patients with RA, the active (phosphorylated) form of AMPK-α1 was higher than in synovium from osteoarthritis patients; in their serum, phosphorylated AMPK-α1 rose with the 28-joint Disease Activity Score (DAS28), and total AMPK-α1 rose with tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [Chen2020]. When fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) from these patients were cultured, metformin at high (millimolar) doses inhibited cell proliferation and migration and reduced TNF-α, interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-6, although the authors questioned whether such doses can be reached in patients [Chen2020].

Three clinical studies suggest a benefit of metformin as an add-on to conventional synthetic (cs) disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs). The larger of the two randomized trials, a double-blind study from Egypt, assigned 120 patients with active RA to metformin 1000 mg or placebo daily, each added to methotrexate 7.5 mg/week, over 12 weeks [Abdallah2021]. At 12 weeks, 80.8% of the metformin group reached an American College of Rheumatology 20% improvement (ACR20) response, compared with 54.7% of the placebo group (P = 0.001), without serious adverse effects in either group [Abdallah2021]. A randomized, single-blind trial enrolled 66 Egyptian patients with moderately or highly active RA, of whom 33 received metformin 850 mg twice daily in addition to csDMARDs and 33 received placebo. After 6 months, C-reactive protein (CRP) levels and DAS28-CRP scores were lower than with placebo, and only three metformin-treated patients withdrew because of gastrointestinal side effects [Gharib2021]. In a non-randomized prospective cohort study, 124 patients received low-dose metformin (250 mg twice daily) in addition to traditional csDMARD therapy for 90 days and were compared with 98 patients receiving csDMARDs only. Metformin use was associated with a faster fall in IL-6, TNF-α, high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and CRP than in the csDMARD-only group [Zhang2023]. A meta-analysis of four randomized trials with 350 patients found that metformin lowered CRP after 3 months (mean difference −5.99 mg/L, 95% CI from −7.46 to −4.52) [Alshammari2026]. Over the same period, DAS28 also fell in the three trials that reported it (mean difference −0.80, 95% CI from −0.87 to −0.73) [Alshammari2026]. However, each of the four trials was conducted in Egypt or India, and two carried a high risk of bias because they were single-blind and did not report trial registration [Alshammari2026].

Data on RA prevention are conflicting. In a large UK cohort of people recently diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, RA risk was the same in metformin users as in users of other oral diabetes drugs (hazard ratio [HR] after adjustment 1.00, 95% CI 0.82 to 1.22) [Zemedikun2021]. A Mendelian randomization study suggested that metformin may protect against RA, but its genetic markers reflect metformin use and therefore largely track diabetes [Liang2023]. In an Israeli cohort of 113,749 people starting metformin, adherence was associated with a lower RA risk, which was lowest when 40–59% of days were covered rather than under 20% (adjusted HR 0.62, 95% CI from 0.45 to 0.84) [Naffaa2020]. In analyses stratified by sex, this association was limited to women [Naffaa2020]. Therefore, metformin may be a safe and inexpensive add-on to csDMARDs to lower RA disease activity. Whether it prevents RA is still unclear, and larger and longer double-blind randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that also check joint damage on X-ray are needed.



## 4.2. Lipid-Lowering Drugs

Patients with RA have up to twice the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease seen in the general population, a level similar to that in diabetes [Hansildaar2021], which makes lipid-lowering drugs a logical choice to test. The two classes examined here, statins and fenofibrate, also affect immune cells and may benefit both the joints and the heart.


## 4.2.1. Statins
> 📖 الإملاء: [[4.2a-Statins]] · 🎯 ≥450 كلمة (حد أدنى)
> ✏️ اكتب تحت السطر ده:
Statins act mainly as competitive inhibitors of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMG-CoA reductase), binding the enzyme at nanomolar concentrations and displacing its natural substrate, which blocks de novo cholesterol synthesis [Greenwood2006]. Statins also have effects beyond lipid lowering, because fewer isoprenoid intermediates leave small guanosine triphosphatases (GTPases) such as Ras, Rho, Rac and Cdc42 less prenylated and therefore less active [Greenwood2006]. They also block interferon-γ (IFN-γ)-induced major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II expression and thus MHC-dependent T-cell activation [Kwak2000], a key driver of joint inflammation in RA [Xing2015].

Animal studies of statins are mostly positive, but not all. In a preclinical study, mice with serum-induced arthritis were given atorvastatin, pravastatin or simvastatin intravenously (0.2 mg/kg on days 3, 5 and 7). Atorvastatin and pravastatin reduced paw swelling and knee destruction on histopathology, but simvastatin had no significant effect [Walker2017]. This regimen corresponds to approximately 1 mg per dose in a 60-kg adult after body-surface-area conversion [NairJacob2016]. That is far below the 40 mg/day used in RA trials [McCarey2004][Kitas2019], although intravenous and oral doses are not directly comparable.

Earlier work in CIA was favorable. In mice given daily i.p. simvastatin at 10, 20 or 40 mg/kg between days 21 and 40, arthritis was suppressed in a dose-dependent manner [Leung2003]. A 14-day course of 40 mg/kg, started one day after clinical onset, also inhibited established disease [Leung2003]. Because these doses did not significantly lower plasma cholesterol, the effect appeared largely independent of lipid lowering [Leung2003]. Findings with other statins were less favorable. In a later CIA study, atorvastatin and rosuvastatin had no effect on clinical, histologic, immunologic or biochemical markers of disease [Palmer2004]. The benefit of i.p. simvastatin was reproduced, but the authors attributed it to severe toxicity with raised glucocorticoid levels [Palmer2004]. Deaths reduced the simvastatin group from 18 to 7 mice over the study, so the earlier benefit may reflect toxicity rather than a true antiarthritic effect [Palmer2004]. When Vandebriel et al. gave CIA mice oral atorvastatin at 0.2 mg/day or oral pravastatin at 0.8 mg/day, arthritis started earlier, and every treated mouse became arthritic, against 7 of 12 controls [Vandebriel2012]. After body-surface-area conversion for a 60-kg adult [NairJacob2016], the i.p. simvastatin doses of 10–40 mg/kg [Leung2003] correspond to approximately 49–195 mg/day. The highest dose was therefore about five times the 40 mg/day used in RA trials [McCarey2004][Kitas2019]. The oral atorvastatin dose used by Vandebriel et al., about 10 mg/kg, corresponds to approximately 49 mg/day and is close to the trial dose, whereas pravastatin at four times that dose corresponds to approximately 195 mg/day [Vandebriel2012]. Earlier arthritis onset after statin treatment has, to our knowledge, been reported only by this group [Vandebriel2012].

Results in patients are mixed. Two meta-analyses showed that statins reduced disease activity measured by DAS28, with standardized mean differences (SMD) of −0.55 (95% CI −0.83 to −0.26) and −0.78 (95% CI −1.08 to −0.49) [Xing2015][Ren2023]. However, the included trials were small and mostly conducted at single centers [Xing2015], and both meta-analyses reported high heterogeneity [Xing2015][Ren2023]. In an unblinded randomized study conducted in Egypt, 30 patients with early RA were divided into two groups of 15 and treated for 6 months with MTX and prednisone, either alone (group 1) or with atorvastatin (group 2). DAS28 was significantly reduced from 6.09 to 3.9 in group 2 compared with 6.19 to 5.27 in group 1 [ElBarbary2010]. The double-blind Trial of Atorvastatin in Rheumatoid Arthritis (TARA) randomized 116 patients to atorvastatin 40 mg/day or placebo added to their existing DMARDs [McCarey2004]. Its co-primary outcomes were change in DAS28 and European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) response [McCarey2004]. After 6 months, DAS28 improved more with atorvastatin than with placebo (between-group difference −0.52, P = 0.004; 95% CI from −0.87 to −0.17) [McCarey2004]. In a 12-week open-label study without placebo, 55 patients with RA received atorvastatin 20 mg/day or no statin [Tang2011]. DAS28 fell only slightly with atorvastatin (−0.41 vs −0.03; P < 0.001), and CRP and ESR also decreased [Tang2011]. In contrast, the largest trial we found, the double-blind Trial of Atorvastatin for primary prevention of Cardiovascular Events in RA (TRACE RA), enrolled 3,002 UK patients for cardiovascular end points [Kitas2019]. It found no difference in DAS28 between patients who received atorvastatin and those who received placebo [Kitas2019]. Atorvastatin did lower CRP, but the authors judged this fall too small to matter clinically in RA [Kitas2019]. Smaller trials, including the double-blind TARA [McCarey2004] and an open-label study [Tang2011], therefore reported lower DAS28, whereas TRACE RA did not [Kitas2019]. TRACE RA targeted cardiovascular end points, and reported compliance in its atorvastatin arm fell from 89% at 3 months to 39% by 60 months [Kitas2019]. These design features may partly explain the discordant results.

Observational data on statin use and RA onset are inconsistent: in an Israeli cohort of 211,627 new statin users, persistence was measured using the proportion of days covered (PDC) [Chodick2010]. Patients with PDC ≥80% had a lower risk of RA than those with PDC <20%, with HR 0.58 (95% CI from 0.52 to 0.65) [Chodick2010]. A US case–control study that matched 16,363 RA cases with the same number of controls initially found a slightly higher risk with statin use [Peterson2021]. After adjustment for hyperlipidemia, the association disappeared, with OR 0.95 (95% CI from 0.90 to 1.01) [Peterson2021]. However, hyperlipidemia is often diagnosed when statin treatment begins, which may limit the accuracy of this adjustment [Peterson2021]. Genetic data, in contrast, suggest a protective role. In a drug-target Mendelian randomization study, genetically proxied HMG-CoA reductase inhibition was linked to lower RA risk in both the discovery (OR 0.45, 95% CI from 0.32 to 0.63) and replication (OR 0.37, 95% CI from 0.23 to 0.61) datasets [Hu2024]. Although consistent, these data come from European populations only and address RA onset rather than disease activity in established RA [Hu2024]. A small prevention trial was also inconclusive: among 62 anti-citrullinated protein antibody (ACPA)-positive patients with arthralgia, atorvastatin 40 mg/day was compared with placebo for a planned 3 years, but the trial was stopped early because of low inclusion [vanBoheemen2021]. Arthritis developed in 9/31 (29%) atorvastatin recipients and 6/31 (19%) placebo recipients (HR 1.40, 95% CI from 0.50 to 3.95) [vanBoheemen2021]. The difference was not significant, and no protective effect was shown [vanBoheemen2021]. For now, statins in RA are prescribed to lower lipids and cardiovascular risk, as in other patients [Kitas2019]. Larger, longer placebo-controlled trials with disease activity as the primary end point are needed before statins can be used to treat RA itself.

## 4.2.2. Fenofibrate
> 📖 الإملاء: [[4.1b-PPAR-agonists]] · 🎯 ≥150 كلمة (حد أدنى)
> ✏️ اكتب تحت السطر ده:

Fenofibrate activates PPAR-α and is mainly used to treat high triglycerides [Zhou2012]. In mouse T cells, fenofibrate (5–20 μM) inhibited Th17-cell differentiation in a dose-dependent manner, by 74% at 20 μM, whereas other PPAR-α agonists (WY14643, GW7647, and bezafibrate) had no such effect, which suggests that fenofibrate acts here through another pathway [Zhou2012]. In AIA rats, fenofibrate (300 mg/kg/day for 12 days) lowered the arthritis score from 11.0 to 5.83, a fall of about 47% (P < 0.01) [Castillero2012]. Clinical data on fenofibrate in RA are very limited, and to our knowledge no placebo-controlled trial has yet tested whether the effects seen in animals also benefit patients with RA.
