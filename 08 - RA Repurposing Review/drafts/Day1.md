## 4.1. Antidiabetic Drugs

Several drugs for type 2 diabetes also act on pathways that drive inflammation in RA, such as AMPK and NF-κB (Section 3). Among 124 patients with RA, insulin resistance, estimated with the homeostasis model assessment (HOMA) index, correlated weakly with disease activity (ρ = 0.21) and more strongly with IL-6 (ρ = 0.63) [Chung2008]. Whether RA itself is linked to higher insulin resistance remains unsettled. In untreated very early RA (disease duration under 12 weeks), mean HOMA-IR was higher in 90 patients than in 90 controls of matched age, sex and body mass index (2.3 ± 1.1 vs 1.6 ± 1.0, P < 0.001) [Ye2022]. In these patients, none of whom was receiving prednisone or disease-modifying treatment, HOMA-IR correlated with disease activity (r = 0.50) [Ye2022]. In contrast, insulin resistance was no more common in 89 patients with established, well-controlled RA than in 80 non-diabetic controls of similar age, sex and body mass index [ManriqueArija2021]. In this cross-sectional study, 25 of the 89 patients (28.1%) had a HOMA-IR of at least 2.29 [ManriqueArija2021]. Within this cohort, insulin resistance was linked to higher cumulative disease activity (OR 2.8, 95% CI from 1.3 to 6.0), higher IL-1β (OR 1.6, 95% CI from 1.1 to 2.4) and greater fat mass, with the odds rising by about 10% for each extra kilogram [ManriqueArija2021]. IL-6 and TNF-α, however, did not differ by insulin resistance status [ManriqueArija2021]. These discrepant findings may partly reflect differences in disease stage and treatment between the two cohorts. This section first covers metformin and pioglitazone, followed by glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors.

## 4.1.1. Metformin
> 📖 الإملاء: [[4.1a-Metformin]] · 🎯 ≥400 كلمة (حد أدنى)
> ✏️ اكتب تحت السطر ده: 
Metformin is a common oral treatment for type 2 diabetes, but how it acts at the cellular level was long unclear [Zhou2001]. The drug appears to act mainly by activating AMPK, which in hepatocytes lowers acetyl-CoA carboxylase activity, increases fatty acid oxidation and suppresses lipogenic enzyme expression [Zhou2001]. Activated AMPK phosphorylates raptor, a step needed to inhibit mTORC1 under energy stress [Gwinn2008]. The mTORC1 complex enhances Th17 cell differentiation, and its loss after Rheb or Raptor deletion impairs it, whereas the role of mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2) remains controversial, as Rictor deletion had no effect in several studies [Nagai2013].

Two animal studies showed that metformin reduced arthritis and protected the joint. In the collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model (Table 1), immunized mice were given 5 mg/mouse of metformin orally three times per week for 9 weeks, starting 7 days after the first immunization. This treatment significantly reduced arthritis severity and shifted the Th17 to regulatory T (Treg) cell ratio, with fewer Th17 cells and more Treg cells. The numbers of T cells positive for phosphorylated STAT3 and STAT5 fell and rose, respectively, and osteoclast activity was reduced [Son2014]. Similarly, in rats with CIA, intraperitoneal (i.p.) metformin (100 mg/kg every 3 days for 3 weeks) protected the knee joints on micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) and on histopathology. Micro-CT showed preserved trabecular bone, histology showed less osteoclast formation and synovitis, and metformin also lowered markers of systemic inflammation, chondrocyte apoptosis and cartilage matrix degradation [Fan2020]. After body-surface-area conversion for a 60-kg adult [NairJacob2016], the oral dose of 5 mg/mouse [Son2014] equals approximately 810–1,220 mg per dose. This range assumes a 20–30-g mouse, because body weight was not reported. The i.p. dose of 100 mg/kg in rats [Fan2020] equals approximately 970 mg per dose. These per-dose values lie within the range of daily doses (500–2,550 mg) observed among metformin users in a large Israeli cohort [Naffaa2020]. Averaged over the dosing intervals of three times weekly and every 3 days, however, they correspond to approximately 350–520 mg/day and 320 mg/day, respectively, close to or below the lower end of that range [NairJacob2016].

Human joint tissue data also suggest a link between AMPK and RA activity. RA synovium expressed more AMPK-α1 than osteoarthritis synovium; in RA serum, phosphorylated AMPK-α1 correlated weakly with DAS28 (r = 0.27), and total AMPK-α1 correlated moderately with TNF-α (r = 0.46) [Chen2020]. In cultured RA FLS, metformin at 5 and 10 mM inhibited proliferation, whereas a lower concentration (1 mM) promoted it, and migration was inhibited at 5 mM [Chen2020]. In addition, at 5 mM the drug decreased the messenger RNA of IL-6 and the protein of TNF-α and IL-1β in these cells [Chen2020]. The authors concluded that, at these concentrations, metformin is unlikely to be an effective drug for RA [Chen2020].

Three clinical studies suggest a benefit of metformin as an add-on to csDMARDs. The larger of the two randomized trials, a double-blind study from Egypt, assigned 120 patients with active RA to metformin 1,000 mg or placebo daily, each added to methotrexate (MTX) 7.5 mg/week, over 12 weeks [Abdallah2021]. At 12 weeks, 80.8% of the metformin group reached an American College of Rheumatology 20% improvement (ACR20) response, compared with 54.7% of the placebo group (P = 0.001), without serious adverse effects in either group [Abdallah2021]. A randomized, single-blind trial enrolled 66 Egyptian patients with moderately or highly active RA, of whom 33 received metformin 850 mg twice daily in addition to csDMARDs and 33 received placebo. After 6 months, C-reactive protein (CRP) levels and DAS28-CRP scores were lower than with placebo among the 60 patients who completed the trial, and three metformin-treated patients (9%) withdrew because of gastrointestinal intolerance, while three control patients withdrew because of non-compliance [Gharib2021]. Only the 60 completers (30 per arm) were analysed, and no intention-to-treat analysis was reported [Gharib2021]. A completer analysis of this kind may overestimate the benefit. In a non-randomized prospective cohort study, 124 patients received low-dose metformin (250 mg twice daily) in addition to traditional csDMARD therapy for 90 days and were compared with 98 patients receiving csDMARDs only. Metformin use was associated with a faster fall in IL-6, TNF-α, high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and CRP than in the csDMARD-only group [Zhang2023]. A meta-analysis of four randomized trials with 350 patients found that metformin lowered CRP after 3 months (mean difference −5.99 mg/L, 95% confidence interval [CI] from −7.46 to −4.52) [Alshammari2026]. Over the same period, DAS28 also fell in the three trials that reported it (mean difference −0.80, 95% CI from −0.87 to −0.73) [Alshammari2026]. This interval appears implausibly narrow. The three trials were small, and baseline DAS28 had a standard deviation of about 1.5 units [Abdallah2021]; the pooled estimate should therefore be read with caution. However, each of the four trials was conducted in Egypt or India, and two carried a high risk of bias because they were single-blind and did not report trial registration [Alshammari2026]. Therefore, metformin may be a well-tolerated add-on to csDMARDs for lowering RA disease activity in short trials, but larger and longer double-blind randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that also check joint damage on X-ray are needed.

Data on RA prevention are conflicting. In a large UK cohort of people recently diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, metformin use showed no clear association with RA risk compared with other oral diabetes drugs (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] 1.00, 95% CI from 0.82 to 1.22) [Zemedikun2021]. A Mendelian randomization study suggested that metformin may protect against RA (odds ratio [OR] 0.023, 95% CI from 0.0016 to 0.34) [Liang2023]. This effect size is implausibly large for a drug effect and should be interpreted with caution. Genetic variants linked to metformin use may also reflect the genetic liability to diabetes itself. In an Israeli cohort of 113,749 people starting metformin, adherence was associated with a lower RA risk, which was lowest when 40–59% of days were covered rather than under 20% (adjusted HR 0.62, 95% CI from 0.45 to 0.84) [Naffaa2020]. Because the risk was lowest at intermediate rather than the highest adherence, the association did not rise steadily with adherence, which leaves healthy-adherer bias and chance as plausible explanations. In analyses stratified by sex, this association was limited to women [Naffaa2020].



## 4.2. Lipid-Lowering Drugs

Patients with RA have up to twice the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease seen in the general population, a level similar to that in diabetes [Hansildaar2021], which makes lipid-lowering drugs a logical choice to test. The two classes examined here, statins and fenofibrate, also affect immune cells and may benefit both the joints and the heart (Sections 4.2.1 and 4.2.2).


## 4.2.1. Statins
> 📖 الإملاء: [[4.2a-Statins]] · 🎯 ≥450 كلمة (حد أدنى)
> ✏️ اكتب تحت السطر ده:
Statins act mainly as competitive inhibitors of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMG-CoA reductase), binding the enzyme at nanomolar concentrations and displacing its natural substrate, which blocks de novo cholesterol synthesis [Greenwood2006]. Statins also have effects beyond lipid lowering, because fewer isoprenoid intermediates leave small guanosine triphosphatases (GTPases) such as Ras, Rho, Rac and Cdc42 less prenylated and therefore less active [Greenwood2006]. They also block interferon-γ (IFN-γ)-induced major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II expression and thus MHC-dependent T-cell activation [Kwak2000], and T cells form the largest lymphocyte population in rheumatoid synovium [Gao2024].

Animal data on statins are conflicting. In a preclinical study, mice with serum-induced arthritis were given atorvastatin, pravastatin or simvastatin intravenously (0.2 mg/kg on days 3, 5 and 7), and atorvastatin and pravastatin lessened clinical arthritis and preserved joint structure on paw histology, but simvastatin had no significant effect [Walker2017]. This regimen corresponds to approximately 1 mg per dose in a 60-kg adult after body-surface-area conversion [NairJacob2016], far below the 40 mg/day used in RA trials [McCarey2004][Kitas2019], although intravenous and oral doses are not directly comparable.

An earlier CIA study with simvastatin was favorable. In mice given daily i.p. simvastatin at 10, 20 or 40 mg/kg between days 21 and 40, only 40 mg/kg significantly lowered the incidence and severity of arthritis, whereas 10 and 20 mg/kg did not differ from vehicle [Leung2003]. A 14-day course of 40 mg/kg, started one day after clinical onset, also inhibited established disease [Leung2003]. This effect occurred without a significant change in total plasma cholesterol [Leung2003]. Findings with other statins were less favorable. In a later CIA study, atorvastatin and rosuvastatin had no effect on clinical, histologic, immunologic or biochemical markers of disease [Palmer2004]. The benefit of i.p. simvastatin at 40 mg/kg/day, the dose used by Leung et al., was reproduced, but the authors attributed it to severe side effects, namely massive peritoneal inflammation with intestinal occlusion and hemorrhage, together with raised serum corticosterone [Palmer2004]. Moreover, oral simvastatin had no effect on disease activity, and 3 of 6 non-immunized mice given i.p. simvastatin also died within 2 weeks [Palmer2004]. Deaths reduced this group from 18 to 7 mice over the study, which suggests that the earlier benefit may reflect toxicity rather than a true antiarthritic effect [Palmer2004]. When Vandebriel et al. gave CIA mice oral atorvastatin at 0.2 mg/day or oral pravastatin at 0.8 mg/day, arthritis started earlier, and every treated mouse became arthritic, against 7 of 12 controls [Vandebriel2012]. After body-surface-area conversion for a 60-kg adult [NairJacob2016], the i.p. simvastatin doses of 10–40 mg/kg [Leung2003] correspond to approximately 49–195 mg/day, with the highest dose about five times the 40 mg/day used in RA trials [McCarey2004][Kitas2019]. The oral atorvastatin dose used by Vandebriel et al., about 10 mg/kg, corresponds to approximately 49 mg/day and is close to the trial dose, whereas pravastatin at four times that dose corresponds to approximately 195 mg/day [Vandebriel2012]. Earlier arthritis onset after statin treatment has, to our knowledge, been reported only by this group [Vandebriel2012].

Results in patients are mixed. In two meta-analyses, statin use lowered DAS28, with standardized mean differences (SMDs) of −0.55 (95% CI −0.83 to −0.26) [Xing2015] and −0.78 (95% CI −1.08 to −0.49; 16 studies) [Ren2023]. However, the included trials were small and mostly conducted at single centers [Xing2015], and both meta-analyses reported high heterogeneity [Xing2015][Ren2023]. In the analysis by Ren et al., heterogeneity was concentrated in studies from Asia (I² = 92.3%, compared with no more than 17.5% in North America and Europe) [Ren2023]. The Egger test also suggested possible publication bias for DAS28 (P = 0.012), and the authors judged their findings not sufficiently consistent [Ren2023]. In an unblinded randomized study conducted in Egypt, 30 patients with early RA were divided into two groups of 15 and treated for 6 months with MTX (0.2 mg/kg/week) and prednisone (10 mg/day), either alone (group 1) or with atorvastatin 40 mg/day (group 2). DAS28 was significantly reduced from 6.09 to 3.9 in group 2 compared with 6.19 to 5.27 in group 1 [ElBarbary2010]. The double-blind Trial of Atorvastatin in Rheumatoid Arthritis (TARA) randomized 116 patients to atorvastatin 40 mg/day or placebo added to their existing DMARDs [McCarey2004], with co-primary outcomes of change in DAS28 and EULAR response [McCarey2004]. After 6 months, DAS28 improved more with atorvastatin than with placebo (between-group difference −0.52, P = 0.004; 95% CI from −0.87 to −0.17) [McCarey2004]. This difference was slightly below the 0.6-unit change the trial was powered to detect, but EULAR response was more frequent with atorvastatin (31% vs 10%) [McCarey2004], whereas the small unblinded Egyptian study reported a between-group difference of about 1.3 units [ElBarbary2010]. In a 12-week randomized study without placebo (blinding not reported), 55 patients with RA received atorvastatin 20 mg/day or no statin [Tang2011]. DAS28 fell by 0.41 with atorvastatin and by 0.03 without it (P < 0.001), and CRP and ESR also decreased [Tang2011]. The DAS28 change, however, was below the 0.6-unit threshold used to power TARA [McCarey2004]. In contrast, the largest trial we found, the double-blind Trial of Atorvastatin for primary prevention of Cardiovascular Events in RA (TRACE RA), enrolled 3,002 UK patients for cardiovascular end points [Kitas2019]. It found no difference in DAS28 between the atorvastatin and placebo groups [Kitas2019]. Atorvastatin did lower CRP, but the authors judged the fall too small to matter clinically [Kitas2019]. TRACE RA targeted cardiovascular end points, reported compliance in its atorvastatin arm fell from 89% at 3 months to 39% by 60 months, and the trial was stopped early because cardiovascular events were fewer than expected [Kitas2019]. These design features, rather than a lack of efficacy alone, may partly explain why TRACE RA did not confirm the smaller trials.

Observational data on statin use and RA onset are inconsistent: in an Israeli cohort of 211,627 new statin users, persistence was measured using the proportion of days covered (PDC) [Chodick2010]. Compared with a PDC of <20%, a PDC of ≥80% was associated with lower RA risk (adjusted HR 0.58, 95% CI from 0.52 to 0.65), and to test for healthy-user bias, the same study also examined osteoarthritis [Chodick2010]. For osteoarthritis, high persistence was associated with only a modest risk reduction (HR 0.85, 95% CI from 0.81 to 0.88) [Chodick2010]. In a matched case–control analysis of Korean national health insurance data, 3149 patients with RA were compared with 12,596 controls (1:4 matching) for statin prescriptions in the 2 years before the index date [Kwon2022]. Use for more than 365 days was associated with lower odds of RA (any statin, adjusted OR 0.73, 95% CI from 0.63 to 0.85; lipophilic statins, adjusted OR 0.71, 95% CI from 0.61 to 0.84), whereas hydrophilic statins and shorter use showed no such association [Kwon2022]. Exposure was based on prescription days rather than observed intake, and the database held no information on family history or autoantibodies [Kwon2022]. A US case–control study that matched 16,363 RA cases with the same number of controls initially found a slightly higher risk with statin use [Peterson2021], but after adjustment for hyperlipidemia the association disappeared, with OR 0.95 (95% CI from 0.90 to 1.01) [Peterson2021]. However, hyperlipidemia is often diagnosed when statin treatment begins, which may limit the accuracy of this adjustment [Peterson2021]. A meta-analysis pooled nine studies published up to October 2023 (four cohort, four case–control and one randomized trial) [Pan2024]. It found no clear association between statin use and RA (pooled OR 0.93, 95% CI from 0.82 to 1.06; I² = 75%) [Pan2024]. Neither cohort studies (OR 1.01, 95% CI from 0.92 to 1.10) nor case–control studies (OR 0.88, 95% CI from 0.69 to 1.13) showed a significant association on their own [Pan2024]. The analysis, however, combined odds, hazard and risk ratios, most studies did not report follow-up, and most data were from Western populations [Pan2024]. The pooled analysis appears to include the studies by Kwon et al. and Peterson et al. discussed above and is therefore not independent of them [Pan2024]. Genetic evidence is also conflicting. In a drug-target Mendelian randomization study, HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR) inhibition was proxied genetically by a one-standard-deviation lower low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) level [Hu2024]. This proxy was associated with lower RA risk in the discovery (OR 0.45, 95% CI from 0.32 to 0.63) and replication (OR 0.37, 95% CI from 0.23 to 0.61) datasets [Hu2024]. These estimates come from European populations and address RA onset rather than disease activity in established RA [Hu2024]. The posterior probability that LDL-C and RA share a causal variant at the HMGCR locus was, however, only 22%, and LDL-C itself showed no causal link to RA [Hu2024]. A second drug-target study reported the opposite direction, with HMGCR inhibition associated with a slightly higher RA risk (inverse-variance weighted OR 0.995, 95% CI from 0.991 to 0.998, per unit higher HMGCR-mediated LDL-C) [Ma2023]. In a third study, genetically predicted LDL-C was not significantly linked to RA risk across two datasets with 19,562 cases in total [Qiao2024]. A fourth drug-target study drew on European genome-wide association data [Huang2024]. Genetically predicted lowering of HMGCR-mediated LDL-C by one standard deviation was associated with lower RA risk in a meta-analysis across the RA datasets (OR 0.77, 95% CI from 0.64 to 0.92), consistent in direction with the estimate of Hu et al. [Huang2024][Hu2024]. As in the study by Hu et al., lipid levels themselves showed no causal association with RA [Huang2024][Hu2024]. The authors nevertheless suggested that HMGCR inhibitors may increase RA risk, on the basis of their own expression analysis, in which genetically predicted higher blood HMGCR expression was associated with lower RA risk (OR 0.79, 95% CI from 0.66 to 0.96) [Huang2024]. This reading runs against the direction implied by the LDL-C estimate, which leaves the genetic evidence on statins unresolved. The second and third analyses drew on the same lipid dataset of 173,082 participants and are therefore not independent [Ma2023][Qiao2024]. The first and fourth analyses also used overlapping Global Lipids Genetics Consortium and FinnGen data and may likewise not be independent [Hu2024][Huang2024]. A small prevention trial was also inconclusive: among 62 anti-citrullinated protein antibody (ACPA)-positive patients with arthralgia, atorvastatin 40 mg/day was compared with placebo for a planned 3 years, but the trial was stopped early because of low inclusion [vanBoheemen2021]. Arthritis developed in 9/31 (29%) atorvastatin recipients and 6/31 (19%) placebo recipients (HR 1.40, 95% CI from 0.50 to 3.95), a non-significant difference [vanBoheemen2021]. Only 62 of the planned 220 participants were enrolled, which left the trial too small to confirm or exclude a protective effect [vanBoheemen2021]. In TRACE RA, the primary cardiovascular end point did not differ significantly between atorvastatin and placebo (HR 0.66, 95% CI from 0.39 to 1.11; P = 0.115), but the trial had less than 20% power to detect the specified risk reduction [Kitas2019]. The authors concluded that their findings do not support statins for all patients with RA and that prescribing should follow each patient's individual cardiovascular risk [Kitas2019]. Larger, longer placebo-controlled trials with disease activity as the primary end point are needed before statins can be used to treat RA itself.

## 4.2.2. Fenofibrate
> 📖 الإملاء: [[4.1b-PPAR-agonists]] · 🎯 ≥150 كلمة (حد أدنى)
> ✏️ اكتب تحت السطر ده:

Fenofibrate is a hypolipidemic drug and a synthetic PPAR-α ligand [Varga2011]. In vitro, fenofibrate (5–20 μM) reduced the proportion of IL-17+ cells among cultured mouse splenic T cells in a concentration-dependent manner, from 1.54% to 0.40% at 20 μM (74% lower) [Zhou2012]. The PPAR-α agonists WY14643 and GW7647 and the pan-PPAR agonist bezafibrate had no effect, and the authors proposed that this action of fenofibrate might be PPAR-α independent [Zhou2012]. In AIA rats, fenofibrate (300 mg/kg/day by gavage) was given for 12 days from day 4 after adjuvant injection, before arthritis onset [Castillero2012]. The arthritis score (maximum 16) was then 5.83 with fenofibrate versus 11.0 in vehicle-treated arthritic rats, about 47% lower (P < 0.01), although this score was a secondary readout in a study of muscle wasting and adipokines [Castillero2012]. This dose was far above clinical doses. By the body-surface-area method for a 60-kg adult, 300 mg/kg/day corresponds to approximately 2,900 mg/day, about 15–20 times the 145–200 mg/day used in RA studies [NairJacob2016][Castillero2012][Shirinsky2013][Goto2010]. In a randomized study without a placebo arm, 44 patients with RA and dyslipidemia were assigned to fenofibrate 200 mg/day (n = 23) or statins (n = 21) for 6 months [Goto2010]. At 6 months, the prednisolone dose was lower in the fenofibrate arm than in the statin arm (P = 0.0449) [Goto2010]. Within the fenofibrate arm, it had fallen (P = 0.0098 vs baseline) from a mean of 3.6 (standard deviation [SD] 2.7) to 2.0 (SD 2.2) mg/day [Goto2010]. However, this small study tested several outcomes, so the result is exploratory. CRP changes were non-significant in both arms [Goto2010]. In an uncontrolled pilot study, 27 patients with active RA received 145 mg/day of fenofibrate for 12 weeks in addition to stable DMARDs, and median DAS28 fell from 6.28 to 4.83 [Shirinsky2013]. Without a control group, part of this fall may reflect regression to the mean in patients enrolled during high disease activity. CRP and IL-6 decreased by 36% and 41%, respectively, while 2 patients withdrew because of severe back pain considered possibly drug-related [Shirinsky2013]. Clinical evidence therefore remains limited to these two small studies, and to our knowledge no placebo-controlled trial has evaluated fenofibrate in RA.
