# Exenatide ameliorates inflammatory response in human rheumatoid arthritis fibroblast-like synoviocytes

## Abstract

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory autoimmune disease of unknown etiology characterized by degradation of cartilage and bone, accompanied by unimpeded proliferation of synoviocytes of altered phenotype. In the present study, we investigated the involvement of the glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor on human fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) in the pathogenesis of RA using the selective GLP-1 agonist exenatide, a licensed drug used for the treatment of type 2 diabetes. ==Our results indicate that exenatide may play a role in regulating tumor necrosis factor-α-induced mitochondrial dysfunction by increasing mitochondrial membrane potential, oxidative stress by reducing the production of reactive oxygen species, the expression of NADPH oxidase 4, expression of matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-3 and MMP-13, release of proinflammatory cytokines including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, and high-mobility group protein 1, as well as activation of the p38/nuclear factor of κ light polypeptide gene enhancer in B-cells inhibitor, α/nuclear factor κB signaling pathway in primary human RA FLS.== These positive results indicate that exenatide may have potential as a therapeutic agent for the treatment and prevention of RA. © 2019 IUBMB Life, 9999(9999):1-9, 2019.

## INTRODUCTION

Despite recent in-depth research, the factors driving the development and progression of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) remain poorly understood. Characterized by synovitis, chronic destructive arthritis, and various systemic comorbidities, RA is an autoimmune chronic inﬂammatory disease of unknown etiology (1). Normally, the synovium encapsulates the joint such that the synovial ﬂuid consisting of macrophage-like and ﬁbroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) reduces friction between cartilages. However, in RA, FLS take on a pathological apoptosis-resistant phenotype and lose their innate ability of contact inhibition, resulting in unimpeded proliferation of pathologic FLS, invasive pannus formation, and subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and a positive inﬂammatory feedback loop (2, 3). Additionally, FLS produce cytokines and chemokines, such as interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and monocyte chemoattractant protein (MCP)-1, and express cartilage degrading enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-3 and MMP-13, thereby perpetuating the cycles of inﬂammation and cartilage destruction (4, 5). Of these, sustained activation of the NF-κB pathway is considered to be of critical importance to the pathogenesis of RA (6, 7). Recruitment of immune cells to the pannus further induces inﬂammation and promotes the release of cytokines and chemokines. High-mobility group protein 1 (HMG-1) is a chemokine that recruits large amounts of immune cells to the RA synovium, where they release autoantigens that attack healthy bone and cartilage tissues. Additionally, HMG-1 interacts with toll-like receptors to upregulate activation of the NF-κB proinﬂammatory pathway. Recently, HMG-1 was cited as a potential treatment target for regulating cell migration in RA (8).

Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) has been shown to be vastly abundant in RA synovium (9). Numerous studies have demonstrated the involvement of TNF-α in perpetuating the pathological inﬂammatory response in RA by promoting the release of proinﬂammatory cytokines including IL-1β and IL-6, decreasing mitochondrial function, increasing the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), upregulating expression of degradative enzymes such as MMP-3 and MMP-13, and activating key inﬂammatory pathways including the p38/MAPK and NF-κB pathways (10–13). While therapies directly targeting TNF-α have proven beneﬁcial for some patients, a portion of patients have little or no response to such therapies (14). Therefore, new therapies are desperately needed for the treatment of this painful and debilitating disease.

Exenatide, a glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist (GLP-1RA), has been licensed for the treatment of type 2 diabetes (15). In addition to improving hyperglycaemia and promoting weight loss, exenatide has displayed a variety of pharmacological functions in different types of tissues and cells. For example, it has been recently reported that exenatide may be protective against the harmful effects of iron accumulation in testis by reducing oxidative stress and cell death (16). Exenatide is able to exert cardioprotective effects by improving endothelial function and acting as an antihypertensive agent (17). Additionally, exenatide possesses an anti-inﬂammatory effect and modulates regulation of extracellular matrix components by inhibiting the expressions of metalloproteinases including MMP-1 and MMP-2 in human retinal pigment epithelium (HRPE) (18). In the following experiments, we used TNF-α to simulate RA in primary human FLS and determined the effects of the selective GLP-1R agonist exenatide on various factors involved in the pathogenesis of RA.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell Isolation and Treatment FLS were separated from knee joint synovial tissues from volunteer donors (n = 12). Experiments involving human subjects were carried out in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki Ethical Principles for Medical Research. Experiments using human samples were approved by the ethics committee of our institute. Written informed

consent was signed by all participants. Knee joint synovial tissues were cut into small pieces and digested in 0.05% trypsin for 15 min at 37C. Cells were treated with 10 ng/mL TNF-α in the presence or absence of 10 and 20 nM exendin-4 (an active ingredient form of exenatide) (19, 20) for 24 h.

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Determination FLS were seeded into six-well plates and treated with 10 ng/mL TNF-α in the presence or absence of 10 and 20 nM exendin-4 (a form of exenatide) for 24 h. To assess mitochondrial function, intracellular mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was examined by tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM). Media was removed from live cells and 1 mL of 250 nM TMRM was added and incubated for 30 min at 37C. Cells were washed three times with PBS. Red ﬂuorescence was imaged using a tetramethylrhodamine ﬁlter. The levels of MMP in human FLS were quantiﬁed by assessing ﬂuorescence density using the software Image J. We deﬁned the regions of interest (ROI) in the ﬂuorescent image and counted the average number of cells present in the deﬁned ROI. The integrated density value in ROI was calculated and divided by the average number of cells. Results were used to reﬂect average level of intracellular MMP.

Cytochrome c Oxidase Activity Assay Cytochrome c oxidase is the principle terminal oxidase and has a high afﬁnity for oxygen in the aerobic metabolic environment in mitochondria. FLS were seeded into six-well plates and treated with 10 ng/mL TNF-α in the presence or absence of 10 and 20 nM exendin-4 (a form of exenatide) for 24 h. To test the effects of exenatide on mitochondrial function, cytochrome c oxidase activity of FLS was determined using a cytochrome c oxidase assay kit (#CYTOCOX1, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Reactions were assayed by detecting the decrease in absorption at 550 nm at room temperature (RT; 25C) using a kinetic program: 5 sec delay; 10 sec interval; six readings.

## RESULTS

Improvement of Mitochondrial Dysfunction Loss of MMP and reduced cytochrome C oxidase activity by FLS are indicators of mitochondrial dysfunction (21). To determine the effects of agonism of GLP-1R on TNF-α-induced decreased MMP and cytochrome C oxidase activity, human primary FLS were exposed to 10 ng/mL TNF-α for 24 h in the presence of absence of 10 and 20 nM exendin-4, a form of exenatide. A shown in Fig. 1, treatment with exenatide rescued MMP and reduced cytochrome C oxidase activity in cytosol in a dosedependent manner, with higher dose returning the levels of both MMP and cytochrome C oxidase activity to near-basal levels.

Prevention of NOX-4 Expression and Oxidative Stress NOX-4 has been shown to be involved in the release of superoxide by RA synoviocytes (22). As shown in Fig. 2, exposure of FLS to 1 ng/mL TNF-α for 24 h resulted in signiﬁcant

upregulation of NOX-4, which was ameliorated by treatment with 10 and 20 nM exenatide (exendin-4) in a dose-dependent manner. Additionally, oxidative stress has been cited as a major player in the progression of RA (23). As shown in Fig. 3, using β-actin as a control, FLS were treated with 10 ng/mL TNF-α for 24 h in the presence or absence of 10 and 20 nM exenatide (exendin-4) to determine the effects of GLP-1 in regulating oxidative stress in RA. The results of real-time PCR analysis and Western blot analysis indicate that exenatide treatment prevented oxidative stress in RA-FLS in a dose-dependent manner at both the mRNA and protein levels, respectively.

The balance between oxidants and antioxidants plays a vital role in maintaining homeostasis in diverse tissues and cells. In RA, along with an increase in ROS, there is a decrease in the level of GSH, a major antioxidant. To investigate the effects of exenatide (exendin-4) treatment on oxidative stress, we exposed

FLS to 10 ng/mL TNF-α in the presence or absence of 10 and 20 nM exendin-4 for 24 h. As shown in Fig. 4, exenatide rescued the reduction in GSH and reduced the level of ROS in a dosedependent manner. Again, the higher dose of exenatide (20 nM) nearly returned both GSH and ROS to basal levels.

Attenuation of the Release of HMG-1 HMG-1 is an important chromatin protein that plays a regulatory role by interacting with various transcription factors, among other things, in the nucleus. In RA, HMG-1 acts as a cytokine mediator of inﬂammation. Therefore, to investigate the effects of treatment with exenatide on TNF-α-induced increased release of HMG-1, human FLS were exposed to 10 ng/mL TNF-α in the presence or absence of 10 and 20 nM exenatide (exendin-4) for 24 h. As demonstrated by the results of ELISA shown in Fig. 4, exposure to TNF-α increased expression of HMG-1 by roughly three-fold, which was attenuated by 10 and 20 nM exenatide in a dose-dependent manner. ## DISCUSSION

Although the role of GLP-1 in diabetes has been widely studied, the involvement of the GLP-1 receptor in other chronic inﬂammatory diseases is just starting to receive major attention. Part of the reason for this is that naturally occurring GLP-1 is rapidly degraded by the enzyme dipeptidyl peptidase 4, thus making administration of natural GLP-1 inadequate for treatment purposes. However, in 2005, exenatide received FDA approval

as the ﬁrst synthetic exendin-based incretin mimetic for use in humans (24, 25). Although GLP-1 agonists are now widely used for the treatment of type II diabetes and are highly regarded for their ability to trigger insulin secretion from the pancreas, inhibit glucagon release, delay gastric emptying, and satiate hunger, a previous study showed that knockout of GLP-1 in mice resulted in poor bone quality and reduced bone strength, thereby prompting investigations into the role of GLP-1 in bone formation and remodeling (26, 27). There have since been several studies demonstrating the antidegradative effects of exendin-4 in osteoarthritis (28–30). Additionally, administration of GLP-1 agonist has been shown to exert an anabolic effect on bone regeneration in obese type II diabetes patients with glucose intolerance (31). One such study involving obese women found a 16% increase in bone formation after weight loss following treatment with GLP-1 agonist (32). Despite these noteworthy discoveries in other areas, there has been little to no research on the effects of GLP-1 agonism in RA. Thus, we investigated the potential role of exenatide in the treatment of RA and found promising results.

Oxidative stress has been shown to inﬂuence chronic inﬂammation by upregulating the expression of ILs and matrix metalloproteinases in various cell types (34, 35). Here, we found that exenatide not only rescued markers of increased oxidative stress in FLS but also downregulated expression of IL-1β, IL-6, MMP-3, and MMP-13, thereby suggesting an ability of GLP-1 to prevent inﬂammation and degradation of cartilage in RA. Lastly, we revealed that exenatide exerted beneﬁcial effects by inhibiting activation of the p38/MAPK and NF-κB pathways, in part by preventing degradation and phosphorylation of IκBα, a precursor of NF-κB activation. Taken together, our ﬁndings show that the GLP-1 agonist exenatide (in the form of exendin-4) can ameliorate the inﬂammatory response in human RA FLS by rescuing mitochondrial function, reducing expression of NOX-4 and oxidative stress, downregulating expression of cytokines, chemokines, and degradative enzymes, and inhibiting activation of proinﬂammatory signaling pathways. These ﬁndings suggest that exenatide may have potential as a safe and effective therapy for the treatment of RA. Further research is warranted to better understand the exact mechanisms driving these promising results.
