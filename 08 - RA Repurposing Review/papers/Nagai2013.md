Interleukin (IL)-17–producing helper T (Th17) cells serve as a Th subset involved in epithelial cell– and neutrophilmediated immune responses against extracellular microbes and in the development of various autoimmune diseases. The differentiation of Th17 cells is controlled by a number of intracellular signaling cascades and a complex network of transcription factors. Recently, it has been shown that PI3K, Akt, and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) complexes, such as mTORC1 and mTORC2, also positively regulate Th17 differentiation both in vivo and in vitro via multiple mechanisms; here, we review the current knowledge regarding the mechanisms through which these molecules enhance Th17 differentiation.

## Introduction

CD4+ T helper (Th) cells are indispensable for initiating and maintaining adaptive immune responces. Th cells are subdivided into several subsets with distinct functions, including T helper type 1 (Th1), T helper type 2 (Th2), and IL-17– producing T helper (Th17) cells.1 Th17 cells are characterized by the production of IL-17A, IL-17F, and IL-22. These cytokines induce the expression of numerous chemokines and antimicrobial peptides in epithelial cells and ﬁbroblasts that are important for neutrophil-mediated immune reactions against extracellular microbes.1 Th17 cells are also a focus of attention because of their roles in the development of various autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis and multiple sclerosis. The molecular mechanisms of Th17 differentiation have been intensively studied, and numerous intracellular signaling cascades and transcriptional factors have been identiﬁed, including PI3K, Akt, and mTOR complexes. These molecules generally regulate cell survival, proliferation, polarity, and metabolism among many types of cells upon stim-

ulation by various hormones, growth factors, and cell–cell interactions. In addition, recent studies have revealed that PI3K, Akt, and mTOR complexes also regulate the differentiation and function of Th cells.

Here, we summarize the molecular mechanisms governing Th17 differentiation, mainly focusing on the roles of PI3K, Akt, and mTOR complexes (Fig. 1). Although some of these ﬁndings require further conﬁrmation in vivo, understanding the molecular complexities of Th17 differentiation may provide fundamental knowledge about how these cells are generated and how they contribute to host defense and to the development of autoimmune diseases.

mTORC2–Akt–FoxO1/3a signaling and Th17 differentiation

It is well known that mTORC2–Akt–FoxO1/3a signaling negatively regulates the differentiation of CD4+ regulatory T (Treg) cells, which are indispensable for preventing excessive immune reactions and the development of autoimmunity. Treg cells are characterized by the expression of Foxp3. FoxO1 andFoxO3adirectlybindtotheFoxp3promoterand positively regulate the expression of Foxp3. Hence, the deletion of Foxp3 results in the severe autoimmunity due to the collapse of T cell homeostasis.5

FoxO proteins are active in a dephosphorylated state in the nucleus; phosphorylation by Ser473phosphorylated Akt keeps FoxO proteins in the cytoplasm. Therefore, inhibition of PI3K, which impairs the phosphorylation of Akt at both Ser473 and Thr308,increasesTreg differentiationinaFoxO1/3adependent manner.6,7 Accordingly, the deletion of Rictor also promotes Treg differentiation through impaired Akt phsphorylation at Ser473.4

On the other hand, the role of mTORC2–Akt– FoxO1/3a signaling in Th17 differentiation is still

controversial. The expression of a constitutively active form of Akt enhances the differentiation of all Th subsets including Th17 cells,2 and T cell–speciﬁc deletionofFoxO1andFoxO3aresultsinautoimmunity, with increased Th17 differentiation in vivo.5

These observations seem to indicate the positive contribution of mTORC2–Akt–FoxO1/3a signaling to Th17 differentiation; however, deletion of Rictor did not have an effect on Th17 differentiation in several independent studies.4,8

PI3K–Akt–mTORC1 signaling and Th17 differentiation

The activation of PI3K followed by the phosphorylation of Akt at Thr308 leads to the phosphorylation and inhibition of TSC1/2 complex and PRAS40. TSC1/2 complex suppresses mTORC1 activity by inhibiting Rheb, a small GTPase required for mTORC1 activation. Furthermore, PRAS40 directly associates with mTORC1 and downregulates its activity.

In CD4+ T cells, the inhibition of PI3K and/or mTORC1 increases Treg cell differentiation.7 In contrast, activation of PI3K and/or mTORC1 enhances Th17 differentiation, and the dysfunction of mTORC1 caused by deletion of Rheb or Raptor impairs Th17 differentiation.2,8 Thus far, several distinct mechanisms are believed to regulate Th17 differentiation via mTORC1. One is increased expression of HIF-1 downstream of mTORC1.9,10

HIF-1 expression positively regulates Th17 differentiation by enhancing Rorc and Th17-related gene expression and by increasing glycolytic activity required for rapid T cell expansion.10,11 Also, we havedemonstratedthatmildhypoxiccondition(5% oxygen concentration) during Th17 differentiation in the priming phase activates the mTORC1 pathway independently of PI3K, implying the existence of a positive-feedback loop between mTORC1 and HIF-1 during Th17 differentiation.9

## Conclusion

The differentiation of Th17 cells is controlled by a variety of intracellular signaling pathways and a complex transcription factor network including PI3K, Akt, and mTOR, as reviewed above. Signaling via mTORC2–Akt–FoxO1/3a has been well characterized to negatively regulate Treg differentiation in a PI3K-dependent manner, but the contribution of this signaling pathway to Th17 differentiation is still controversial. PI3K–Akt– mTORC1 signaling positively regulates Th17 differentiation via multiple mechanisms including the regulation of HIF-1 expression, STAT3 phosphorylation, Gﬁ1 downregulation, and the nuclear

translocation of RORt. These accumulating pieces of evidence are providing more knowledge about T cell–mediated immunity and opportunities for manipulating immune systems in many inﬂammatory disorders.
