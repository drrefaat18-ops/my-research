# Effect of Glucagon‐Like Peptide 1 Receptor Agonists on Patients With Rheumatoid Arthritis

**Authors:** Kellner David A, Dente Elizabeth, Tran Vincent, Welsh Travis, Tran Victor, Saha Angshuman, Baker Joshua F, Elashoff David A, Ranganath Veena K  
**Journal:** ACR Open Rheumatology (2025)  
**DOI:** 10.1002/acr2.70103  
**PDF:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12423941/


## Abstract


### Abstract


#### Objective

Glucagon‐like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) are approved for weight loss, diabetes, and cardiovascular risk reduction. Despite widespread use, GLP‐1RAs have not been well studied in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA). We examined the effects of GLP‐1RAs on RA disease activity and cardiovascular risk profile in patients with RA and overweight or obesity.


#### Methods

We conducted a retrospective chart review study of patients with RA with a body mass index of ≥27 who were prescribed a GLP‐1RA (semaglutide or tirzepatide).

==Between 2018 and 2024, 173 patients with RA were identified in the treatment group (prescribed and took a GLP‐1RA), and 42 patients with RA were identified in the control group (prescribed but did not take GLP‐1RA)== ^kellner2025-1

. Patients were assessed at three‐month intervals for up to one year after prescription. Outcome measures included RA disease activity, cardiovascular risk markers, and patient‐reported outcomes. Changes in outcome measures within and between groups were assessed with linear mixed effect models, with adjustments for baseline characteristics that differed significantly between groups.


#### Results

GLP‐1RA–treated patients experienced significantly greater reductions in RA disease activity, pain, body weight, total cholesterol, and glycosylated hemoglobin than controls (P < 0.05). Within the treatment group, there were also significant reductions in erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C‐reactive protein values, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol values, and triglyceride values (P < 0.05). Nearly one‐third of the treatment group discontinued the GLP‐1RA during the study period, with the most common adverse effect being gastrointestinal issues.


#### Conclusion

This study suggests that the use of GLP‐1RAs can improve RA disease activity and cardiovascular risk profile. Although further research is needed, this novel finding has significant clinical implications because it suggests that anti‐obesity medications may improve both cardiovascular and RA outcomes.


## INTRODUCTION

Patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and obesity (RAO) face unique challenges because obesity is linked to increased disease activity and poorer treatment outcomes. 1 More than 30% of North American patients with RA are classified as having RAO. 2 These individuals, in addition to individuals with obesity without RA, are in a chronic proinflammatory state due to the overproduction of inflammatory cytokines and adipocytokines. 3 Heightened inflammation, along with worse arthritis symptoms in obesity, may exacerbate RA progression and make remission more difficult to achieve. 1 Patients with RAO also tend to have lower response rates to therapy and higher rates of cycling through RA treatments. 1 Additionally, obesity in patients with RA is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, which remains the leading cause of death in this population. 4 Higher RA disease activity is also associated with increased risk of cardiovascular events. 5 , 6 Research suggests that weight loss may help reduce RA disease activity. 7 , 8 Therefore, it is standard practice to advocate for weight loss in patients with RAO, with many being prescribed a newly popularized class of weight loss medications, glucagon‐like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs).

GLP‐1RAs play key roles in the treatment of diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular risk. 9 , 10 , 11 Although they are increasingly used in the general population, there is a

paucity of data on their clinical effects in patients with RA.

Specifically, their impact on RA disease activity and cardiovascular risk factors in this patient population remains poorly characterized. The objective of this study was to examine the effects of GLP‐1RAs on RA disease activity and cardiovascular risk profile in patients with RA and overweight or obesity.


## PATIENTS AND METHODS


### Patient population

This was a single‐center, retrospective, observational study using data from 2018 to 2024. The study was approved by the University of California, Los Angeles Institutional Review Board (reference no. 19‐1898). Query of the electronic health record (EHR) yielded a cohort of 554 patients who met the following criteria: at least two RA ICD‐10 billing codes, a body mass index (BMI) of ≥27 (chosen based on indications for GLP‐1RA), and a prescription of either semaglutide (oral or subcutaneous [SC]) or tirzepatide (SC) per pharmacy records. Detailed chart review of the 554 patients showed that 229 had RA, confirmed by the presence of an RA diagnosis in two or more rheumatology clinic notes. Of these 229 patients, 173 took the prescribed GLP‐1RA of interest, forming the treatment group. A control group comprised 42 patients with RA and a BMI of ≥27 who were prescribed but did not take a GLP‐1RA during the same period. All patients identified in the treatment and control groups were included in the analysis regardless of treatment duration, and when available, baseline was defined as the date of GLP‐1RA prescription for both groups. However, this date could not always be reliably confirmed in the control group due to EHR documentation limitations. When the prescription date was unavailable, we used the first available rheumatology note after it was indicated that a GLP‐1RA had been prescribed to serve as the start date.


### Data collection

Patient charts were abstracted for demographics, weight, obesity‐related comorbidities, clinical and laboratory values, RA disease activity, RA therapies, and adverse events at three‐month intervals (as available in clinic notes) for up to a year after the prescription of a GLP‐1RA. Race and ethnicity were self‐reported, taken from a questionnaire with fixed categories. Due to incomplete data for standard composite RA disease activity scores, RA disease activity was categorized as remission, mild, moderate, or severe based on two reviewers’ independent assessments of the rheumatology clinic notes. For each patient at each time point, RA disease activity was converted to a numeric score (remission = 0, mild = 1, moderate = 2, severe = 3). The average change in RA disease activity score was computed for each patient across all available time points.

Escalation and de‐escalation of RA therapy during the study was assessed by evaluating any RA treatment change from the previous time point and categorizing as it as escalation, de‐escalation, or the same. RA therapy status at each time point excluding baseline was converted to a numeric score (de‐escalation = −1, same = 0, escalation = 1). The average RA therapy status score was computed for each patient across all available time points.


### Statistical analysis

Baseline demographic variables were compared between treatment and control groups using a two‐sample t‐test for continuous variables and a chi‐square test for categorical variables. Change from baseline was calculated for clinical outcome variables observed at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. To analyze change in clinical outcome variables within groups, linear mixed effect models were fitted separately to the treatment and control groups, with time (baseline vs postbaseline) as the independent variable. To compare the change in clinical outcome variables between groups, a linear mixed effect model was fit; in this model, the independent variables were group, time, and group‐time interaction. After primary analysis, sensitivity analysis was performed, adjusting for diabetes, hypertension, race, and seropositivity. The correlation between weight loss and other clinical variables was calculated with Pearson correlation coefficients. A significance level of 0.05 was used in all analyses. All available observations were included in the analyses, and no data were imputed. Statistical analysis was performed using R, version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, http://www.r-project.org/).


## RESULTS


### Baseline RA patient characteristics for treatment and control groups

At baseline, the treatment and control groups did not differ significantly in terms of BMI, RA disease activity, disease‐modifying antirheumatic drug use, or prednisone use (Table 1). The control group did, however, have a higher prevalence of RA seropositivity. The mean baseline pain visual analog scale (VAS) score was higher in the treatment group. The treatment group had a significantly higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes, as well as higher mean glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and serum triglyceride values. There were significantly more White patients in the treatment group than in the control group.

### Changes in outcomes within the treatment group

Within the treatment group, there were significant reductions in erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR; −3.0 mm/hr, P = 0.004), C‐reactive protein (CRP; −0.6 mg/dL, P = 0.004), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (−7.3 mg/dL, P = 0.002), and triglyceride values (−16.4 mg/dL, P = 0.004), whereas there were no significant reductions in these variables within the control group.

==However, in the between‐group mixed effects model fitted to all data, the change in these variables did not differ significantly between groups.== ^kellner2025-6


### Association of weight loss with outcomes

When assessing for correlation between weight loss and other clinical outcome variables, there were weak but statistically significant correlations with reduction in HbA1c (r = 0.25, P < 0.001), total cholesterol (r = 0.15, P = 0.04), systolic blood pressure (r = 0.18, P < 0.001), and diastolic blood pressure values (r = 0.14, P < 0.001). There was no significant correlation between weight loss and reduction in VAS pain score (r = 0.09, P = 0.08) or acute phase reactants (ESR: r = 0.03, P = 0.61; CRP: r = 0.01, P = 0.87). When we generated mixed effect models including percentage weight loss as a binarized fixed effect (weight loss <5% vs ≥5%), HbA1c values decreased significantly more in those who lost 5% body weight or more (P = 0.02); the other variables mentioned did not differ significantly.


### GLP‐1RA dosing and adverse effects

Patients in the treatment group were started on either SC semaglutide (n = 143, 84%), oral semaglutide (n = 15, 8%), or SC tirzepatide (n = 15, 8%). The average weekly starting dose and 12‐month dose of SC semaglutide, oral semaglutide, and SC tirzepatide were 0.4 mg and 0.9 mg, 4.4 mg and 4.6 mg, and 4.3 mg and 7.1 mg, respectively.

## DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the effect of GLP‐1RA use on RA disease activity and cardiovascular risk profile in patients with RA and overweight or obesity. We found that GLP‐1RA use was associated with significant improvement in RA disease activity, pain, and cardiovascular risk profile. The presence of diabetes did not meaningfully affect these results, suggesting that these benefits were not unique to patients with diabetes. Furthermore,

==neither reduction in acute phase reactants nor reduction in pain was significantly correlated with weight loss, hinting that the effect on these outcomes may be modulated by mechanisms beyond weight loss== ^kellner2025-4

. Interestingly, we observed these results despite suboptimal dosing of GLP‐1RA for weight loss, suggesting that more pronounced effects may be seen at target doses for weight loss.

The cardiovascular benefits of GLP‐1RA in patients with obesity and diabetes are well established. 9 However, the possible anti‐inflammatory effects of this drug class are a subject of ongoing investigation, and their impact on inflammatory arthritis remains unknown. Existing research has shown that GLP‐1RA may down‐regulate the secretion of multiple proinflammatory cytokines, suggesting a possible mechanism by which they may impact RA disease activity. 12 Additionally, GLP‐1RAs have been shown to modulate arthritis pain through multiple possible mechanisms. 13 Recently, a randomized controlled trial of SC semaglutide in patients with obesity and knee osteoarthritis (OA) found that drug use resulted in significantly greater reduction in OA‐related pain than placebo. 14 The extent to which these effects are mediated by weight loss versus weight loss–independent mechanisms is an area of active research; as we have noted, our findings suggest that GLP‐1RA effects on pain and inflammation may be independent of weight loss.

Notably, nearly one‐third of the treatment group discontinued the prescribed GLP‐1RA during the study period, with the most common reasons for discontinuation being gastrointestinal adverse effects and insurance‐related difficulties. These issues are important to address when prescribing GLP‐1RAs because they have the potential to impact patient‐related outcomes and markedly limit drug efficacy if target dosing cannot be achieved.

In this study, there was a significantly higher proportion of White patients in the treatment group than in the control group (71% vs 47%, P = 0.01). Because both groups were prescribed a GLP‐1RA, this difference may reflect disparities in medication use rather than prescribing practices, potentially influenced by factors such as insurance approval, cost, cultural beliefs, trust in the health care system, or concerns about side effects. These barriers may disproportionately affect Black, Hispanic, and Asian patients because retrospective data demonstrate lower GLP‐1RA treatment rates in these groups compared to White patients. 15 Therefore, future studies should prioritize increasing cohort diversity to assess whether results are broadly applicable.

Although encouraging, the results of this study should be interpreted in the context of several key limitations. These include its single‐center, retrospective design; the limited sample size; and the potential for unmeasured confounding factors—such as reasons underlying the decision to initiate GLP‐1RA therapy—that could have influenced RA outcomes. Although we performed sensitivity analyses adjusting for diabetes, hypertension, race, and seropositivity, residual confounding from unmeasured factors may still be present. Additionally, EHR progress notes often lacked composite RA disease activity scores (eg, Disease Activity Score in 28 joints, Clinical Disease Activity Index).

==We assessed disease activity using the categorization system described already, but this is less precise than a validated RA disease activity score.== ^kellner2025-7

Despite these limitations, our observational data provide timely and important insights that may inform future research.

Our findings that GLP‐1RAs are associated with decreases in RA disease activity, cardiovascular risk factors, and pain in patients with RA and overweight or obesity are promising and have substantial clinical implications for this patient population. They suggest that this drug class, typically used for managing diabetes and obesity, may serve as a dual‐purpose treatment for patients with RA, addressing both systemic inflammation and associated comorbidities, such as cardiovascular disease, which is especially prevalent in patients with RAO and remains the leading cause of death in patients with RA overall. Reductions in disease activity and pain can improve patients’ quality of life, whereas improvement in cardiovascular risk factors may mitigate the heightened risk of cardiovascular disease in this population. Going forward, clinicians may consider integrating GLP‐1RAs into the treatment regimens for patients with RAO, not only to target obesity‐related complications but also possibly to target the underlying inflammatory disease process. However, further research is needed to confirm that the improvements in systemic inflammation and joint symptoms observed with these therapies are also tied to improvements in joint inflammation.

In conclusion, we found that GLP‐1RA use was associated with decreases in RA disease activity, pain, and cardiovascular risk factors in a real‐world cohort of patients with RA and overweight or obesity. The recent proliferation of GLP‐1RAs has had a remarkable impact on the management of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular risk in the general population, but the impact of these drugs on patients with RA has, to this point, been poorly studied. Our study is among the first to assess the effects of GLP‐1RAs on patients with RA, and our findings suggest that they may beneficially impact RA care in several important ways. Further study in the form of prospective trials is needed to better characterize their benefits and risks in this patient population.
