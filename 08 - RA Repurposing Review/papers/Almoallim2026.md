# Treat-to-Target Strategy Achieves High Remission Rates with Sex Differences Attenuated Under Treat-to-Target Management in Rheumatoid Arthritis: A Longitudinal Analysis From the Rheumatoid Arthritis Saudi Database (RASD)

## Abstract


#### Purpose

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) affects women two–three times more frequently than men, and registries report sex-based differences in disease activity and remission. However, sex-stratified outcome data from Saudi Arabia are limited. We evaluated sex differences in disease activity, remission rates, and predictors of remission in the Rheumatoid Arthritis Saudi Database (RASD).


#### Patients and Methods

We conducted a prospective cohort study of 647 patients with RA receiving biological or targeted synthetic DMARDs (574 female and 73 male) with at least 12 months of follow-up. Disease activity was assessed using DAS28-CRP, CDAI, FSS, and HAQ-DI across three visits. Remission was defined as DAS28-CRP <2.6. Linear mixed-effects models examined disease activity trajectories by sex, and multivariable logistic regression identified predictors of remission at visit 3. Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied across six Stage-4 chi-square tests and across three pairwise sex comparisons within each outcome.


#### Results

Overall remission rates rose from 50.9% at visit 1 to 83.5% at visit 3, with no significant sex differences after correction for multiple comparisons. DAS28-CRP declined significantly over time in both sexes (p <0.001). Males had higher adjusted CDAI at Visit 1 (13.43 vs 11.12; BH-adjusted p = 0.007), but not at visits 2 or 3; the visit-by-sex interaction was significant (unadjusted Type III p = 0.032). Fatigue and functional disability did not differ by sex. ACPA positivity was associated with higher odds of remission (OR 1.67, 95% CI 1.03–2.68, p = 0.036), while RF positivity was associated with lower odds (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.38–0.96; p = 0.034). Sex was not an independent predictor.


#### Conclusion

In this Saudi RA cohort, both sexes achieved high remission rates under treat-to-target management, and sex differences in disease activity did not persist after multiple-comparison correction. RF and ACPA, rather than sex, were the dominant predictors of remission.


## Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic multisystem autoimmune disease characterized by persistent synovial inflammation, which leads to progressive joint destruction and functional disability.1 Globally, RA affects approximately 0.5–1% of the population, and women are two to three times more likely to develop the disease than men.2–4 This sex difference has attracted attention as to whether sex also influences disease severity, treatment response, and long-term outcomes.

Current evidence suggests that this is the case. Women with RA reported higher disease activity scores, greater pain and fatigue, and worse functional outcomes than men, even though the inflammatory markers were similar.5–7 Several mechanisms may explain this discrepancy. Sex hormones regulate immune function, and the difference between both sexes may be due to immune suppression and immune aggravation due to hormonal effects.6,8 Differences in pain perception further complicate the picture, as women show lower pain thresholds and altered central sensitization, which may inflate the subjective components of composite disease activity measures, such as the Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS28).6,9–11

Previous studies have reported sex-associated differences in RA outcomes; however, the magnitude of these differences may depend partly on the disease-activity measure used. In a large registry analysis, discordance between DAS28-CRP and DAS28-ESR was greatest in women and affected classification at remission and low-disease-activity thresholds.12 In the Swiss Clinical Quality Management registry, men had 21% higher odds of DAS28 remission at 12 months on biological therapy.9 The CORRONA study similarly found that male sex was associated with sustained remission in early RA.2 These findings suggest that a one-size-fits-all approach to treat-to-target strategies is not the best for either sex.

Despite this growing evidence, most of the published studies were from other cohorts. Published Saudi reports have described RASD disease characteristics and remission rates and other cross-sectional outcomes; however, we did not identify a prior study in the supplied reference library reporting sex-stratified longitudinal trajectories of DAS28-CRP, CDAI, FSS, and HAQ-DI among patients receiving biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs. Studies on RA prevalence were similar to those of other studies, with a similar female predominance.3 However, cultural, environmental, and genetic factors in this population may influence disease expression and treatment outcomes in ways that cannot be expected from international data alone.4–7 The Rheumatoid Arthritis Saudi Database (RASD) provides an opportunity to address this gap.6,8,10,13 RA management has been increasingly guided by treat-to-target, with regular assessment of disease activity and timely treatment escalation in pursuit of remission or low disease activity. However, whether this approach attenuates sex-related differences in outcomes in routine practice remains unclear. RASD provides an opportunity to examine these questions in a well-characterized longitudinal cohort managed by specialized rheumatology care. Our study aimed to evaluate sex differences in disease activity trajectories, remission rates, and predictors of remission among patients with RA enrolled in RASD.


## Materials and Methods


### Study Design and Setting

We conducted a prospective cohort study using the RASD data. We aimed to evaluate sex differences in disease activity, remission rates, and clinical outcomes among patients diagnosed with RA. We followed patients with RA managed between January 2015 and December 2025. Institutional review board (IRB) approval was obtained from all participating sites of the RASD. Local IRB approval was obtained at all participating centers, with primary ethical oversight provided by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Research Center, International Medical Center (IMCIRB #: 2023–08-225; NCBE Registration No: H-02-J-010). Our study is in concordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from all participating patients prior to data collection.


### Study Population

The included patients were aged ≥18 years at the time of RA diagnosis, met the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria, had at least 12 months of follow-up, and we did not collect the exact timing of each individual visit for the patients included in the study. However, every single patient included in the analysis had completed 3 visits with 3–6-month intervals and was receiving biological or targeted synthetic DMARD therapy. Patients were excluded if they had an overlap of connective tissue disease at baseline, incomplete records preventing primary outcome assessment, or pregnancy during the follow-up.


### Data Collection and Variables

The collected data included demographics (age, sex, BMI, smoking status, occupation, education, and nationality), disease-related variables (RA duration, diagnostic lag times, and serological markers including ACPA, RF, and ANA), comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, thyroid dysfunction, osteoporosis, and osteoarthritis), and the biologic or targeted synthetic DMARD used.


### Outcome Measures

Disease activity was assessed using the Disease Activity Score in 28 joints based on C-reactive protein (DAS28-CRP) levels at three longitudinal visits. Remission was defined as a DAS28-CRP <2.6, with disease activity further categorized as low (2.6–3.2), moderate (3.2–5.1), or high (>5.1). Additional outcomes included the Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI), Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS), and Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index (HAQ-DI).


### Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range, and categorical variables as frequencies (%). Sex differences were tested using the Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables and chi-square (or Fisher’s exact) tests for categorical variables. Remission rates and disease activity were compared between sexes at each visit, with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) equation.

Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were fitted for the DAS28-CRP, CDAI, FSS, and HAQ-DI to examine changes over time. Each model included visits, sex, and their interaction as fixed effects, with patient-level random intercepts. The DAS28-CRP model was additionally adjusted for ACPA and RF to account for their potential association with the CRP-based disease-activity outcome; the CDAI, FSS, and HAQ-DI models were adjusted for age, RA duration, and BMI. Adjusted mean scores were estimated for each sex at each visit and pairwise differences were tested using BH-corrected p-values.

The predictors of remission at visit three were assessed using logistic regression. The final model included sex, age, RA duration, BMI, smoking status, ACPA status, RF status, baseline DAS28-CRP, and any comorbidity. Candidate variables were first assessed in univariable models; variables considered clinically relevant or associated with remission at p < 0.20 were considered for multivariable modeling. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A post-hoc two-sample proportion calculation was performed for the observed Visit 3 remission rates to assess the sensitivity of the female-versus-male comparison. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.3), with the significance set at p <0.05.


## Results


### Baseline Characteristics

We retrieved and included total of 647 RA patients, about 574 (88.7%) were female and 73 (11.3%) were male. All 647 included participants had DAS28-CRP assessments at Visits 1, 2, and 3; therefore, no participant was excluded from the primary remission analysis because of incomplete DAS28-CRP follow-up. HAQ-DI at Visit 1 was unavailable for 2 female participants. The median age of the patients was 50 years, with no significant difference between the sexes. The median BMI was 29 kg/m2, and 43% of patients were categorized as obese. The majority of our patients were non-smokers (96%); however, as expected, smoking was significantly more prevalent among males (8.2% vs 2.6%, p = 0.013).

The median RA duration was 7 years, which was similar between sexes (p = 0.968). The RF positivity rate was 57%, with no significant sex difference. The median referral time from the first visit to rheumatology was significantly shorter in males (p = 0.006). Comorbidities were common, but there were no significant differences according to sex. ### Independent Predictors of Remission at Visit 3

In univariate analyses, no predictor reached statistical significance for remission at visit three. In the multivariable model, two serological markers were independently significant: ACPA positivity was associated with higher odds of remission (OR 1.67, 95% CI 1.03–2.68, p = 0.036), while RF positivity was associated with lower odds (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.38–0.96, p = 0.034). ### Post-Hoc Power Assessment

A post-hoc two-sample comparison of Visit 3 remission rates (women: 478/574 [83.3%]; men: 62/73 [84.9%]) yielded Cohen’s h = 0.045 and approximate two-sided power of 6.5% at α = 0.05.


## Discussion

In this treat-to-target cohort, the DAS28-CRP remission rates increased from approximately 51% at baseline to 84% at the final visit, with no statistically significant difference between men and women after correction for multiple comparisons. CDAI trajectories revealed a transient early divergence, with men presenting with higher baseline CDAI scores, but this difference was resolved by visit 3 and remained parallel thereafter, consistent with protocol-driven equalization of disease control across sexes. Multivariable logistic regression identified RF positivity and ACPA positivity as independent predictors of remission, whereas biological sex was not a significant predictor after adjusting for serological status, disease duration, and baseline disease activity. These findings indicate that, within a structured management framework using a CRP-based composite outcome, sex does not independently determine the likelihood of remission.

These findings differ from those of the prevailing literature in several ways. Large multinational registries have consistently reported that women are less likely to achieve remission than are men. The Swiss SCQM study reported a 21% higher odds of DAS28 remission in men,9 and the CORRONA cohort found a significant male advantage for sustained remission in early RA.2 In contrast, our cohort showed no statistically significant sex difference in remission rates at any visit after BH correction. Several explanations may explain this difference. First, our cohort data were drawn from a specialized rheumatology center, which may have attenuated sex-based differences in treatment access.8,13 Second, the low median DAS28-CRP at baseline (2.7) may have limited the ability to detect sex differences because many patients were already near the remission threshold, which is consistent with the previous evidence that rheumatoid arthritis in Saudi Arabia tends to be less aggressive compared to other populations.6,7,10,11 Third, early access to biological and targeted synthetic DMARDs in Saudi Arabia may contribute to the high remission rates we observed, which narrows the gender-based differences seen in other settings.14

The present findings contrast with a substantial body of registry data in which a male advantage in remission has been consistently reported, yet the coherence of the literature dissolves considerably once the outcome measure used in each study is examined. The most widely cited source is the QUEST-RA multinational cohort, in which women had lower DAS28-ESR remission rates than men. Similar patterns were reported in SCQM and other ESR-based analyses, whereas studies using DAS28-CRP, CDAI, or imaging-based outcomes often showed attenuation or disappearance of the sex effect. Taken together, this measure-stratified cross-registry evidence provides a coherent framework for the absence of a significant sex difference in the remission rates in the present cohort. A major explanation for this is the choice of measurement. ESR is physiologically influenced by sex, and DAS28-ESR may therefore systematically inflate disease activity in women, independent of the true inflammatory burden. In contrast, the present study used DAS28-CRP as the primary outcome, which reduces this source of bias and is more appropriate for sex comparative analyses. A second explanation is treatment timing; in other registries, women often initiate advanced therapy later in the disease course, whereas treat-to-target frameworks may attenuate this inequity through more uniform escalation. A third explanation is disease duration because sex effects on remission appear more pronounced in early disease and less consistent in established RA.

Our longitudinal mixed-effects model revealed a complex outcome pattern. For DAS28-CRP, both sexes improved significantly over time, while males started numerically higher but ended slightly lower by visit 3, but there was no significant difference in between-sex comparison after adjustment.10 The CDAI analysis was more informative: males had significantly higher scores at visit 1, but this difference was resolved by visit 3, with a significant visit-by-sex interaction confirming that the intersex course of the disease differed. This pattern of higher baseline disease activity in males with subsequent catch-up is consistent with previous studies.15,16

Our finding that RF positivity was associated with higher disease activity and lower odds of remission is consistent with previous studies.17

In contrast, ACPA positivity was independently associated with higher odds of remission in our cohort. This association should be interpreted cautiously, because treatment type, disease duration, and residual confounding may influenced the estimate. Serological status may have clinical relevance beyond prognosis. Although RF positivity was associated with lower odds of overall remission in our cohort, RF and/or ACPA positivity may identify subgroups with more favorable responses to selected therapies. In particular, high anti-CCP antibody titres were associated with a better response to rituximab, and ACPA positivity has been associated with more favorable outcomes with abatacept than with TNF inhibitors.18,19 In our cohort, the opposing effects of RF and ACPA on remission, even after mutual adjustment, suggest that these autoantibodies may capture different dimensions of disease biology relevant to treatment outcomes. Because we did not evaluate drug-specific responses according to serology levels, our interpretation should be confirmed in treatment-stratified prospective studies. Also, sex was not an independent predictor of remission in either univariate or multivariable analyses, reinforcing that serological rather than demographic factors directed the outcome differences in our cohort.

Although ACPA is an indicator of more severe disease progression, the interaction between serological and therapeutic outcomes remains complex.20 Some studies have found that seropositive patients respond better to certain biological therapies, especially rituximab and abatacept.14,18,19 In our cohort, the opposing effects of RF and ACPA on remission, even after mutual adjustment, suggested that these autoantibodies may capture different dimensions of the disease course relevant to treatment outcomes. Importantly, sex was not an independent predictor of remission in either univariate or multivariate analyses, reinforcing that serological rather than demographic factors direct the outcome differences in our cohort.

We also found that a longer RA duration was associated with worse scores across all four outcomes in mixed-effects models. Our findings stress what is already known, that early diagnosis and treatment of RA is the concept of a therapeutic window of opportunity for better outcomes in RA. It also shows that the duration of disease, rather than patient sex, is a more important determinant of disease burden in this population.13,21

Our study has several strengths. It provides the first detailed sex-stratified longitudinal analysis of disease activity outcomes from RASD, filling an important evidence gap, as sex-stratified evidence remains insufficient.6,10 We also used statistical models to analyze patient data over time and applied methods to ensure that our results on sex differences were reliable.

The inclusion of multiple disease activity measures (DAS28-CRP, CDAI, FSS, and HAQ-DI) provides a more detailed picture than studies that rely on a single index.

This study had several limitations. The small number of males reflects the known female predominance of RA but limits the statistical power for detecting modest sex differences. This low observed power indicates limited sensitivity to detect small between-sex differences, particularly given the small male subgroup. We were unable to capture psychosocial factors, treatment adherence, or healthcare utilization patterns that may differ according to sex and affect outcomes. While our study data were obtained from five centers across Saudi Arabia through the RASD registry, inclusion of patients receiving biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs may have selected a treated population that does not represent patients receiving conventional DMARDs or treatment-naive patients; this may limit generalizability. Finally, we used standard DAS28-CRP thresholds without sex-specific adjustment; given recent evidence that these thresholds may systematically misclassify disease activity in women,12 future studies applying sex-stratified cutoffs may yield different conclusions.


## Conclusions

In our RA cohort, both sexes achieved high remission rates under current treat-to-target management, and sex differences in disease activity did not persist after correction for multiple comparisons. RF and ACPA levels were the dominant predictors of remission, whereas sex was not. These findings suggest that sex differences in RA outcomes may be attenuated in this treated cohort; confirmation in larger multicenter studies with adequate male representation is needed. Prospective multicenter studies with adequate male representation and assessment of biological, psychosocial, and healthcare-related factors are needed to fully characterize sex-specific determinants of RA outcomes in this region.
