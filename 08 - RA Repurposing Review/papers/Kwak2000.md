Inhibitors of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase, or statins, are effective lipid-lowering agents, extensively used in medical practice1–3. Statins have never been shown to be involved in the immune response, although a report has indicated a better outcome of cardiac transplantation in patients under Pravastatin therapy4. Major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) molecules are directly involved in the activation of T lymphocytes and in the control of the immune response. Whereas only a limited number of specialized cell types express MHC-II constitutively, numerous other cells become MHC-II positive upon induction by interferon γ (IFN-γ)5. This complex regulation is under the control of the transactivator CIITA (refs 6,7). Here we show that statins act as direct inhibitors of induction of MHC-II expression by IFN-γ and thus as repressors of MHC-II-mediated T-cell activation. This effect of statins is due to inhibition of the inducible promoter IV of the transactivator CIITA and is observed in several cell types, including primary human endothelial cells (ECs) and monocytemacrophages (Mφ). It is of note that this inhibition is specific for inducible MHC-II expression and does not concern constitutive expression of CIITA and MHC-II. In repressing induction of MHC-II, and subsequent T-lymphocyte activation, statins therefore provide a new type of immunomodulation. This unexpected effect provides a scientific rationale for using statins as immunosuppressors, not only in organ transplantation but in numerous other pathologies as well.

Inhibitors of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase (or statins) represent a newly discovered family of chemically related molecules, selected for their lipid-lowering effect. Statins are extensively used in medical practice, and large clinical trials have demonstrated that this class of lipid-lowering drugs greatly reduces cardiovascular-related morbidity and mortality in patients with and without coronary disease1–3,8. Recent in vitro findings indicate that statins, in addition to their lipid-lowering effects, have anti-inflammatory properties and thus may regulate important molecules in vascular biology9.

As part of an exploration of possible interfaces between immune mechanisms and atherogenesis, and to evaluate possible beneficial effects of statins independently of their well-known effect as lipid-lowering agents, we have analyzed their effect on various features of the control of major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) expression and of subsequent lymphocyte activation. We studied the effect of several statins on the regulation of both constitutive MHC-II expression in highly specialized antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and inducible MHC-II expression by interferon γ (IFN-γ) in a variety of other cell types, including primary cultures of human endothelial cells (ECs) and monocytemacrophages (Mφ). Experiments were performed to monitor cell

surface expression (assayed both by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS; Fig. 1a–f), and by immunofluorescence (Fig. 1g–l), as well as mRNA levels (RNase protection assay, RPA; Fig. 2a) of MHC-II. These investigations have led to the following conclusions: (1) Statins effectively repress the induction of MHC-II expression by IFN-γ and do so in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1a,b, g–l). (2) In the presence of L-mevalonate, the effect of statins on MHC-II expression is abolished, indicating that it is indeed the effect of statins as HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors that mediates repression of MHC-II (Fig. 1c). (3) Repression of MHC-II expression by statins is highly specific for the inducible form of MHC-II expression and does not concern constitutive expression of MHC-II in highly specialized antigen-presenting cells (APCs), such as dendritic cells (DCs) and B lymphocytes (Fig. 1d,e). (4) This effect of statins is specific for MHC-II and does not concern MHC class I (MHC-I) expression (Fig. 1f). (5) In order to investigate functional consequences of statin-induced inhibition of MHC-II expression, we carried out mixed-lymphocyte reactions (MLRs; allogeneic T lymphocytes incubated with IFN-γ-pretreated human ECs or Mφ). T-cell proliferation could be blocked by monoclonal antibodies against MHC-II. Pretreatment of ECs or Mφ with statins reduces subsequent T-lymphocyte proliferation, as measured by [3H]thymidine incorporation (Fig. 3a) and interleukin 2 (IL-2) release (Fig. 3b).

The previously unknown effect of statins as MHC-II repressors was also observed and confirmed in other cell types, including primary human smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts, as well as in established cell lines such as ThP1, melanomas, and HeLa cells (data not shown). This effect of statins on MHC-II induction is observed with different forms of statins currently used in clinical medicine. It is, however, notable that the potency of statins as MHC-II “repressors” varied widely according to the form of statin (see Fig. 1a). Of the forms tested, the most powerful MHC-II repressor is Atorvastatin. It will be of value to screen other members of the statin family, as well as statin analogues, in order to optimize the potential for MHC-II repression.

Discovery of this unexpected effect of statins led us to additional exploration of its mechanism of action in inhibiting MHC-II expression. Regulation of expression of MHC-II genes is highly complex, and this tight control directly affects T-lymphocyte activation and thus the control of the immune response. This complex regulation has now been dissected in great detail, largely as a result of a rare human disease of MHC-II regulation, called the bare lymphocyte syndrome (or MHC-II deficiency)5. that binds to the X box of MHC-II promoters5,10, CIITA is the general controller of MHC-II expression, and its own expression is tightly regulated6,7. It is of note that expression of CIITA is controlled by several alternative promoters, operating under distinct physiological conditions11. CIITA promoter I controls constitutive expression in DCs; promoter III controls constitutive expression in B lymphocytes, and CIITA promoter IV is specifically responsible for the IFN-γ-inducible expression of CIITA and thus of MHC-II (ref. 11). The molecular basis of inducibility of CIITA promoter IV has been elucidated in detail12. The observed effect of statins on inducible expression of MHC-II was thus analyzed in this context.

Repression of induction of MHC-II by IFN-γ in statin-treated samples is paralleled by a reduced induction of CIITA mRNA by IFN-γ (Fig. 2a,b), indicating an inhibition by statins of induction

of the CIITA gene. Interestingly, the varying degrees of repression of CIITA mRNA induction observed with the different forms of statins (Fig. 2b) are reflected in the different levels of repression of MHC-II expression observed with the same drugs (Fig.1a). This confirms the quantitative nature of the control of CIITA over MHC-II gene activity13. Constitutive expression of MHC-II, known to be mediated by CIITA promoters I and III, is not affected by statins (Fig. 1d,e). The specificity of statins for repressing inducible and not constitutive MHC-II expression indicates an effect on CIITA promoter IV. Indeed, we show that induction of expression of the first exon specifically controlled by CIITA promoter IV is affected by statins (Fig. 4a). Finally, the statin effect is transcriptional, as demonstrated by actinomycin D experiments used to block de novo RNA synthesis and explore mRNA half-life (Fig 4b). This effect is direct and does not require de novo protein synthesis, as shown by a lack of effect in cycloheximide experiments (data not shown).

the statin effect reported here is not due to an impairment of Stat1α activation. In fact, normal phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of Stat1α occur in the presence of statins (Fig. 4c).

These findings regarding the mechanism of inhibition by statins of MHC-II induction indicate a selective effect of statins on the induction of expression of promoter IV of the MHC-II transactivator CIITA. Future studies will elucidate whether there is a link between mevalonate blockade due to statins and either the availability or the cooperative binding of the three well-defined transcription factors Stat1, USF-1, and IRF-1 required for the activity of CIITA promoter IV (ref. 12). Inhibition of inducible expression of MHC-II molecules on the considerable variety of cells that normally become MHC-II positive under the effect of IFN-γ is expected to have multiple functional consequences. Among these are activation of endogenous CD4 T lymphocytes, as well as recognition of MHC-II molecules by CD4 T cells in an allogeneic context following organ transplantation.

An isolated clinical observation suggesting a beneficial effect of Pravastatin on the outcome of heart transplantation was reported by Kobashigawa and colleagues4. This observation was left unexplained, however, because statins had never before been connected to the immune system. Here we report a newly discovered effect of statins as an effective repressor of MHC-II expression and describe the mechanism of this effect as repression of promoter IV of the MHC-II transactivator CIITA. The fact that statin-induced repression of MHC-II also represses MHC-II-dependent activation of T lymphocytes indicates that statin is likely to have an immunosuppressive effect. This provides a firm scientific rationale to recommend the use of this drug as an immunosuppressor in organ transplantation. It also suggests numerous other practical clinical applications using statins as immunomodulators, particularly in diseases in which aberrant expression of MHC-II and/or aberrant activation of CD4 T lymphocytes are implicated. Besides organ transplantation, then, this could include various

autoimmune diseases such as type I diabetes, multiple sclerosis, and rheumatoid arthritis, as well as psoriasis and chronic inflammatory diseases like atherosclerosis. The high degree of patient tolerance of statins makes them potentially a welcome addition to the limited current arsenal of immunosuppressive agents. In vivo studies will be necessary to confirm their efficacy for such applications.

Immunolabeling. Cells grown on coverslips were fixed for 5 min with methanol at –20 °C. The coverslips were rinsed and incubated successively with 0.2% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 1 h, 0.5 M NH4Cl in PBS for 15 min, and PBS supplemented with 2% bovine serum albumin (Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri) for another 30 min. Cells were then incubated overnight with primary antibody (1:200) in 10% normal goat serum (Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri)/PBS. All steps were performed at room temperature, and cells were rinsed with PBS in between incubation steps. Cells were counterstained with 0.03% Evans blue–PBS. Coverslips were mounted on slides in Vectashield (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, California). Cells were examined using a Zeiss Axiophot microscope equipped with appropriate filters. Specificity of the immunolabeling was checked for by replacing the primary antibody with PBS.

RNAse protection assays (RPAs). Total RNA was prepared with Tri reagent (MRC, Inc., Cincinnati, Ohio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. We carried out RPAs with 15 µg of RNA per reaction as described 12 using human probes for MHC-II (DR-α), CIITA, exon 1 of the promoter IV-specific form of CIITA (pIV-CIITA), and GAPDH as a control for RNA loading. Signal quantitation was determined using a phosphoimager analysis system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, California). Levels of DR-α, CIITA, and pIV-CIITA RNA in any given sample were normalized to the GAPDH signal for that sample.

Western blot analysis. Cells were harvested in ice-cold RadioImmunoPrecipitationAssay (RIPA) solubilization buffer, and total amounts of protein were determined using a bicinchoninic acid quantification assay (Pierce, Rockford, Illinois). Under reducing conditions, 50 µg of total protein/lane were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and blotted to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore Corp., Bedford, Massachusetts) using a semidry blotting apparatus (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, California). Mixed-lymphocyte reaction (MLR). MLRs were performed as described17. Briefly, human ECs and Mφ were cultured on 96-well plates pretreated with the respective stimuli and irradiated. Purified allogeneic human T lymphocytes were then mixed (2 to 4 × 105) with the coated cells for five days at 37 °C. Cells were pulsed with 5 µCi [3H]thymidine (NEN Life Science Products, Boston, Massachusetts) and cultured for an additional 24 h. [3H]Thymidine incorporation was measured by harvesting cells with a cell harvester (Tomtec, Orange, Connecticut) and counting the amount of radioactivity with a beta-plate reader (Wallac, Gaithersburg, Maryland). Results in counts per minute per well (maximum around 30,000 for lymphocytes plated on IFN-—treated ECs and 20,000 on IFN-—treated Mφ) were normalized to relative units in order to combine results from ECs and Mφ in one figure. Samples were assayed in triplicate.

Cytokine assay. Release of IL-2 from T lymphocytes was measured using ELISA kits, as suggested by the manufacturer (R&D System, Abingdon, United Kingdom). Experiments were performed in the presence of polymyxin B (1 µg/ml). Antibody binding was detected by adding p-nitrophenyl phosphate (1.39 mg/ml), and absorbance was measured at 405 nm in a Dynatech plate reader (Vienna, Virginia). The amount of IL-2 detected was calculated from a standard curve prepared with human recombinant IL-2. Samples were assayed in triplicate.
