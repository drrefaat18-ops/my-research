# Adherence to metformin and the onset of rheumatoid arthritis: a population-based cohort study

**Authors:** Naffaa ME, Rosenberg V, Watad A, Tiosano S, Yavne Y, Chodick G, Amital H, Shalev V  
**Journal:** Scandinavian Journal of Rheumatology (2020)  
**DOI:** 10.1080/03009742.2019.1695928  
**PDF:** pdf/Naffaa2020.pdf

## Abstract

**Objective:** The aim of this retrospective cohort study was to examine whether adherence to metformin treatment may be associated with lower onset of rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

**Method:** Using the computerized databases of a 2.3-million state-mandated health services organization in Israel, we identified incident RA cases among a cohort of 113 749 adult patients who initiated metformin therapy between 1998 and 2014. Adherence was assessed by calculating the mean proportion of follow-up days covered (PDC) with metformin.

**Results:** During the 18 year study period, there were 558 incident RA cases (61 per 100 000 person-years). Adherence to metformin treatment was associated with a lower risk of developing RA, with the lowest risk recorded among patients with a PDC of 40–59% [adjusted hazard ratio (HR) 0.62, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.45–0.84] compared with non-adherent patients (PDC < 20%). A mean daily metformin dose of 2550 mg or more was also associated with a lower risk of developing RA (adjusted HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.46–0.84) compared to a daily dose of 850 mg or less. In stratified analyses by gender, the negative association between adherence and the risk of RA was limited to women alone.

**Conclusions:** Adherence to metformin treatment is associated with a reduced risk of developing RA in women. Further studies are needed to assess the effect of metformin on RA development in other patient populations.

## Introduction

Metformin, an oral anti-hyperglycaemic agent, is a central drug in the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) (1). Other indications for metformin include various conditions where insulin resistance is present, such as polycystic ovary syndrome, human immunodeficiency virus-associated lipodystrophy, acanthosis nigricans and, possibly, dementia-type neurodegenerative disorders (2–5).

It has been reported that metformin may have anti-inflammatory and anti-cancer properties, in addition to its anti-hyperglycaemic activity. Reduced cancer risk was tentatively indicated in the UK Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) and has subsequently been identified in large database analyses, suggesting that metformin may protect against certain cancers in individuals with T2DM, notably in the bowel where drug exposure is high (6–8). Experimental studies in animals have suggested that metformin may play a role in treating several chronic inflammatory disorders, such as inflammatory bowel disease (9, 10), multiple sclerosis (11), and autoimmune arthritis (12–14).

A clinical study suggested that metformin may reduce the risk of developing psoriasis (15). However, little is known about the association between metformin use and the risk of rheumatoid arthritis (RA), a complex inflammatory disease. RA is characterized by an abundant infiltration of inflammatory cells into the synovial tissue, which leads to the release of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines and matrix-degrading enzymes, facilitating the progressive joint destruction (16).

The aim of the current study was, therefore, to examine whether adherence to metformin treatment is associated with the long-term risk of RA in a large population of new users of metformin.

## Method

This retrospective cohort analysis was conducted using the data of Maccabi Healthcare Services (MHS), a 2.3-million member state-mandated health services organization operating in Israel. According to the Israeli National Health Insurance Act, MHS may not bar any citizen who wishes to join it, and therefore every section in the Israeli population is represented in MHS. The age (mean 33.6 years) and gender (48.7% female) distributions of MHS's countrywide member population are representative of the general Israeli population (17). All data were obtained from MHS's automated databases, which have previously been described (18), and were used to elicit information on all dispensed prescriptions, hospital discharge data, and biochemistry test results, using a unique personal national identification number. Research ethics approval was obtained from Bait-Balev Medical Centre institutional review board.

### Cohort definitions

We identified all adult (aged ≥18 years) patients who initiated therapy with metformin between 1998 and 2014 (inclusive). Date of first dispensed metformin was defined as the index date. Included patients had been MHS members for at least 12 months before the index date. Excluded were patients with a documented history of RA or purchase of the following immunosuppressive medications (methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, leflunomide, sulphasalzine, azathioprine, prednisone, tofacitinib, infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab pegol, golimumab, etanercept, abatecept, tocilizumab, rituximab, anakinra, auranofin) before the index date. To avoid potential surveillance bias, we also excluded RA cases diagnosed with proximity (up to 12 months) following the index date.

### Study outcomes

Incident cases of RA were defined according to the first diagnostic codes associated with RA [Rheumatoid arthritis; International Classification of Diseases, 9th revision (ICD-9) codes 714.x] given by a rheumatologist. To increase specificity, RA diagnoses given by primary care physicians (PCPs) or orthopaedists were considered valid only if given on at least two separate visits. To further validate the definition for RA patients, we collected data on purchases of disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs) and/or corticosteroids (methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, leflunomide, prednisone, tofacitinib, infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab pegol, golimumab, etanercept, abatecept, tocilizumab, rituximab, anakinra) following the RA diagnosis.

### Assessing adherence with metformin

Adherence was measured by assessing the proportion of follow-up days covered (PDC) with dispensed metformin (19, 20), by dividing the supply days of dispensed metformin by the total follow-up time (including the day when metformin was last dispensed). Since metformin daily dose may vary between patients (range 500–2550 mg), we also assessed the mean daily dose of metformin during the period of metformin treatment.

### Other study variables

Demographic variables at the index date included baseline values of age, gender, and socioeconomic level. Socioeconomic status (SES) was based on a score ranked from 1 (lowest) to 10 (Points Location Intelligence®) using geographic information systems (GIS) and data such as expenditure related to retail chains, credit card scores, and housing. This score is highly correlated with SES as measured by the Central Bureau of Statistics (21). SES was categorized into low (1–4), medium (5–7), and high (8–10). We also collected data on smoking status (ever smoked, never smoked). Patients with any of the following comorbid conditions at baseline [diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease (CVD), morbid obesity] were identified using the MHS computerized registries (22). Information on cancer history was provided by the Israel National Cancer Registry (INCR), which has collected information on diagnosed cancer cases from all medical institutions in Israel since 1960. Information on health services utilization, such as the number of hospitalizations in general hospitals and number of visits to the PCP, was based on data collected for the year prior to the index date. Laboratory test results for the presence of rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (ACPA) in patients diagnosed with RA were also included.

According to a previous study, adherence to statin treatment is associated with reduced risk of developing RA (23). Therefore, we included adherence to statin treatment by calculating the PDC during the study follow-up period.

### Statistical analysis

Chi-squared tests for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables were performed to determine significant differences in baseline characteristics and levels of metformin PDC [categorized by ≤20% (the reference category), 20–39%, 40–59%, 60–79%, and ≥80%]. In the primary analysis, a Cox proportional hazards model with years of follow-up as the timescale was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) (24), as well as to identify variables significantly associated with an increased risk of RA. Each study patient was followed from the index date to the earliest of the following events: RA diagnosis, death, leaving MHS, or 31 December 2015. The maximum follow-up was therefore 18 years. We also performed a Kaplan–Meier analysis for cumulative survival as RA-free patients, according to the levels of PDC with metformin. Analyses were further stratified by gender.

The full multivariable model included the following baseline values: age at baseline, gender, socioeconomic level, smoking status, presence of morbid obesity, visits to the PCP in the year prior to the index date, adherence to statin treatment during the study period, adherence to metformin treatment, and mean metformin daily dose. All analyses were performed by the IBM-SPSS version 24.0 standard statistical software.

## Results

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, we identified a total of 113 749 patients eligible for the study (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. The mean ± sd age was 57.5 ± 13.1 years, 48% were women, and the mean follow-up time was 8.0 ± 4.9 years. Patients with a high PDC with metformin (≥80%) were more likely to be older, and to have a higher prevalence of chronic disorders such as diabetes, CVDs, cancer, and morbid obesity compared to non-adherent patients. They also had more frequent hospitalizations and visits to the PCP, and more frequent use of statins.

During the study follow-up period (908 121 person-years), there were 558 incident RA cases, representing a crude incidence rate of 61 per 100 000 person-years. In all age groups, the incidence rate of RA among women was higher than in men and increased with age, with the maximum incidence identified among women in the 65–74 year age group (124 per 100 000 person-years). RF tests were available for 94.8% of the newly diagnosed RA cases, of whom 362 (64.8%) were positive for RF at ≥20 IU/mL and 210 (37.6%) were positive for RF at ≥40 IU/mL. ACPA tests were available for only 20.4% of them. Among the RA cases, we identified 341 (61.1%) patients who purchased DMARDs and/or prednisone at least once following the RA diagnosis.

In the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, we observed that patients with a PDC < 20% had the lowest survival as RA-free patients, while those with a PDC of 40–59% had the highest survival (Figure 2). In the fully adjusted model, older age, female gender, and number of visits to the PCP were significantly (p < 0.05) associated with increased RA occurrence, while obesity and use of statins were associated with a lower risk of RA (Table 2).

Adherence to metformin treatment was associated with a decreased risk of developing RA, with a PDC of 40–59% at the lowest risk (adjusted HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.45–0.84) compared to PDC of 20% or lower. Similarly, patients receiving a mean daily metformin dose of 2550 mg or more were less likely to develop RA (adjusted HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.46–0.84) compared to patients receiving 850 mg or lower dose. In a sensitivity analysis, we also included in the model the interaction between being adherent (PDC ≥40%) and taking a mean daily dose of metformin of ≥1700 mg. The analysis revealed that these patients were less likely to develop RA (adjusted HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.43–0.86).

In stratified analysis, adherence to metformin treatment was associated with a reduced risk of developing RA in women, reaching statistical significance at PDC of ≥40% compared to non-adherent patients. Metformin daily dose of ≥2550 mg was associated with a reduced risk of RA in women (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.43–0.88) and men (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.36–1.07) (Table 3).

## Discussion

The results of our study demonstrate a clinically important association between exposure to metformin treatment and decreased risk of developing RA. The association between adherence to metformin and RA risk was non-linear, levelling off at a PDC of 40%. This association is further supported by a significant inverse relationship between the daily dose of metformin and risk of RA. When we tested for the possibility of interaction between adherence to metformin and the total daily dose, we revealed that the association between exposure to metformin and risk of RA was even more prominent in those who were more adherent and at the same time treated with a higher dose of metformin, indicating an additive interaction effect.

Metformin exerts its anti-inflammatory effect through multiple mechanisms, of which the main one is thought to be activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK). AMPK activation can facilitate the anti-inflammatory effect of metformin in several ways. One such example is the inhibition of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) (25), which plays a key role in various biological processes, including immune response. Incorrect regulation of NF-κB contributes to the development of various autoimmune and inflammatory disorders, including RA (26). The inhibition of NF-κB leads to subsequent reduction in the production of nitric oxide, prostaglandin E2, and pro-inflammatory cytokines [interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, and tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)] by macrophages. Metformin can also exert an anti-inflammatory effect by direct inhibition of IL-6, TNF-α, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), as a result of a significant reduction in mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) phosphorylation (27). Moreover, the improvement in hyperglycaemia, weight gain, and lipid profile resulting from metformin use may have an indirect inhibitory effect on chronic inflammation (28). It has been reported that metformin alters the gut microbiota by several mechanisms, one of which is the regulation of the expression of genes encoding metalloproteins in the gut bacteria (29). Several clinical studies have investigated the relationship between metformin treatment and gut bacteria in T2DM patients. Their results revealed a shift in gut bacteria during metformin treatment which could be associated with glucose tolerance (30, 31). This finding may, at least partially, explain some of the anti-inflammatory effects of the medication. Furthermore, it has been reported that metformin can be useful in protecting bones, especially in the first stages of RA, and can decrease inflammation, CVD, and cancer in RA patients (32).

Multiple studies have reported an association between metformin and reduced cancer incidence and mortality. Chae et al reviewed the clinical trials addressing the issue of repurposing metformin for cancer treatment (33). The authors concluded that although metformin has been shown to have a favourable effect on markers of tumour proliferation, there is still no evidence on its effect on survival rates and therefore more evidence is needed to consider repurposing metformin as an immunotherapy agent in the treatment of cancer. Heckman-Stoddard et al also reviewed the issue of repurposing metformin for the prevention of cancer and cancer recurrence (34). In their review, the authors described two potential routes of action that contribute to its anti-neoplastic activity: an indirect route related to its insulin-lowering activity, which may slow tumour proliferation in individuals with hyperinsulinaemia; and direct action in target tissues against respiratory complex I of the electron transport chain in mitochondria of pre-neoplastic and neoplastic cells, reducing energy consumption in the cell (35). They also stated that both routes of action involve the activation of AMPK by metformin, which inhibits the mTOR pathway, reducing cell proliferation, and inducing apoptosis and cell-cycle arrest (36, 37). Despite the biological plausibility of a cancer-preventive effect of metformin, repurposing the drug for the treatment and prevention of cancer requires additional studies to determine which patients are most likely to benefit from metformin treatment.

We observed a non-linear association between statin adherence and the risk of RA, with patients in the medium adherence category (PDC 20–79%) having the lowest risk, with little difference from the third tertile. These findings differ from the results of our previous study, where risk of RA decreased as statin adherence increased (23).

The major indication for metformin in our cohort was T2DM. The relationship between T2DM and RA is not clear. While some studies suggested that diabetes increases the risk of RA (38), others demonstrated that the prevalence of RA is lower and occurs at an older age in patients with pre-existing T2DM in comparison to people without T2DM (39). These studies did not take metformin use into account. However, in limiting our analysis to non-T2DM patients, the results were essentially similar to the primary model. Therefore, differences in the prevalence of T2DM or T2DM severity cannot explain the observed negative association between metformin use and the lower likelihood of RA development.

An interesting finding in our study is the association between obesity (body mass index > 30 kg/m2) and the reduced risk of developing RA. Obesity is generally considered as a risk factor for pro-inflammatory disorders, yet previous studies which examined the association between obesity and the development of RA produced inconsistent results (40, 41). The association found in the current study may be attributed to the fact that the majority of our cohort was diabetic, and better glucose control in these patients may contribute to the decreased risk of developing RA (15). Glycaemic control can also be achieved by the use of other anti-diabetic medications. Two population-based cohort studies compared dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitor combination therapy (with metformin) with non-DPP-4 inhibitor combination therapy with regard to the development of auto-immune diseases, including RA. Their results revealed a slightly lower risk of RA in patients who were treated with DPP-4 inhibitor combination therapy, but since these patients were also treated with metformin, there is very little evidence supporting an RA-lowering risk effect, by glycaemic control or anti-inflammatory properties for other anti-diabetic medications (42, 43).

Persistence with metformin treatment was associated with a lower risk of developing RA in women, but not in men. The relatively low number of incident cases in men, in addition to the fact that RA is more prevalent and incident in women, may explain our results.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between metformin treatment and the incidence of RA. The strengths of this historical cohort study include the relatively long follow-up period, large and unselected study population, systematic collection of data, and availability personal-level data on clinical and sociodemographic factors, as well as on healthcare utilization before the index date. However, some limitations should be discussed. We had no data on clinical assessments of RA and its severity, raising the possibility of misdiagnosis. However, a total of 61% of the incident RA cases in our study purchased DMARDs, which is comparable to 63% reported among Medicare patients (44), and even higher than the proportion reported in previous real-world studies (4–37%, depending on the age group) (45). Missing data on pack-years of smoking, which has been repeatedly associated with increased risk of RA (46), can explain the increased, yet statistically non-significant association that we found. Data on ACPA positivity, which has been known to precede the development of RA (47), were also unavailable for a significant proportion of patients who were diagnosed with RA before the introduction of commercial ACPA testing kits. Surveillance bias should also be considered. Patients with higher adherence visited their PCP more frequently and, therefore, these patients were more likely to be diagnosed with RA. However, adjusting to the number of visits to PCPs did not change risk estimates, suggesting a low risk of bias. Finally, the majority of patients included in the study had T2DM, which may limit the generalization of our results to other populations.

## Conclusion

The results of our study suggest that adherence to metformin treatment is associated with a reduced risk of developing RA, mainly in women with T2DM. Further studies are needed to assess the effect of metformin on RA development in other patient populations.
