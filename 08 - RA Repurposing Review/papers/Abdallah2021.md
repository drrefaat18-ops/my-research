# The AMPK modulator metformin as adjunct to methotrexate in patients with rheumatoid arthritis: A proof-of-concept, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial

**Authors:** Abdallah Mahmoud S., Alarfaj Sumaiah J., Saif Dalia S., EL-Naggar Mostafa E., Elsokary Mohamed A., Elsawah Hozaifa K., Zaki Shimaa Abdelsattar, Wahsh Engy A., Abo Mansour Hend E., Mosalam Esraa M.  
**Journal:** International Immunopharmacology (2021)  
**DOI:** 10.1016/j.intimp.2021.107575  
**PDF:** pdf/Abdallah2021.pdf

## Abstract

**Background:** Metformin (MET) may exert anti-rheumatic effects and reduce cartilage degradation through its immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory actions.

**Methods:** This was a double-blind placebo-controlled study, 120 adult patients with active rheumatoid arthritis (RA) were randomized to receive MET (1000 mg) or placebo daily with methotrexate (MTX, 7.5 mg/week) for 12 weeks. American College of Rheumatology (ACR)20, ACR50, and ACR70 response rates, Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS-28), and drug safety were the efficacy endpoints. Serum levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17A, NF-κB, TGG-β1, MDA together with gene expression of AMPK and IGF-IR were assessed before and after the therapy.

**Results:** A total of 80.8% of the patients in the MET group, compared with 54.7% in placebo group, met the criteria of ACR20 response after 12 weeks (P = 0.001). Statistically significant enhancements in the DAS28-3 (CRP) were observed after 4 and 8 weeks for the MET group compared with placebo and were sustained after 12 weeks. MET group showed statistically significant increase in percentage of patients achieving DAS remission after 12 weeks (P = 0.015). Significant improvements in ACR50, ACR70, Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index (HAQ-DI), and DAS28-3 (CRP) were also reported. MET was well-tolerated, and no serious adverse effects were reported in both groups. Furthermore, the MET group was superior in improving the measured parameters compared to the placebo.

**Conclusions:** MET improved the anti-rheumatic effect of MTX; suggesting it to be a beneficial adjuvant in patients with RA. Trial registration ID: NCT04068246.

**Keywords:** Rheumatoid arthritis; Metformin; Methotrexate; Inflammatory markers; AMPK; Adjunctive therapy

## 1. Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic auto-inflammatory disorder that highly contributes to global morbidity and mortality [1]. The complex cross talk between environment, genetics, and immunological response plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of RA [2]. Current treatment strategies comprise conventional disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs), and if it is necessary to achieve low disease activity or remission, the biological DMARDs could be used afterward [3]. Therapeutics that are used in RA had certain limitations represented in the terms of tolerability, access, and response duration and its magnitude. Consequently, finding out safe adjunctive treatment option for RA is an urgent demand to boost the therapeutic response [4].

T helper 17 (Th17) cells were implicated in the pathophysiology of RA [5,6]. Interleukin (IL)-17, mostly secreted by Th17, has synergistic inflammatory activities with tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukins-1β (IL-1β), and IL-6 [7]. Conversely, regulatory T (Treg) cells have been found to possess anti-inflammatory property. The balance between Th17 and Treg cells plays a crucial role in the development and the outcome of RA [8].

Other evidences implicated the impaired immune-metabolism in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases including RA [9,10]. Adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a major regulator for the metabolic pathways in different types of the cells [11]. AMPK can prohibit the inflammatory response through blocking various transcription factors such as nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) [12,13]. Both NF-κB and STAT1 modulate the inflammatory response through influence on the immune cells and their released cytokines like IL-10 and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β1) [14]. Methotrexate (MTX) is a fixed component of the conventional DMARDs that modify the progression of RA through various mechanisms that involve AMPK modulation [15,16]. Intracellular accumulation of NF-κB is stimulated by the expression of insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF-IR) by the peripheral blood leukocytes in RA patients [17].

Metformin (MET) is a traditional antidiabetic drug that exerts glucose-lowering effect by activating AMPK since it is a critical enzyme in lipid and glucose metabolism [8,18]. Besides the antidiabetic effect, MET can inhibit lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammation by blocking the production of NF-κB [19]. Furthermore, MET-activated AMPK may inhibit mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), which regulates the differentiation of T cells [20–22]. In particular, AMPK inhibits Th17 cells through suppressing the phosphorylation of mTOR and its downstream signal transducers [23]. MET has been shown to effectively alleviate cartilage degradation and aging through its immune-modulatory and anti-inflammatory actions in preclinical studies [24,25]. These modulatory properties of MET make it a promising candidate as an adjuvant for RA.

In this context, we hypothesized that MET could reduce inflammatory factors in RA patients and modify disease progression. In the present study, we proposed to assess the adjunctive role of MET in RA patients who are taking MTX and do not complain other problems. Moreover, we assessed the relationship between DAS28 and the gene expression of AMPK and IGF-IR as well as several peripheral biomarkers and their diagnostic and therapeutic role in RA.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1. Study design

This was a prospective, randomized, double-blinded, placebo-controlled study, which was conducted in Menoufia University Hospital, Egypt (August 2019 to December 2020).

### 2.2. Participants

Eligible patients were one hundred and twenty who aged 24–58 years fulfilling 2010 American College of Rheumatology - European League against Rheumatism (ACR-EULAR) classification criteria for RA [26] and had active inflammatory RA with no limit for the disease duration. The active state of the disease was defined by finding Four or more tender/painful joints with four or more swollen joints together with higher than 28 mm/h for erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR, Westergren method) or higher than 7 mg/L for C-reactive protein (CRP). Patients who were receiving MTX, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, acetaminophen, selective COX-2 inhibitors, and low dose of oral corticosteroids were permitted to enroll the trial. Patients who were taking intra-articular hyaluronate sodium; intravenous, intramuscular, or intra-articular corticosteroids were not permitted to participate until 4 weeks after stopping these medications.

Exclusion criteria included refusing to give informed consent, congestive heart failure, diabetes, previous sensitivity to MET, oral prednisolone>10 mg/day, biological DMARDs, severe anemia, active infection, pregnancy or lactation, and clinically significant renal or hepatic diseases.

The participated patients and their authorized representatives rendered informed consent in an agreement with the measures stated by the local ethical committee and they were notified that they can disengage from the trial at any timepoint. The study design was confirmed by the ethical committee of Faculty of Medicine, Menoufia University, Egypt. The study was conducted in compliance with the ethical standards laid down in the 1964 declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. The screened patients were examined for the presence of metabolic syndrome, diabetes, liver or heart diseases in a comprehensive clinical, electrocardiographic, and laboratory examination.

### 2.3. Demographic data

The medical history of the patients was recorded to confirm the absence of any interfering/interacting diseases or drugs. Demographic data included age, gender, weight, height, body mass index (BMI), smoking, duration of the disease, the used medications, and familial history of RA.

### 2.4. Sample size

Calculation of sample size was based on assuming ACR20 response rate of 50% in the MET group and 25% in the placebo group after 12 weeks [27]. Using 80% power and two-sided significance of 5%, the sample size of 60 subjects was estimated, assuming a 10% attrition rate.

### 2.5. Randomization and blinding

To comply with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines, we used an electronic random number calculator to shuffle the participants based on an 1:1 ratio to take either MET or the placebo beside their standard medications. Allocation blinding was attained using opaque, numbered, and stapled envelopes. Randomization, allocation, and consultations were done by different persons. The patients, the doctor who referred them, the person who administered the drugs, and the statistician were blinded. The responsible rheumatologist was unblinded if the patient’s trial drug showed an effect that needs immediate emergency. Once the blinding had been cracked, the patient was managed as off-trial.

### 2.6. Intervention

Sixty patients in the placebo group received MTX 7.5 mg IM once weekly plus one placebo tablet daily, whereas the other 60 patients in the MET group received MTX 7.5 mg IM once weekly plus XR MET 1000 mg tablet once daily with food for 12 weeks. Participants were off trial if they missed seven successive days of the trial medications. Placebo tablets were formed by Sigma Pharmaceutical Company, Menoufia, Egypt. The trial medications were dispensed by the trial pharmacist and the unused tablets were audited.

### 2.7. Outcomes

The primary outcome was ACR 20% improvement criteria (ACR20) response rate [28] based on the count of tender/swollen joints, assessment of pain, patient and physician global assessment of arthritis, Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index (HAQ DI), and the level of CRP after 12 weeks. The secondary outcomes were analyzed after 4, 8, and 12 weeks and involved ACR20, ACR50, and ACR70 response rates. DAS assessed on CRP level and 28-joint subsets (DAS28-3 [CRP]) and ESR (DAS28-4 [ESR]). After 12 weeks, the Short Form 36 (SF-36) health survey involved physical component score [PCS] and the mental component score [MCS] was done. Comparing the efficacy after 12 weeks with the placebo stratified by baseline DAS score (DAS ≤5.1 = low to moderate disease activity, DAS > 5.1 = high disease activity) at the baseline.

The patients were phoned each week to assess compliance to the medications, adverse effects (AEs), and signs of any infection. The remained tablets in each medication set given to the participants were counted to assess their compliance to the treatment. All AEs and their severity (mild, moderate, or severe) were reported over the study. Physical and clinical examination as well as electrocardiograms were performed at planned appointments and the investigator was committed to follow up the patients until AEs were resolved.

Other outcomes were determination of the serum level of each of TNF–α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17A, NF-κB, TGF-β1, malondialdehyde (MDA) together with the assessment of the gene expression level of AMPK and IGF-IR at the beginning and after the intervention to evaluate the biological effect of the trial medications.

### 2.8. Samples collection and measurements

Blood samples were withdrawn from everyone usually in the morning. All samples were divided into two portions; whole blood portion for quantitative real time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and another to separate serum for ELISA measurements. Whole blood samples were collected in heparinized vacutainers, whereas the other aliquots were collected in plain vacutainers to obtain serum samples. The plain tubes were then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min. The separated serums were conveyed to eppendorf tubes and kept at −80 °C freezer until time of analysis.

The serum levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17A, NF-κB, TGF-β1, MDA, and cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies (Anti-CCP) were determined with specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, which were purchased from R&D Systems® (USA). All measurements were done according to the manufacturer’s instructions using a Biotek ELx800 UV–Vis microplate reader (USA). On the other hand, AMPK and IGF-IR gene expression level was determined by qRT-PCR.

### 2.9. qRT-PCR for AMPK and IGF-IR gene expression

RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and amplification SYBR Green PCR kits were purchased from (QIAGEN, Germany). RNA samples were automatically extracted by QIAcube (QIAGEN, Germany) in line with the manufacturer’s specifications in RNeasy Mini kit. The extracted RNA samples were used as templates for formation of cDNA, which were created using QuantiTect® reverse transcription by GeneAmp® PCR System 9700 thermal cycler. Then, cDNA samples were used for amplification of AMPK and IGF-1R using QuantiTect® SYBR® Green PCR by PikoReal™ RT- PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific®, Finland).

Sequence of AMPK and IGF-IR genes was obtained from www.rgd.mcw.edu. Sequences for the used primers were designed by Primer 3 Plus software (Table 1). Glyceraldehyde- 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as a reference gene. Primers were bought from Biosearch Technologies (USA). The amplification process was initiated by activation of DNA polymerase at 95 °C for 15 min. Then, forty cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 15 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and finally extension at 72 °C for 30 s were done. The results were expressed as relative copy number (RCN), which was calculated using formula: 2^−ΔCt, where ΔCt = Ct (target gene) – Ct (reference gene) [29].

### 2.10. Statistical analysis

We reported continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and the categorical variables were reported as number (percentage) unless stated otherwise. All tests of the treatment efficacy were conducted at 0.05 two-sided significance level, and Bonferroni adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. Type III sums of squares were used to adjust unbalanced data in the interactions of these models of variance. Mixed-effects model repeated measures (MMRM) analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed for the primary analysis of any change from baseline to endpoint in ACR20. Furthermore, two-way-repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) for ACR20 (time-treatment interaction) was done. The two groups as a between-subjects factor (group) and the four times interval measurements during treatment as the within-subjects factor (time) were considered. Moreover, ANCOVA, controlling for the baseline score, was used to compare the change in biological markers at 12th week in the two groups. Fisher’s exact test was used for the qualitative variables. Pearson’s correlation was calculated to assess the relationship among variables. The statistical analysis was performed using IBM® SPSS® Statistics version 22 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All graphs were created with GraphPad Prism 6.01 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla CA, USA).

### 3.1. Efficacy

After 3 months, a total of 80.8% of the patients in the MET group, compared with 54.7% in the placebo group, met the criteria for an ACR20 response (X2 = 9.956, df = 1, p = 0.001; number need to treat (NNT) = 3.75) (Fig. 2A). Supporting MMRM ANCOVA results, two-factor ANOVA exposed that the difference between the two treatments was statistically significant as indicated by the effect of group; the between-subjects factor (F (1, 106) = 77.89, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.687). The behavior of the two treatment approaches was not similar across the time (groups-by-time interaction, F (3, 432) = 112.78, p = 0.002, η2 = 0.439).

The corresponding percentage of the patients who met the criteria for an ACR50 response after 12 weeks was 32.1% in the MET group versus 13.5% in the placebo group (X2 = 7.956, df = 1, p = 0.0047), whereas the percentage of the patients who met the criteria for an ACR70 response was 16.4% in the MET group versus 6.8% in the placebo group (X2 = 8.356, df = 1, p = 0.0038) (Fig. 2B and C).

After 12 weeks, the least squares mean changes from the baseline in HAQ DI scores were −0.49 points in the MET group compared with −0.38 points with placebo ((X2 = 9.356, df = 1, p = 0.0022). There was significant enhancement from the baseline in the count of the swollen and tender joints, assessment of pain, physician and patient global assessments, HAQ-DI score, and CRP level in comparison with the placebo were detected for MET treatment group after 12 weeks. The rate of clinical improvement in HAQ-DI response (0.22) was significantly greater for the MET group (79.2%; (X2 = 6.21, df = 1, p = 0.012) compared with the placebo (53.9%) after 12 weeks.

Statistically significant improvements in the DAS28-3 (CRP) were noticed after 4 and 8 weeks for the MET compared with the placebo; these improvements were continued even after 12 weeks. Furthermore, statistically significant improvements in the DAS28-4 (ESR) were observed after 4 and 8 weeks for the MET compared with the placebo and were sustained after 12 weeks (Fig. 2D and E).

In patients with high activity of the disease at baseline (DAS > 5.1), the MET group showed an increase in the percentage of patients achieving DAS remission after 12 weeks (50.7%) in comparison with 21.5% for the placebo (X2 = 8.17, df = 1, p = 0.004). In addition, MET group showed an increase in the percentage of patients achieving low disease activity score (LDAS) after 12 weeks (78.7%) in comparison with 47.5% for the placebo (X2 = 7.312, df = 1, p = 0.0068). In patients with low to moderate disease activity at the baseline (DAS ≤5.1), the MET group showed an increase in percentage of patients achieving DAS remission after 12 weeks (80.0%) in comparison with 50% in the placebo (X2 = 5.89, df = 1, p = 0.015). Furthermore, the MET group showed an increase in the percentage of patients achieving LDAS after 12 weeks (53.7%) compared to the placebo group (85.7%) (X2 = 5.67, df = 1, p = 0.017).

Enhancements from the baseline to the end of the trial in the secondary outcomes, including ACR component scores are presented in Table 3. The rate of clinical improvement in SF-36 (an increase of ≥2.5 from baseline) revealed statistically significant improvements (X2 = 9.67, df = 1, p = 0.00187) in the PCS for the MET group (75.0%) in comparison with the placebo group (25.0%) after 12 weeks. Regarding the MCS, statistically significant improvement (X2 = 4.98, df = 1, p = 0.025) was shown in the MET group (57.7%) in comparison with the placebo group (29.2%).

### 3.2. Effect on biological markers

The serum levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17A, NF- κB, TGG-β1, MDA, Anti-CCP, and the expression of AMPK and IGF-IR were statistically non-significant between the placebo and MET groups at the baseline (p > 0.05). MET group showed a statistically significant reduction in the serum levels of TNF–α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, NF- κB, TGG-β1, MDA, Anti-CCP, and IGF-IR expression in comparison with the placebo group after 12 weeks of the treatment as indicated by the effect of group and between-subjects factor ((F (1, 105) = 112.3, p = 0.01, η2 = 0.516), (F (1, 105) = 102.3, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.493), (F (1, 105) = 114.7, p = 0.002, η2 = 0.522) , (F (1, 105) = 212.6, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.669), (F (1, 105) = 294.8, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.737), (F (1, 105) = 125.96, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.545), (F (1, 105) = 115.89, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.425), (F (1, 105) = 389.3, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.791), and (F (1, 105) = 142.78, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.57), respectively). In contrast, the MET group exhibited a statistically significant increase in the serum level of IL-10 and significant up-regulation of AMPK compared to the placebo group ((F (1, 105) = 132.3, p = 0.002, η2 = 0.557) and (F (1, 105) = 152.3, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.592), respectively) after 12 weeks.

It is noteworthy that the serum levels of TNF–α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, NF-κB, TGG-β1, MDA, Anti-CCP, IGF-IR expression showed a statistically significant decrease after 12 weeks of the treatment relative to their baseline values in both groups as indicated by the effect of groups-by-time interaction ((F (1, 105) = 52.4, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.333), (F (1, 105) = 58.4, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.357), (F (1, 105) = 65.6, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.385), (F (1, 105) = 69.8, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.399), (F (1, 105) = 77.8, p = 0.000, η2 = 0.426), (F (1, 105) = 56.9, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.351), (F (1, 105) = 155.89, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.598), (F (1, 105) = 88.89, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.458), and (F (1, 105) = 93.7, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.471), respectively). In contrary, there was a statistically significant increase in IL-10 serum level and significant up-regulation of AMPK after 12 weeks of the treatment in the two groups compared to their baseline levels ((F (1, 105) = 88.78, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.458), and (F (1, 105) = 73.89, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.413), respectively) as shown in Table 4.

It was found that the serum levels of TNF–α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, NF- κB, TGG-β1, MDA, and IGF-IR expression have statistically significant positive correlation with DAS28-3 (CRP) before the treatment (r = 0.60, p = 0.025; r = 0.552, p = 0.009; r = 0.645, p = 0.031; r = 0.731, p = 0.023; r = 0.545, p = 0.001; r = 0.645, p = 0.001; r = 0.652, p = 0.002, and r = 0.552, p = 0.008, respectively), and also after the treatment (r = 0.674, p = 0.000; r = 0.619, p = 0.000; r = 0.686, p = 0.000; r = 0.701, p = 0.002; r = 0.676, p = 0.004; r = 0.576, p = 0.008; r = 0.682, p = 0.009, and r = 0.582, p = 0.007, respectively).

### 3.3. Clinical adverse effects

The incidence of AEs between the MET and the placebo groups was statistically non-significant. Therefore, dropout owing to lack of efficacy or development of AEs was limited. The mostly developed AEs in both groups were nausea (8.33% placebo, 10% MET), vomiting (1.67% placebo, 3.33% MET), stomatitis (10% placebo, 11.67% MET), abdominal pain (5% placebo, 6.67% MET), heartburn (6.67% placebo, 8.33% MET), bloating (10% placebo, 11.67% MET), constipation (5% placebo, 6.67% MET), diarrhea (6.67% placebo, 5% MET), decreased appetite (10% placebo, 6.67% MET), fatigue (6.67% placebo, 8.83% MET), dry mouth (5% placebo, 6.67% MET), insomnia (10% placebo, 11.67% MET), headache (13.33% placebo, 11.67% MET), dizziness (6.67% placebo, 8.33% MET), reddened eyes (6.67% placebo, 8.33% MET), hair loss (6.67% placebo, 5% MET), decreased white blood cells (WBCs) count (8.33% placebo, 10% MET), pharyngitis (8.33% placebo, 10% MET), rash (6.67% placebo, 5% MET), increased ALT (15% placebo, 6.67% MET), increased AST (15% placebo, 6.67% MET), increased blood cholesterol (13.33% placebo, 11.67% MET), increased blood triglycerides (8.33% placebo, 11.67% MET), and increased LDL (13.33% placebo, 11.67% MET). The other developed AEs were temporary and resolved spontaneously. Table 5 showed that the rate of AEs was statistically non-significant between the trial groups.

## 4. Discussion

This study is the first adequately powered randomized, double-blind, and placebo-controlled trial, which assessed the adjunctive role of MET as AMPK modulator in the management of patients with RA.

Despite the extensive use of conventional and biological DMARDs has amended the quality of life of patients with RA, the rates of side effects and other comorbidity remain high [30]. A recent study has revealed a statistically significant increase in the risk of diabetes among patients with RA [31]. Conversely, another retrospective cohort study has been reported that adherence to MET is linked with a reduced risk of evolving RA in female patients [32]. Thus, using MET in combination at the start of the treatment strategy may afford additional therapeutic benefits in RA patients [33].

Although the direct comparison is relatively difficult among the different study designs, the average decline in the DAS28, which could be ascribed to the addition of MET, was −2.4. This was found to be superior to the decrease of the same score by −0.59 and −1.66 using atorvastatin and resveratrol; respectively, in combination to established DMARDs therapy [34,35].

Overall, mean baseline DAS28 score were around 5.1 and hence relatively small reductions in DAS28 were essential to achieve LDAS and remission. In addition, the relatively high percent of the patients with lower and moderate disease severity, based on DAS28 score in our trial relative to the previous studies [34,35], might explain the higher remission and response rates in our study. However, the clinically observed anti-inflammatory effect of MET and the significant decrease in DAS28 provides evidence that pathways that are targeted by MET may act as a promising opportunity in inflammatory diseases to improve the therapeutic response.

This clinical enhancement in the MET group could be attributed to its anti-inflammatory effect and AMPK activation, which led to the fundamental reduction in the serum levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, NF-κB, TGG-β1, MDA, anti-CCP, and the down-regulation of IGF-IR together with a significant increase in the serum level of IL-10 and AMPK up-regulation relative to their baseline values and to the placebo [8,36]. Our finding is consistent with other studies, which concluded that MET decreases inflammation through activation of AMPK pathway. AMPK probably prevents the inflammatory responses through blocking the action of various transcription factors including NF-κB and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ co-activator 1α (PGC-1α), which in turn can suppress the expression of NF-κB-induced inflammatory factors as IL-6 [12,13,36]. Suppression of NF-κB by AMPK ultimately contributes to the activation of the anti-inflammatory cytokine; IL-10 [14].

In the same context, the AMPK had inhibitory effect on TGF-β1 [37], which is a pleiotropic cytokine with paradoxical role in inflammation; it can act as pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory and it was elevated in our study at the baseline but decreased after completion of the treatment [38]. Furthermore, MET decreases the expression of IL-1β and IL-6 irrespective of the diabetes status [39,40]. MET can also decrease TNF-α -dependent gene expression of pro-inflammatory and cell adhesion biomolecules to suppress endothelial cell inflammation [41,42]. It was also reported that MET decreases IL-17A through balancing the pool of Th17 and Treg cells via activating AMPK and inhibiting mTOR [24].

Improvement of CRP by MET can be explained by its anti-inflammatory role, which reflected by the decreased levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. As IL-6, IL-1β; since theses cytokines induce the synthesis of CRP in the hepatocytes via NF-κB and STAT3 pathways [43]. IGF-IR signaling is implicated in severe RA by activating T cell-dependent inflammation in arthritis. Therefore, blocking IGF-IR on the level of insulin receptor substrates mitigates arthritis by restricting IL-6-induced synthesis of Th17 cells [44]. It was reported that MET down-regulates insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) and inhibits its action on IGF-IR in PC-3 prostatic cancer cell line [17,45]. Our results were in agreement with previous studies indicating that MET treatment suppresses inflammation and improves disease activity in experimental and autoimmune arthritis [8,17,46,47].

Regarding the placebo group, MTX has anti-proliferative and anti-inflammatory properties, which mediated by reducing the pro-inflammatory cytokines as well as its impact on AMPK expression [15,16]. It was shown that MTX treatment resulted in a decreased T cell population that secretes TNF-α, whereas the T cells-producing IL-10 were increased after the polyclonal activation [48]. These properties of MTX could disclose the significant decrease in the serum levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, NF-κB, TGG-β1, MDA, Anti-CCP, and IGF-IR down-regulation alongside with the significant increase in the serum level of IL-10 and up-regulation of AMPK relative to their baseline values.

The enhanced anti-rheumatic effect in the combination therapy group could be ascribed to the addition of MET. Our results propose that adjunctive treatment with MET could enhance the effect of MTX in rheumatic patients. They support the outcomes of earlier studies suggesting that agents with anti-inflammatory activities may produce anti-rheumatic effect [33,35]. This outcome have been particularly strengthened by studies suggesting that MET attenuated the rate of admission in diabetic patients with RA [33].

It is worth mentioning that there were no reported pharmacokinetic interactions between MET and MTX. In addition, it was reported that no association was identified between the low-dose pharmacokinetics of MTX and the clinical response, suggesting that the assessment of serum MTX concentrations will not be helpful in the therapeutic management of patients undergoing MTX therapy with RA [49]. Moreover, it has been reported that MET could exert hepatoprotection against MTX-induced hepatotoxicity [50,51]. Also, there were no clinically significant AEs due to the short treatment period, and small dose of MET. The serum level of vitamin B12 was determined because MET treatment might cause vitamin B12 deficiency [52] and it was within normal values for both groups before and after the intervention.

Recognizing the biomarkers that are implicated in RA pathophysiology is a basic priority for rheumatologists to determine the appropriate treatment approach [53]. Consequently, the serum level of each of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, NF- κB, TGG-β1, MDA, anti-CCP, IL-10, and the expression level of AMPK and IGF-IR were assessed and correlated with the DAS28 score to evaluate the biological effect of the trial medications. The serum levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, NF- κB, TGG-β1, MDA, Anti-CCP, and IGF-IR expression have been reported to be raised in patients with RA [54–56]. Likewise, those biomarkers were high at the baseline and decreased after the treatment. In contrary, the serum level of IL-10 and the expression of AMPK are lower in RA patients [57,58]. Therefore, we found that the serum level of IL-10 and expression of AMPK were low at the baseline and increased after the intervention.

Nevertheless, our trial had some limitations; despite the promising findings concerning the use of MET in the management of RA, it is early to consider MET as a primary treatment. The quite small sample size and the fact that all participants were from the same geographic area, making generalization of our outcomes needs further investigations. In addition, we did not perform radiographic investigations to support the positive outcomes of the clinical and biochemical markers of the disease used in our study. This was due to the short term of the study period, which may not reveal significant radiographic changes. Therefore, the role of MET in the treatment of RA is better described as an adjuvant until more is explored about its efficacy through more comprehensive and larger scale, multicenter clinical studies. Therefore, we recommend study replication with further investigations for longer duration.

## 5. Conclusion

Co-administration of MET as, a modulator of AMPK, with MTX to RA patients enhanced the anti-rheumatic effect through its anti-inflammatory and immune-modulatory properties, which reflected clinically by decreased disease activity. Theses outcomes suggest MET to be promising candidate as an adjuvant to conventional DMARDs but further investigations with larger sample size and longer follow-up period are recommended to overcome the limitations of this study.
