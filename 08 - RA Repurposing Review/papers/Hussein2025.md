# Repurposing losartan potassium against rheumatoid arthritis via transdermally-delivered leciplexes: Accentuated efficacy through modulation of angiotensin II/AT1R/AT2R axis

**Authors:** Hussein Amira A., Aldosari Basmah N., Zaki Randa M., Afzal Obaid, Ali Adel A., Aboud Heba M., Ahmed Yasmin M., Naguib Demiana M.  
**Journal:** International Journal of Pharmaceutics: X (2025)  
**DOI:** 10.1016/j.ijpx.2025.100354  
**PDF:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12276446/


## Abstract

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune inflammatory ailment which preferentially impacts the synovial membranes of joints and ultimately triggers cartilage and bone erosion. Angiotensin II (Ang II) participates in the pathogenesis of RA; hence, Ang II receptor blockade, accomplished through a specific inhibitor such as losartan potassium (LST), may confer an effective therapeutic avenue for RA. This study aimed to develop, optimize, and characterize LST-loaded leciplexes (LST-LPXs) to ameliorate its bioavailability and prolong its therapeutic efficacy for combating RA. To accomplish this objective, LST-LPX dispersions were assembled through a single-step process and optimized via D-optimal design for various physicochemical traits employing Design-Expert® software. Also, pharmacokinetic studies were explored in rats. Additionally, in complete Freund's adjuvant-induced RA in Wistar rats, RF, COMP, NADPH oxidase, NO, IL-6, TNF-α, besides Ang II and its receptors (AT1R & AT2R) were measured. The optimum LST-LPXs formulation elicited acceptable entrapment efficiency (88.05%), nano-scaled spherical morphology (246.71 nm), controlled release over 24 h (86.33%), and adequate permeation properties through the skin (417.83 μg/cm2). The pharmacokinetic analysis disclosed a snowballed bioavailability of the optimized LST-LPXs gel by 3.08- and 1.2-fold versus the oral solution and crude gel, respectively. The optimum LST-LPXs gel divulged accentuated anti-arthritic effects, evidenced by significant suppression of rheumatoid, oxidative stress, and inflammatory biomarkers coupled with corrections of AT1R and AT2R protein expression. Practically, the current findings proposed a compelling proof-of-principle that the transdermal LST-LPXs could serve as a non-invasive promising nanoparadigm for RA tackling.


## Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is one of the most ubiquitous and devastating autoimmune inflammatory chronic diseases, not only affecting the synovial lining of joints but also the extraarticular tissues and showing signs of symmetrical destructive polyarthritis. It is marked by persistent synovial inflammation leading to tissue destruction and bone erosion, representing a substantial cause of physical debilitation and loss of productivity, and impacting around 0.2–1% of the world's population (Gao et al., 2024; Mitrović et al., 2023). Some forms of arthritis can also affect the body's internal organs, particularly the spleen. Despite the efficacy of therapeutic antirheumatic medications in controlling inflammation and reducing bone damage, the total remission rates of RA remain inadequate. The management of RA encompasses several therapeutic selections, such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), glucocorticoids, disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMADs), and biological therapies. Although current medications have proven their efficacy in treating RA, they do not significantly modulate the fundamental mechanisms behind its development. Furthermore, such medications experience various hurdles after brief treatment intervals and are frequently associated with insufficient results, such as low selectivity for arthritic joints, short half-lives, and inconsistent pharmacokinetics (Ren et al., 2024b). Consequently, innovative therapeutic approaches that ensure safety during prolonged RA therapy are warranted.

Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), or “Sartans” have been promoted as suitable contenders for treating RA based on their anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory properties. This is supported by comprehensive in vitro data and substantial experimental and clinical trials confirming the pleiotropic impacts of ARBs in RA (Price et al., 2007; Silveira et al., 2013). Losartan potassium (LST) is an angiotensin II (Ang II) receptor blocker where there are two types of Ang II receptors; AT1R and AT2R commonly located in the heart, blood vessels, lung, adrenal cortex and kidney. LST has antihypertensive effects primarily through the selective inhibition of AT1R, resulting in a diminished suppressor response to Ang II. It is often used in the treatment of hypertension and heart failure, especially in patients who experience cough, as an adverse effect of angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors. Additionally, it is utilized to treat diabetic nephropathy, lower the risk of stroke in patients with left ventricular hypertrophy, and has been tested for myocardial infarction (Al-Majed et al., 2015). There is a growing evidence that Ang II is a major mediator of inflammation by stimulating the AT1R and AT2R (Wang et al., 2019) which elicits both pro-inflammatory and pro-stress actions via AT1R, as well as tissue proliferation, inflammation, and fibrosis via AT2R (Chang and Wei, 2015). These abnormal physiological events trigger cartilage destruction, bone erosion, and, finally, loss of joint function that may be ameliorated through AT1R blocker LST administration (Bansal et al., 2025; Cardoso et al., 2018; Sherwani et al., 2024). Recently, it has been reported that LST confers anti-inflammatory activities during arthritis, osteoporosis, and periodontitis via pathways that potentially involve modulation outside of AT1R and AT2R signaling (Li et al., 2024).

Unfortunately, LST elicits a low aberrant oral bioavailability (33%) and brief plasma half-life of 1–3 h due to its pervasive metabolism by CYP2C9 and 3A4 (Al-Majed et al., 2015). Also, diverse adverse events like insomnia, dizziness, diarrhea, nasal congestion, muscle cramps, and impotence have been encountered with oral LST administration (Kommana et al., 2020). Considering LST administration for prolonged treatment, it is essential to refine its bioavailability while maintaining a sustained release profile to abridge both dosage and frequency, hence boosting patient compliance. Consequently, administration through a different approach is imperative to address these constraints. Our study is devoted to assessing the transdermal route for LST delivery, a feasible approach that offers favorable and uncomplicated self-administration for patients. It eradicates the necessity for frequent dosing and fluctuations in plasma levels accompanying oral administration and injections, thereby ensuring steady drug concentrations while facilitating the delivery of drugs with a short half-life. The evasion of hepatic and gastrointestinal tract metabolic pathways for drugs with scant bioavailability is an additional benefit of transdermal administration. Nonetheless, the skin presents a formidable barrier to the penetration of substances, permitting only minimal amounts of a drug to infiltrate gradually over time (Leong et al., 2023). Various strategies have been attempted to enhance transdermal delivery of drugs, including the exploitation of vesicular nano-cargos.

Leciplex (LPX) is a contemporary vesicular nano-drug delivery technology that integrates cationic surfactants (SAAs) with negatively charged phospholipids (PL). LPXs are synthesized by an instantaneous one-step procedure and possess a positive surface charge. This charge promotes their close interaction with the negatively charged exterior of stratum corneum (SC), hence facilitating drug permeability into the deepest layers of the skin (Salama et al., 2019). According to earlier studies, LPX formulations have boosted the bioavailability of numerous drugs, such as enhancing carvedilol ocular bioavailability/transcorneal permeability (Hassan et al., 2018), and upgrading oral quercetin anti-inflammatory/anticancer efficacy relative to its crude form (Date et al., 2011a).

The present work aimed to transdermally deliver LST using cationic SAA-based nanoparadigm, LPXs, incorporated into gel to accentuate its systemic bioavailability for RA management. Subsequent to LPXs fabrication, a physicochemical examination was conducted to assess their suitability for transdermal delivery. Furthermore, pharmacokinetic studies in rats were executed to compare the pharmacokinetic parameters of LST following oral and transdermal administrations. Finally, an in vivo study was conducted to assess the crafted formulation in a rat model of complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA)-induced RA.

### Fabrication of LST-LPXs

The formulation of LPXs incorporating LST was executed utilizing a one-step manufacturing protocol, as formerly delineated (Eid et al., 2024; Hassan et al., 2018). The process entailed the combination of PL and cationic SAAs, specifically DDAB or CTAB, at various molar ratios as depicted in Table 1. The weighed quantity of PL was dissolved in Transcutol® P (0.5 mL) by heating to 70 °C in a temperature-regulated water bath. Concurrently, an aqueous solution comprising the predetermined levels of LST and cationic SAA in distilled water (9.5 mL, 70 °C) was rapidly blended with the PL/Transcutol® P mixture under continuous magnetic stirring at 1200 rpm until a consistent white dispersion of LST-LPXs was obtained. The final formulations were then chilled overnight at 4 °C to ensure stability prior to subsequent characterization and analysis.


### In vitro characterization of LST-LPXs


#### Determination of EE%

The EE% of LST was calculated by assessing LST in the supernatant and subtracting this value from the overall quantity incorporated during formulation. The supernatant comprising free LST was isolated using a cooling centrifuge (SIGMA 3-30 K, Steinheim, Germany) at 4 °C (15,338.96 ×g, 2 h) (El Menshawe et al., 2024). The supernatant was subsequently separated utilizing a nylon syringe filter with a pore size of 0.45 μm. Following suitable dilution, the concentration of LST was determined using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV − 1800, Tokyo, Japan) at a wavelength of 227 nm (Al-Majed et al., 2015). A calibration curve was established in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) pH 7.4, spanning a concentration range of 2–25 μg/mL (R2 = 0.9981). The EE% of LST was assessed utilizing the subsequent formula (Salem et al., 2024a):(1)EE%=Total drug concentration−free drug concentrationTotal drug concentration×100


#### Determination of Z-Ave, polydispersity index (PDI), and ζ potential

The Z-Ave, PDI, and surface charge of the LPX formulations were determined using a Zetasizer Nano ZS device (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK) by dynamic light scattering (DLS). To eliminate the multi-scattering phenomenon, 0.1 mL of each dispersion was diluted with 10 mL of deionized water before measurements. All analyses were accomplished in triplicate at 25 °C (Tulbah et al., 2023).


#### In vitro drug release study

The in vitro release profiles of LST-LPX dispersions were evaluated versus free LST solution using a USP I dissolution apparatus (Erweka DT-720, Erweka GmbH, Heusenstamm, Germany) employing the dialysis diffusion approach (Salem et al., 2022). LPXs dispersion from various formulations (equivalent to 3 mg LST) was inserted into cylindrical glass tubes measuring 6 cm in length and 2.5 cm in internal diameter. One side of the cylinder was securely covered with a dialysis membrane, possessing a molecular weight cut off of 12,000 Da, which had been submerged in the receptor milieu overnight. The glass cylinders were secured to the shafts of the USP dissolution tester. The release milieu comprised 100 mL of Sorensen's phosphate buffer pH 5.5 to guarantee stable sink conditions (Kommana et al., 2020). The rotational velocity was adjusted to 100 rpm, while the temperature was consistently maintained at 32 ± 0.5 °C during the release study. Aliquots of 2 mL were collected from the release milieu at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h, and refilled with an equivalent volume of fresh milieu to ascertain constant volume. The LST content was assayed spectrophotometrically at λmax 227 nm following filtration of the specimens using a 0.45 μm membrane filter. The release testing was conducted in triplicate for all formulations, with the results presented as mean values ± SD. The cumulative percentage of LST-LPXs released was graphed over time. The release kinetics for LST-LPXs were analyzed by fitting the obtained data into Higuchi, Hixson–Crowell, Korsmeyer–Peppas, zero-order, and first-order models. The magnitude of the determination coefficients (R2) was used to determine the appropriate mathematical model.


#### Ex vivo permeation study

The permeation profiles of the assembled LST-LPX formulations were assessed using a USP I dissolution device (Erweka DT-720, Erweka GmbH, Heusenstamm, Germany). The permeation testing adopted the same procedure and experimental settings as the release analysis, substituting the cellophane membrane for a shaved dorsal portion (5 cm2) of rat skin. Wistar rats (6–8 weeks old, 100–120 g) were euthanized by decapitation under anesthesia, and each rat dorsal skin was excised. Animal skin hairs were detached through shaving, subcutaneous tissues were surgically separated, and the dermal surface was treated with isopropyl alcohol to eradicate the remaining adipose material, followed by rinsing with PBS pH 7.4 (El Menshawe et al., 2019). The diffusion milieu (PBS pH 7.4, 100 mL) was kept at 37 ± 0.5 °C and stirred at 100 rpm. The test was conducted in triplicate employing non-occlusive circumstances. The predetermined volumes of LPX dispersions containing a constant amount of LST (3 mg) were added to the dorsal surface of rat skin. Samples from the receptor compartment were collected at specified time intervals (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h), after which the receptor chamber was refilled with equivalent quantities of fresh milieu. The collected samples were filtered using a 0.45 μm membrane filter and subsequently analyzed at 227 nm using a spectrophotometer. The cumulative drug permeated amount per unit area (μg/cm2) was graphed against time (h) for each formulation. The permeation parameters, including Q24 (μg/cm2), lag time (min), permeability coefficient (Kp, cm/h), and drug flux (Jss, μg/cm2 h), were calculated for all formulations as well as the control LST solution (Alsaidan et al., 2024).


### Optimization of LST-LPXs

Utilizing the desirability function, the Design-Expert® program was employed to choose the optimal dispersion of LST-LPXs. The procedure of optimization aimed to establish a formulation that exhibited the highest EE%, CR%, and Q24 while also having the lowest Z-Ave. The proposed LST-LPXs formulation with a desirability index closer to one was elected. To ascertain the model validity, the opted formulation was developed, scrutinized, and compared to the predicted solution (Salem et al., 2022).


### Characterization of the optimized LST-LPXs formulation


#### Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

DSC was employed to assess potential interactions among formulation components (Aboud et al., 2024b; Eid et al., 2024). Each sample, weighing approximately 5 mg, comprised LST (pure drug), PL, DDAB, Transcutol P, and a physical mixture of each component in a 1:1 ratio along with LST. The samples were placed in aluminum pans, which were then hermetically sealed. DSC measurements were conducted using a DSC-60 calorimeter (DSC-60, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). An empty aluminum pan served as the reference. The sample and reference pans were heated from 25 °C to 300 °C at a constant rate of approximately 10 °C/min under a nitrogen flow maintained at 20 mL/min. Baseline calibration was performed using two empty aluminum pans under the same experimental conditions.


#### Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

An FT-IR spectrometer (IR435-U-04, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was employed to analyze chemical interactions between LST and the functional groups of the components used in LPXs assembly. The KBr pellet method was performed under inert conditions within the 4000–400 cm−1 range. FT-IR spectroscopy was used to analyze the spectral profiles of various purified constituents, including LST (pure drug), PL, DDAB, Transcutol P, and the physical mixtures of LST with each of these constituents (1:1) (Aboud et al., 2024b; Eid et al., 2024).


#### Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The optimum LST-LPXs formulation was visualized using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400, Jeol, Tokyo, Japan) operating at 80 kV to observe its morphological appearance. On a carbon-coated copper grid, a small amount of the diluted vesicular dispersion was placed. The grid was then treated with 2% w/v phosphotungstic acid and left to dry at ambient temperature for 10 min before examination (Elkomy et al., 2024).


#### Physical stability study of LST-LPXs

The optimized LST-LPXs formulation was stored in a glass vial for three months at 4 °C for stability assessment. Samples from the optimal formulation were obtained post-preparation and thereafter at 30, 60, and 90 days of storage. The retracted samples were analyzed for EE%, Z-Ave, and ζ potential, with measurements conducted thrice (Aboud et al., 2024b).


### In vivo experimentations


#### Experimental animals

Adult female albino Wistar rats (200–240 g) sourced from the animal house of Nahda University, Beni-Suef, Egypt, were enrolled in this investigation. Rats were housed in a pathogen-controlled, air-conditioned (25 ± 1 °C) animal facility for seven days for acclimation prior to laboratory experimentations with unrestricted access to standard forage and tap water ad libitum. The institutional animal ethics committee of Beni-Suef University approved the experimental protocol for the animal investigation (Approval no: 024–094). The procedures complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (2011) established by the United States National Academy of Sciences.


#### Skin irritation studies

The optimum LST-LPXs formulation was transformed into a gel by integrating 1% w/w hydroxypropyl methyl cellulose K4M for transdermal use. To evaluate the safety of this transdermal nanogel, an in vivo study was conducted using 12 female Wistar rats. The rats were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6): a normal control group and a gel-treated group. Prior to gel application, the dorsal hair of each rat was shaved. The transdermal gel was applied to the designated area daily for two weeks. Throughout the treatment, any signs of local adverse effects, such as skin irritation or erythema, were monitored and recorded at the site of gel application to assess dermal safety. Upon completion of the study, the animals were euthanized under anesthesia via cervical decapitation. Skin samples from both the gel-treated areas and corresponding untreated regions of the same animals (serving as control) were excised. The collected tissues were perfused with normal saline, fixed in 10% neutral phosphate-buffered formalin, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were deparaffinized using xylol and hydrated using ethyl alcohol. Following the protocol described by Bancroft et al., paraffin-embedded tissue slices (5 μm thick) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Bancroft et al., 1989). The stained sections were visualized and documented using a digital camera attached to a light microscope.


#### Pharmacokinetic studies


##### LST administration to rats

The pharmacokinetics of transdermally-delivered LST-LPXs gel were assessed in comparison to those of orally supplied crude LST solution along with traditional LST gel. The animals were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 4). The dosage of LST provided was 20 mg/kg for different treatments (Nguyen et al., 2022; Refaat et al., 2013). The first group received free LST dissolved in water (8 mg/mL) via oral gavage. The second and third groups were transdermally-administered the conventional LST gel and optimized LST-LPXs gel, respectively. The transdermal gel (0.5 g) was spread over 4 cm2 surface area on the pre-shaved/cleaned dorsal side of each rat. The animals had unrestricted access to food and water until the evening prior to dosing, after which they maintained a 12-h fasting. Blood specimens (0.5 mL) were gathered at specified intervals through retro-orbital insertion (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h post-administration) into heparinized tubes. Blood was promptly centrifuged at 704.34 ×g for 10 min, and the isolated plasma specimens were stored at −20 °C until analysis.


##### Chromatographic conditions

The collected plasma samples were examined for LST via a validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method (Wadie et al., 2020). The configuration comprised a Shimadzu Prominence (Japan) series LC system featuring a degasser (DGU-20A3), an auto-sampler (SIL-20 AC), and an Agilent Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column measuring 4.6 × 50 mm with a particle size of 2.7 μm. The mobile phase comprised acetonitrile and 0.2% formic acid (98:2, v/v). Aliquots (10 μL) were injected into the LC apparatus at a 1 mL/min flow rate. A positive ion mode was employed for the quantitation of LST and simvastatin (internal standard) and operated via electrospray ionization (ESI) within the mass spectrometer. The temperatures of the autosampler and column were kept at 25 °C. The voltage of the ion discharge was set to 5 kV. LST was quantified utilizing the multiple reaction monitoring mode (m/z 423.26 → m/z 207.12) alongside the internal standard (m/z 441.4 → m/z 325.2). The calibration curve was highly linear (R2 = 0.999) within the 50–600 ng/mL concentration range.


##### Sample preparation for analysis

The frozen plasma specimens were defrosted at ambient temperature before performing the liquid-liquid extraction approach. A volume of 200 μL of plasma was blended with 10 μl of the internal standard solution (6 μg/mL) and 100 μL of 0.2% formic acid, vortexed for 20 s, followed by adding 5 mL methyl tert-butyl ether, the extraction solvent. The mixture was vortexed for 2 min to ensure thorough mixing and then centrifuged at 704.34 ×g for 10 min. The clear supernatant obtained was evaporated to dryness at 45 °C using a vacuum concentrator. The resulting residue was reconstituted in 200 μL of acetonitrile and injected into the LC system for analysis.


##### Pharmacokinetic analysis

Non-compartmental analysis was employed via the PK solver, an add-in for Microsoft Excel, to compute the various pharmacokinetic parameters (Arafa et al., 2022). Cmax, expressed in ng/mL, was the highest concentration documented throughout the experiment. The time required to attain the maximal concentration, Tmax (h), was also computed. The trapezoidal rule was employed to calculate the integral of the plasma concentration-time curve. The concentration at the final time point divided by the elimination rate constant produced the residual area, which was subsequently utilized to calculate AUC0–∞. #### In vivo study in CFA-induced RA in rats


##### Experimental design

Rats were arbitrarily assigned to four weight-matched groups, each consisting of 10 rats. The first group served as a standard control and was administered only vehicles. The second group was kept as a positive control arthritis group and received CFA (1.2 mL, S.C. over 12 days). The third group acquired CFA as the second group and standard treatment dexamethasone orally in a dose of 1.5 mg/kg/day (Ahmed et al., 2015). The fourth group got CFA as the second group and oral LST solution. The fifth group received CFA as the second group and transdermal LST gel. The sixth group received transdermal LST-LPXs gel in addition to CFA as the second group.

##### Blood and tissue sampling

Blood samples (1.5 mL) were collected from the retro-orbital plexus in plain tubes from all rat groups. Serum obtained after blood centrifugation at 704.34 ×g for 15 min was utilized for the determination of rheumatoid factor (RF) level as a specific rheumatoid arthritis biomarker. After cervical dislocation, the entire right and left rat joints were dissected and irrigated with saline. The first joint was kept at −80 °C until the assay of nitric oxide (NO) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase as oxidative stress biomarkers, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) as cytokines, Ang II, AT1R, and AT2R receptor signaling as inflammatory biomarkers; while the second joint and spleen were collected for histopathological examinations.


##### Estimation of serum RF level and joint inflammatory biomarkers

Serum RF level, besides joint tissue NO, NADPH oxidase, IL-6, TNF-α, and Ang II levels, were quantified using ELISA kits via an ELISA screening system (Model Spectra Max Plus-384 Absorbance Microplate Reader, Philadelphia, Bridgeport, CT, USA) based on the previously outlined sandwich technique (Lequin, 2005).


##### Western blotting estimation

The tissues of all groups were homogenized, and the total protein concentration was measured utilizing a BCA protein quantification kit (23,225, Thermo Fisher). The tissue lysates were applied to 7, 10, or 12% SDS–PAGE gels for separation, in accordance with the molecular weight of the target proteins, and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). At ambient temperature, the membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST on a shaker for 2 h prior to its incubation with diluted primary antibodies (1:1000) as follows: AT1R and AT2R, β-actin (Elabscience), at 4 °C overnight. The membrane was washed with TBST for 10 min, repeated three times. Subsequently, it was treated with the suitable horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody against IgG for rabbits and mice. The membrane was then rinsed again with TBST three times for 10 min. The target protein was subjected to an ECL chemiluminescent system (Tanon-5200Multi, Shanghai, China). The target protein band value was then calculated using Image J1.8.0 software.


##### Histopathological study of joint and spleen samples

Rats were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and spleens were gently dissected and preserved in 10% formalin solution in saline for histopathological examination. Joints were also dissected after removing hair and preserved in 10% formalin solution in saline for histopathological examination. According to Bancroft et al., after tissue hardening, samples were processed by paraffin embedding technique (Bancroft et al., 1989). After fixation, the samples underwent decalcification using EDTA solution for 10 days, followed by dehydration in graduated ethyl alcohol (50, 70, 95, and 100%) for 2 h each and clearance in 2 changes of xylene. The samples were then inserted in paraffin wax (3 changes, 2 h each) at 70 °C before blocking. Microtomy was done using a Leica microtome, and 5 μm tissue sections were fixed on a clean glass slide and were stained using routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stain for histopathological evaluation utilizing a light microscope attached to a digital camera.


##### Radiological study

Radiographs of lower limbs were obtained utilizing an X-ray device with a 0.5 mm focal spot, with a focal film distance of 61 cm, and exposure settings of 30 s at 45 kVp and 3 mA. Joint alterations were evaluated according to joint space and deformity. Radiographs were explored by an experienced radiologist who was unaware of treatment groups.


### Statistical analysis

All continuous data were enlisted as average ± SD. A one-way ANOVA and a Tukey post hoc test were employed to ascertain the statistical significance of the differences between the groups utilizing the computer software SPSS 22 (SPSS, Chicago, IL). A p value of less than 0.05 delineated statistical significance.


## Results and discussion


### D-optimal design optimization

Empirical designs are extensively exploited in the fabrication and optimization of drug delivery systems pursuant to their aptitude to minimize the number of required experimentations. In the current study, D-optimal design was opted as it effectively accommodates both numerical and categorical causal covariates (Romes et al., 2022). This statistical approach optimizes complex mixed processes by reducing the variance of parameter estimators via diminishing the standard deviation of the covariance matrix. It was evidenced that the D-optimal design requires fewer trials than conventional designs while maintaining the same level of error and variance of the estimated parameter (Franceschini and Macchietto, 2008). Herein, 16 experimental runs were generated based on the adopted D-optimal design for the assembly of LST-LPXs as recorded in Table 1. The crafted nanodispersions were assessed for EE% (Y1), Z-Ave (Y2), CR% (Y3), and Q24 (Y4) as dependent variables. The Design-Expert® tool was employed to conduct multiple regression analysis for statistical examination. The most adequate model was identified based on its significant p value besides an insignificant lack of fit and this model was then used to represent each response. The correlations between the causal variables and responses were scrutinized utilizing regression formulas, with the magnitude and sign of each variable providing key insights. A positive sign designates a synergistic effect, whilst a negative sign suggests an antagonistic impact. Table 2 offers a detailed analysis of the design estimates, demonstrating a robust correlation between the predicted and adjusted R2 values for all responses. Also, the adequate precision of all responses exceeded 4, underscoring the reliability of the results (Arafa et al., 2022). Additionally, ANOVA results confirmed that all causal factors significantly impacted the response variables. #### Effect of causal factors on EE% (Y1)

The modified LPXs aptitude to incorporate elevated LST levels is crucial for their specific exploitation as an innovative nanoparadigm to guarantee enhanced therapeutic effects. Consequently, it is imperative to minimize the leakage of clasped compounds. The generated formulations were assessed for their EE% to quantify the amount of entangled LST. As denoted in Table (1), the EE% values fluctuated from 51.37 ± 2.84 to 95.39 ± 1.21%, elucidating fruitful entrapment of LST within the vesicular nano-cargo. All independent parameters significantly impacted LST retention in LPXs, as documented in Table 2, p < 0.0001. The ANOVA analysis revealed that the 2-FI model had the best fit for the observed EE% data, as evidenced by its highest R2 value.

The results of the current study indicated that PL:SAA molar ratio (X1) significantly influenced EE% (p < 0.0001). Such relationship proved direct proportionality, indicating that an increment in the molar ratio resulted in an upsurge in EE%. This observation could be owed to the solubilization of PL by SAA, leading to the drug's leakage from LPXs when both components possess analogous ratios. Nonetheless, as the PL concentration escalated, the SAA elicited an inadequacy for solubilizing the surplus PL, evoking dense PL bilayers and donating the drug more space to be assimilated. As a result, the LST encapsulation efficiency was concurrently boosted (Refai et al., 2022). Our findings align with those of Eid et al., who reported augmented EE% values (62 to 94%) of ambrisentan-loaded LPXs upon elevating the PL:SAA molar ratio (Eid et al., 2024).

With respect to surfactant type (X2), the present study revealed that enrolling both SAAs contributed to an increment in EE% as they could potentially reinforce the drug incorporation into the PL bilayers, Fig. 1a. The inconsistencies in the EE% capabilities of CTAB and DDAB arise from their lipophilicity and chemical structures. CTAB manifests a singular alkyl chain structure. However, DDAB possesses a dual alkyl chain configuration, conveying immense lipophilicity and more surface area to interact with LST, forming stable nanovesicles with compelling drug encapsulation (Salama et al., 2019). Also, the hydrophilic lipophilic balance (HLB) of the cationic SAA could markedly impact the explored EE%. LPXs crafted utilizing CTAB with the higher HLB value of 21.4 disclosed lower EE% versus LPXs based on DDAB with the lower HLB of 18.1(El Menshawe et al., 2019). The current outcomes correspond with those of Hassan et al., who claimed magnified EE% (˃ 95%) of DDAB-based LPXs relative to those employing CTAB (Hassan et al., 2018). On the other hand, the aforementioned findings were contradictory to prevailing literature, which disclosed that CTAB had a more significant impact on raising the EE% than DDAB (Abdellatif et al., 2022b; Eid et al., 2024; Refai et al., 2022). This discrepancy could be argued in terms of variations in the physicochemical traits of the laden drugs.

In parallel, the EE% of LPXs disclosed a simultaneous enhancement as the concentration of LST ascended (X3), p < 0.0001. This enrichment could be probably succumbed to the electrostatic interactions between LST and both cationic SAA and PL. Earlier studies demonstrated that LST dissociates into losartan anions and potassium ions in aqueous solutions. The losartan molecule (Log P 5.37) contains ionizable groups, such as a carboxylate group and a tetrazole ring, which confer a negative charge and lipophilic trait to the molecule (Palli et al., 2022). Additionally, cationic SAAs possess positively charged head groups, exemplified by quaternary ammonium ions. In solution, negatively charged compounds such as losartan can engage in electrostatic interactions with the cationic SAA and neighboring N+(CH3)3 domains of the PL, conferring a robust anchor at the aqueous/lipid interface (Palli et al., 2022). Another reasonable interpretation is that LST contains hydrophobic regions that allow it to form van der Waals interactions between the butyl alkyl chain and the biphenyl group of losartan with the acyl chains of PL and the lipophilic moieties of SAA (Shakeel and Mahmood, 2021). Hence, such amphipathic interactions would snowball the entrapment of LST into the vesicular nanovector. The present data are comparable with those achieved by Hodzic et al., who narrated the capability of LST residence at the polar–apolar interfaces (Hodzic et al., 2012).


#### Effect of causal factors on Z-Ave (Y2)

Bioactive transport via the skin has been proven to be significantly impacted by the Z-Ave of lipidic vesicles. Nanosized vesicular ranges could elicit greater accessibility throughout the body, whereby they are freely transported via the systemic circulation into diverse organs/tissues (El Menshawe et al., 2019). The Z-Ave of the tailored LST-LPXs formulations spanned from 158.23 ± 7.63 to 436.82 ± 11.51 nm, pointing out a nanoscaled range as listed in Table 1. The ANOVA revealed that all independent variables significantly affected the Z-Ave of the yielded LPXs formulations (p < 0.0001). Additionally, the ANOVA results for the Z-Ave data indicated that the 2-FI model most accurately represented the dataset provided, with an F-value of 106.6.

As remarked in Fig. 1b, upraising PL:SAA molar ratio triggered the assembly of significantly larger LPXs, p < 0.0001. The detected findings might be ascribed to the SAA inadequacy in diminishing interfacial tension, leading to larger and stiffer LPXs formation, particularly at greater PL concentrations. On the other hand, an elevation in cationic SAA concentration could lower the Z-Ave due to the SAA-mediated solubilization of lipids (Abdellatif et al., 2022b). A further probability might involve electrostatic repulsion from SAA molecules, at higher levels, impeding vesicle assembly with a synchronous drop of the Z-Ave. Moreover, reducing aqueous-lipid interfacial tension could provoke LST leakage from LPXs coupled with vesicular size shrinkage. El Sisi et al. declared parallel results upon fabrication of chitosan-coated LPXs encapsulating mirtazapine for nose-to-brain delivery (El Sisi et al., 2025).

As regards to the SAA type (X2), the Z-Ave analysis of LST-LPXs disclosed that utilizing single-chained cationic SAA, CTAB, generated smaller vesicles than the double-chained SAA, DDAB. This is likely due to the influence of DDAB double alkyl chains, which should engage a greater surface area than the single-alkyl-chained variant. Consequently, DDAB-enriched LPXs disclosed proportionally expanded vesicle diameters due to the larger spatial occupation of DDAB within the vesicular architecture (Albash et al., 2021). Also, the potential of CTAB to yield nanovesicles with lesser aggregation and greater stability could be another plausible explanation for our findings (Albash et al., 2021). Moreover, CTAB could elicit superiority in reducing the interfacial tension than DDAB, provoking the generation of smaller Z-Ave with CTAB-enriched LPXs. Our results are aligned with those of Date et al., whose study proved that DDAB-based lecithin nano-cargos had a much larger particle size of 400 nm versus 240 nm for CTAB-based vesicles having equimolar ratios (Date et al., 2011b).

Of note, the Z-Ave of LST-LPXs was synergistically influenced by the concentration of LST (X3), p < 0.0001. This phenomenon could be reasonably attributed to the increased quantity of LST that would intensify electrostatic binding with SAA and PL molecules. Additionally, as delineated before, LST exhibits hydrophobic moieties that could facilitate van der Waals interactions and further contribute to the structural integrity and dynamic behavior of the vesicular nanosystem (Hodzic et al., 2012; Trapani et al., 2011). Consequently, such interactions would snowball the entrapment of LST, resulting in larger vesicles. These observations are in a close harmony with those of Arafa et al., who studied the influence of drug concentration on particle size of tunable mixed micelles with varying praziquantel concentrations (Arafa et al., 2022).

One way to gauge the distribution of particle sizes in a sample is to consider the PDI. PDI has a numerical value between 0 and 1. A PDI of 0.3 or below is considered adequate in drug delivery applications utilizing lipid-based nanovectors and denotes a homogeneous population (Ali et al., 2021). In the present work, the PDI values of LST-LPXs oscillated between 0.20 and 0.42, indicating an acceptable distribution of the assembled nanovesicles.


#### Effect of causal factors on CR% (Y3)

The CR% of LST from various LPXs formulations as compared to crude LST is graphically represented in Fig. (S1, supplemental file). The release of crude LST over 2 h was approximately 99.13 ± 3.77%, elucidating that the employed dialysis bags did not hamper the drug efflux and the sink condition was markedly fulfilled. Also, LST is a hydrophilic drug, rendering it readily released into the hydrophilic release milieu. On the other hand, the CR% from the crafted LST-LPXs varied between 64.54 ± 2.78 and 99.52 ± 6.27% as clarified in Table 1. The comparative release data pointed out the discernible reservoir impact of the nanovesicular systems that could monitor and extend the release of the laden drug over time (Aboud et al., 2024a; El Menshawe et al., 2019; El Menshawe et al., 2024). In general, all LPX formulations elicited in vitro release profiles that featured a biphasic pattern: an initial burst (first 2 h) release preceding a sustained release phase. This overt biphasic release could be correlated to the initial prompt efflux of the corona-tethered drug, followed by a prolonged release of LST from the nanovesicular core. The ANOVA results pointed out that all independent variables significantly affected the CR% data (p < 0.0001) and fit best to a linear model.

The CR% values demonstrated an inverse association with the PL:SAA molar ratio (X1), Fig. 1c. This negative correlation could be elucidated by a declined bilayer flexibility with upraised vesicle stiffness, and medium viscosity at higher PL levels (El Sisi et al., 2025). Consequently, the rate of drug release into the dissolving milieu was lessened. On the contrary, the recorded CR% values were greater with elevated SAA levels, likely due to the formation of smaller vesicles, which contributed to a broadened surface area. Our results are consistent with those reported by Abdellatif et al., who explored the release behavior of vancomycin-loaded LPXs (Abdellatif et al., 2022a). According to their investigation, the higher the SAA molar ratio, the greater the steady-state flux values achieved.

Further analysis of the results revealed that the SAA type (X2) remarkably influenced the CR% of LST from the fabricated LPX formulations (p < 0.0001). Presumably, incorporating the SAA DDAB, which divulges more hydrophobicity with a Log P value of 11.8, could reduce the CR% values relative to the SAA CTAB, which possesses lesser hydrophobicity with a Log P value of 8 (Eid et al., 2024). An additional explanation previously proposed was that the incorporation of DDAB into LPXs provoked the formation of larger vesicles, and this phenomenon was accredited to the molecular structure of the double-tailed DDAB in contrast to the single-tailed CTAB. Such findings are endorsed by earlier literature reports (Albash et al., 2021; Date et al., 2011b; El Sisi et al., 2025).

Likewise, there was a significant antagonistic association between LST concentration (X3) and CR%, p < 0.0001. The results disclosed that formulations encompassing higher LST levels had inferior CR% than those containing lower concentrations. This phenomenon might be attributed to the vesicle size; larger vesicles often have a smaller relative surface area-to-volume ratio, resulting in slower release rates (Salem et al., 2022). This would justify the notion that the more the LST content within the LPXs, the bigger they were. Moreover, they could have more robust bilayers, reducing membrane permeability and drug efflux. These findings coincide with those suggested by previous arts (Arafa et al., 2022; Azouz et al., 2025; Sahu and Das, 2014).

The kinetic examination of LST release data by linear regression designated that the drug was predominantly released from the LPXs formulations following Higuchi kinetics, enlightening a diffusion-controlled mechanism, Table (S1, supplemental file). Various investigations indicated that drug-laden nano-cargos would adhere to a controlled-release approach, coinciding with Higuchi plots (Aboud et al., 2024b; Arafa et al., 2022; Azouz et al., 2025). The Korsemeyer-Peppas model was also utilized, revealing that the release exponent (n) varied from 0.55 to 0.73 across various dispersions. Based on these values, LST release could occur via a non-Fickian (anomalous, 0.5 < n < 1) mechanism, in which molecular diffusion might be hybridized with copolymer chain relaxation and erosion (Azouz et al., 2025; Mahmoud et al., 2017).


#### Effect of causal factors on Q24 (Y4)

The skin presents a significant barrier to the entry of substances, barely permitting any amount of a drug to get through over time. Apparently, hydrophilic drugs, such as LST, elicit poor skin absorption; therefore, developing an effective formulation involving the drug assembly into a compatible nanosystem might be beneficial to optimize the drug physicochemical traits for transdermal delivery (El Menshawe et al., 2019). The prepared LPXs exhibited strikingly greater skin penetration than the drug solution containing an equivalent amount of LST, Fig. (S2, supplemental file). As marked in Table 1, the Q24 of the tailored LST-LPXs dispersions ranged from 204.90 ± 13.88 to 534.56 ± 16.34 μg/cm2, meanwhile, only 107.72 ± 16.78 μg/cm2 of LST solution permeated the rat skin. Collectively, the computed permeation parameters of the examined LST-LPXs through rat skin were compiled in Table (S2, supplemental file). The assessed LPXs showed transdermal flux values that varied from 8.24 ± 1.49 to 22.01 ± 6.22 μg/cm2 h, compared with 4.28 ± 1.08 μg/cm2 h for the standard LST solution. These results proclaimed the superiority of LST-LPXs permeation over the crude LST. Permeation enhancement of LST-LPXs can be ascribed to diverse mechanisms: (i) vesicles could behave as transcutaneous drug carrier nanosystems whereby intact vesicles penetrate the SC holding the laded drug moieties under the impact of natural hydration gradient (Mahmoud et al., 2017); (ii) vesicles can function as penetration enhancers, wherein vesicle bilayers traverse the SC and consequently modulate its intercellular lipids, thus, accentuating its fluidity and allowing solitary penetration of drugs; (iii) the greater affinity of PL for biological membranes could trigger the blending of the vesicular bilayers with the dermal intracellular lipid layers which further contributes to the LPXs permeation enhancement (El Menshawe et al., 2019); (iv) the presence of SAAs (DDAB or CTAB) in LST-LPX formulations also operates as permeation enhancers, which symbiotically impacts LST permeation through the skin. This is because cationic SAAs could disrupt the structure of the cellular bilayer of lipids, enhancing drug absorption and unleashing tight junctions between epithelial cells (Azouz et al., 2025); (v) the positively charged LPXs allow more contact and attraction to negatively charged corneocytes of the SC; thus, better permeation would be accomplished (Baspinar and Borchert, 2012) and (vi) being elastic vesicles, LPXs might undergo a cascade of stress-reliant modifications to curtail their motion resistance through the transepidermal confining channels conferring a reproducible and non-invasive drug transport (Mahmoud et al., 2017).

Statistical analysis using ANOVA indicated that the recommended sequential model for predicting the Q24 response was 2-FI, with 0.9307 as an adjusted R2 value. This implies that the model could interpret approximately 93% of the total variations in the transdermal Q24. The three causal variables significantly influenced the Q24 parameter (p < 0.0001), and PL:SAA molar ratio (X1) was the most predominant factor. It is worth mentioning that all the independent variables adversely impacted the permeation results, Fig. 1d. Actually, these observations are not surprising if they are interpreted on the basis of the vesicular size wherein larger Z-Ave with a reduced surface area disclosable to the diffusion milieu were produced at higher PL:SAA molar ratio (X1), greater LST concentration (X3) and in presence of DDAB, thereby limiting LPXs interaction with the SC with a sluggish drug diffusion. The dependency of the permeation behavior on the Z-Ave of the tailored nano-cargos had already been documented elsewhere (El Menshawe et al., 2019; Salem et al., 2024a; Salem et al., 2022). Mahmoud and co-workers reported congruent findings upon assessing the transdermal properties of various SAAs-comprised transfersomes loaded with atorvastatin (Mahmoud et al., 2017). Also, Shah et al. shared similar outcomes regarding enhanced transdermal idebenone penetration employing CTAB rather than DDAB (Shah et al., 2015).

#### Short-term stability

To appraise the physical stability of the optimal LST-LPXs nanoformulation, parameters like EE%, Z-Ave, and ζ potential were assessed after three months of storage at 4 °C. ζ potential tests are crucial for predicting the stability of colloidal dispersions, as they are influenced by various factors such as temperature, solvent viscosity, pH, ionic strength, and surface properties. Even slight variations in these parameters can substantially impact the absolute value of ζ potential, which is a key indicator of nanoformulation stability. A ζ potential value between +30 mV and +60 mV reflects excellent stability with minimal aggregation tendency (Salem et al., 2024a). In the present investigation, the mean ζ potential of LST-LPXs was +31.63 ± 3.72 mV, designating a dispersive quality. Also, our results manifested no aggregation or aberration during the storage period. As displayed in Fig. (S3, supplemental file), the storage duration did not significantly affect the EE%, Z-Ave, or ζ potential of the nanoformulation, p ˃ 0.05. The coexistence of the cationic SAA, DDAB, would have enriched the overall positive charges on the LPXs corona, which could snowball the aqueous stability of the crafted nano-cargo through electrostatic repulsion (Azouz et al., 2025).


### In vivo experimentations


#### Skin irritation study

Histopathological examination demonstrated minimal structural alterations in the skin of rats treated with the LPXs gel compared to the untreated skin of the normal control group, thereby substantiating the dermal tolerability and biocompatibility of the applied LST-LPXs transdermal formulation (Fig. S4, supplemental file). Moreover, throughout the treatment period, no signs of adverse dermal reactions, such as erythema or irritation, were observed at the gel application site in any of the animals.

The LST-LPXs gel exhibited a T1/2 of 6.36 ± 1.23 h following transdermal application. Nevertheless, the T1/2 values were 3.77 ± 0.601 and 4.26 ± 0.54 h for the oral and traditional gel administrations of LST, respectively. The extended T1/2 clarified ameliorated and sustained absorption of LST following the transdermal application of LST-LPXs gel. The MRT of LST after the oral and transdermal administrations of both the crude and LPXs gels were 5.28 ± 0.77, 7.37 ± 1.06, and 11.04 ± 1.29, respectively. The present observations could verify the aptitude of the crafted LPXs to guard against the prompt clearance of LST via cutaneous blood vessels and maintain it long enough on/in/beyond the skin barrier (Mahmoud et al., 2017). The AUC0–24 values of LST in plasma were 16,720.92 ± 1721.28, 19,881.83 ± 1239.56, and 47,295.25 ± 2254.75 ng h/mL upon the administration of the oral LST solution, traditional LST gel, and LST-LPXs gel, respectively. It is essential to note that the AUC0–24 of the transdermal LST-LPXs gel was significantly greater than the parallel values of the oral solution and traditional gel, exhibiting differences of approximately 2.83- and 1.19-fold, respectively (p < 0.05). The Frel of LST was nearly 120.47 and 308% for the traditional and LST-LPXs gels, respectively, compared to the oral solution. The LPXs gel outperformed the oral solution and conventional gel in delivering LST to the bloodstream. This finding might be ascribed to abnegating the first-pass metabolism associated with oral LST delivery and the enhanced penetrating capabilities of the tailored nano-cargo. Largely, the combined results of boosted AUC/Frel and prolonged T1/2/MRT could underscore the unique and unprecedented potential of the transdermal LPXs gel to accentuate LST systemic bioavailability in Wistar rats.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous art has explored the effect of transdermal LST-LPXs gel on experimental RA. Ang II was reported to act on AT1R and AT2R receptors in osteoblasts, inhibiting their proliferation, while the blockage of these receptors was discovered to hinder bone loss and enhance cartilage healing (Ren et al., 2024a).

## Conclusion

The current work entailed the assembly of LPXs as a cationic nanowagon for transdermal delivery of LST based on D-optimal design for tackling RA. The optimal LPXs formulation, which comprised a PL:SAA molar ratio of 3:1, drug concentration of 10.7 mg, and DDAB as a cationic SAA, elicited adequate EE%, small Z-Ave, and ameliorated LST release and permeation characteristics compared to the crude drug. The pharmacokinetic analysis proclaimed an accentuated bioavailability of the optimal LST-LPXs gel of approximately 3.08-fold compared to the oral drug solution and extended the T1/2 to circa 6.36 ± 1.23 h. Additionally, the optimum LST-LPXs gel showed boosted effects against experimental CFA-induced RA, evidenced by lowering arthritic, inflammatory, and oxidative stress markers levels, besides modulating protein expression levels of AT1R and AT2R. Thus, the elaborated LPXs could confer an effective and tolerable nanovector for transdermal delivery of LST that would overwhelm its oral-related encumbrances, meanwhile accomplishing clinical benefits for RA management.
