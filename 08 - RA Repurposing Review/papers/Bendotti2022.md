## Abstract

In the last few years, a great interest has emerged in investigating the pleiotropic effects of Glucagon Like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonists (GLP-1RAs). While GLP-1RAs ability to lower plasma glucose and to induce weight loss has allowed them to be approved for the treatment of diabetes and obesity, consistent evidences from in vitro studies and preclinical models suggested that GLP-1RAs have anti-inflammatory properties and that may modulate the immune-system. Notably, such anti-inflammatory effects target different pathways in different tissues, underling the broad spectrum of GLP-1RAs actions. This review examines some of the currently proposed molecular mechanisms of GLP-1RAs actions and explores their potential benefits in reducing inflammatory responses, which may well suggest a future therapeutic use of GLP-1RAs in new indications.

## 1. Introduction

Glucagon Like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonists (GLP-1RAs) are a new emerging drug class in treatment of the two most world widespread metabolic diseases: diabetes and obesity [1]. GLP-1 receptor (GLP-1R) was originally identified in the endocrine pancreas and later was discovered in the intraepithelial lymphocytes and in immune cells in mice and humans too [2], thus highlighting its large spectrum of actions. Considering these premises, in the last years several studies have been conducted, demonstrating that GLP-1RAs not only control glucose ho­ meostasis, but also exhibit a specific contribution in regulating immune system and inflammation [3]. Evidence for the importance of GLP-1RAs’

anti-inflammatory proprieties derives at first from studies on pancreatic β-cell and subsequently on heart, vascular system, liver, kidney, brain, and eye [4–6]. Indeed, several molecular pro-inflammatory players such as glucotoxicity, oxidative stress, immune cells recruitment, cytokines production and lypotoxicity, are modulated by GLP-1RAs [7]. This is foremost importance because of the link between inflammation and long-term complications of diabetes and obesity [8,9], and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis [10]. It is conceivable that GLP-1RAs may reduce inflammation by direct mechanism in immune cells expressing GLP-1 receptors or by indirect mechanism through glycemic control and weight loss [3]. 1.2. In vitro evidences of GLP-1RAs anti-inflammatory and immunological properties

Many studies showed as GLP-1RAs in vitro can inhibit the inflam­ matory responses and modulate immune response [22,23]. Exendin and exenatide, two GLP-1RAs, suppressed the macrophage secretion of different inflammatory cytokines including IFN-γ, IL-17, IL-2, TNF-β, IL-6, IL-1β [22,23]. Particularly, it has been shown that exenatide modifies the macrophages phenotype toward anti-inflammatory phenotype by significantly increasing the level of IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine, and decreasing both TNF-α and IL-1β in cultured human monocytes/macrophages [24]. In isolated murine macrophages, pretreatment with exendin-4 for 6 h, inhibited lipopolysaccharides-induced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) [25]. Furthermore, in human monocyte-derived macrophages cultured with GLP-1, surface expression of CD163 and CD204 was upregulated, IL-10 secretion was increased, and the STAT3 pathway was activated, while STAT1 was inhibited [26,27]. After exposing human monocytes to exendin-4 during differentiation into macrophages, CD36 expression was inhibited as well, and foam cell formation was suppressed in vitro [19]. Some of the aforementioned effects were not observed in monocytes from obese patients, assuming an altered GLP-1R signaling in these subjects [28]. Liraglutide was

Further, semaglutide reduced monocytes migration and infiltration regardless of weight loss by decreasing the expression of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, an inflammatory chemokine produced by immune system cells, which mediates monocyte recruitment from the circulation to sites of inflam­ mation [32]. Similarly, after treatment with exendin-4 a reduction of the circulating levels of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 and CXCL-1 was observed, thus reducing the neutrophil recruitment [33,34]. Another non glucose-dependent GLP-1RAs anti-inflammatory effect was reported in normoglycemic mice after infusion with exendin-4 for 4 week [35]. The authors described a reduction in macrophage expression of CD11b, which is essential in monocyte adhesion, and a suppression in the levels of several modulators of macrophage function, including CXCL10, CXCL1, CCL2 and TIMP-1 [35]. GLP-1 improved the inflam­ matory milieu in adipose tissue of diabetic ob/ob mice decreasing F4/80+ CD11b+ CD11c+ macrophage population and the production of IL-6, TNF-α, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 [36], in a SIRT-dependent manner [37]. Exendin-4 also reduced macrophage infiltration and proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β), regardless of blood glucose levels in streptozotocin-induced rat model of diabetes [38]. Levels of CXC chemokine receptor-4 and toll-like receptor-2 and toll-like receptor-4 were also lowered by treatment with exenatide in chronic stressed-induced mice [39]. A decrease in the expression of pro-inflammatory markers IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-6 was also observed after treatment with dulaglutide in muscle of aged and immobilized mice [40, 41]. Interestingly, dulaglutide demonstrated to exert an early anti-inflammatory effect by reducing Mac-2 and CD-68 in a mouse model of atherosclerosis [42]. Other studies demonstrated that GLP-1RAs significantly downregulated pro-inflammatory pathways. Through activating cAMP signaling, exendin-4 suppressed expression of TNF-α [43] and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 in C57BL/6 or ApoE-/- mice isolated macrophages [44]. In a type 1 diabetes (T1D) rat model, exendin-4 reduced TGF-β1 levels and the expression of CD14 in macrophages, independently from blood glucose levels [45]. In adult diabetic mice, a 4-week treatment with exendin-4 reduced F4/80+

macrophages in murine hearts and improved the cytokine profile [46]. In Glp1r+/+ mice, exendin-4 increased cAMP accumulation in purified intraepithelial lymphocytes and downregulated the levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-22, IL-12β, TNF-α, CCL2, CXCL1, and CXCL2 mRNA transcripts [18]. The immune modulating effect of liraglutide was also evaluated in vivo in an islet transplantation model [47]. During the first few hours post-grafting, GLP-1RAs decreased macrophage infiltration [47] and attenuated M1 markers (CCR7, IL-6 and TNF-alpha) and increased M2 cell markers (Arg-1, IL-10 and CD163) [27]. In a murine heart transplant model liraglutide was able to ihnibit the infiltration of CD4+, CD8+, and CD11b+ cells in the allografts tissue [48]. Moreover, a GLP-1R-dependent reduction in the levels of MCP-1, TNF-α and TGF-β1 was reported [48].

Prom­ ising GLP-1RAs anti-neuroinflammation effect were examined in animal models. In mouse oxygen-induced retinopathy model, intravitreal administration of long-acting GLP-1RAs, downregulated levels of CD11b+ CD45+ cells [53] and suppressed proinflammatory molecules, including IL-1, TNF-α, C1q, IL-6 e CXCL2 in retinal inflammation model [53,54]. Dulaglutide treatment modulated the population of dendritic cells and macrophages in the central nervous system, by suppressing expression of granulocyte-macrophage-colony-stimulating factor in the central nervous system and of encephalitogenic Th1/Th17 cells [55]. Liraglutide in early stage of Alzheimer’s disease mouse models reduced the levels of C-reactive protein and IL-10 in the brain, which are both considered neuroinflammation markers in Alzheimer’s disease [56], but

also activated invariant natural killer T cells in C57BL/6 mice [57]. In murine models of diabetes, GLP-1RAs increased the number of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the lymph nodes [58] and reduced leukocyte islet-infiltration [59]. Liraglutide increased Treg cells in a diet-induced T2D murine model [60], while decreasing CD4+IFN-γ+ T cells (Th1) and CD4+IL-17a+ T cell (Th17), in the spleen [61] and also in a model of liver fibrosis [62]. Recently, it has been demonstrated in a T-cell-me­ diated murine model of nephritis, that liraglutide blocked Th1 and Th7 generation [63]. Interestingly, all these cited results were independent by any effect on body weight [61]. The use of GLP-1RAs in animal models downregulated the inflammatory cascade through different molecular mechanisms and promoted anti-inflammatory phenotype of lymphocytes and macrophages.

It is well demonstrated that markers of inflammation play a critical role in increasing the risk of cardiovascular diseases regardless of metabolic parameters [64]. Antinflammatory therapy with monoclonal antibody targeting IL-1β demonstrated to lower the rate of cardiovas­ cular events in patients with previous myocardial infarction [64]. Hogan and colleagues showed that 8 weeks of liraglutide therapy reduced the levels of sCD163, which is a macrophage marker of activation and decreased inflammatory cytokines as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 in obese patients with T2D [65]. Interestingly, these results were obtained independently by glycemic control or weight loss [65]. Similar findings were reported after exendin-4 treatment in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with T2D [66]. Indeed, exendin-4 suppressed TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 expression and CXCL10 and CXCL5 production via MAPK signaling pathways, improving immune dysregulation [66,67]. In human trials, GLP-1RAs demonstrated to significantly reduce inflammatory bio­ markers, especially when compared to other standards anti-diabetic treatments [6]. In patients with T2D and stroke, high-sensitive C-reac­ tive protein, TNF-α, and IL-6 levels were significantly higher in the sitagliptin groups as compared to the liraglutide group after 6 months of treatment [68]. The efficacy of GLP-1RAs in reducing inflammatory markers was also reported in patients affected by T2D and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [69]. The levels of high-sensitive C-reactive protein were reduced after 12 weeks of treatment with liraglutide as compared to metformin [69]. As far as the potential confounding effects of weight loss, it is well demonstrated that body weight loss has a positive effect in decreasing peripheral pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, IL-8 and MIP-1β [70]. After 12 months of liraglutide or dulaglutide treatment, circulating levels of IL-6 were markedly reduced in patients with T2D and this change was strongly related to the changes in waist circumference [71]. In a 52–week weight management trial, a reduction of high-sensitive C-reactive protein levels was observed during treat­ ment with semaglutide in non-diabetic obese patients [72,73]. However, statistical significance was lost when adjusted by body weight [72,73]. Despite these results, GLP-1RAs showed to have anti-inflammatory ef­ fects independently of weight loss or glycemic state [2,74]. In a clinical trial, twenty-four obese subjects with T2D were prospectively random­ ized to be injected with either exenatide 10 μg twice daily or placebo twice daily for 12 weeks [74]. Exenatide significantly decreased the expression of TNF-α, IL1-β, JNK1, SOCS3, and TLR4 in circulating mononuclear cells, both after acute administration and after long-term treatment with GLP-1RAs [74]. In addition, exenatide for 12 weeks reduced circulating levels of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 and IL-6 without any changes on body weight [74]. Moreover, in random­ ized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, cross-over trial, liraglutide treatment led to significantly reduction of TNF-α [75]. This result was confirmed after univariate and multivariate models, showing no asso­ ciation between changes in TNF-α and changes in weight [75]. Also, in patients with T2D, in the presence of euglycemic state, co-infusion of GLP-1 decreased IL-6, both during a normoinsulinemic as well as a hyperinsulinemic clamps [76]. with T1D; indeed the co-infusion of insulin and GLP-1 markedly sup­ pressed circulating levels of inflammatory biomarkers [77]. Specific immune effect of GLP-1RAs was supported after observing a decrease in IL-1β production by M1 macrophages and an increase in IL-10 produc­ tion by M2 macrophages in response to GLP-1RAs in 10 obese diabetic patients [65]. Further, it has been reported that treatment for 8 weeks with liraglutide in patients affected by T2D, activated iNKT cells [57]. Interestingly, GLP-1RAs administration promoted a downregulation of PD-1 expression in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in patients with pul­ monary diseases [78]. Emerging interesting treatment are being studied to limit the autoimmune β-cells destruction in T1D [79]. Since inflam­ matory pathways were recognized to alter insulin release from pancre­ atic tissue, several anti-inflammatory drugs were administered in patients with T1D, but unfortunately, they were associated with a series of adverse effects [79]. In the last decades, GLP-1RAs were tested for their immunological and β-cells protective effects. Von Herrath and colleagues reported a smaller decrease of C-peptide in subjects with recent-onset T1D treated with combination of liraglutide and an anti-interleukin IL-21 antibody as compared to placebo. Nevertheless, this result was not confirmed in arm treated with liraglutide only [80]. Further, in another study, significant changes in mean C-peptide con­ centrations were not achieved both in C-peptide positive or negative T1D subject after 26 weeks of liraglutide administration [81]. Further studies are needed to evaluate the impact of GLP-1RAs on inflammatory markers in T1D. Lastly, liraglutide and dulaglutide were administred in patients underwent to solid organ transplantation, demonstrating a significant improvement in metabolic control and no interference with immunosuppressive drugs [82]. Thanks to their cited immunological proprierties in vitro, GLP-1RAs could prove relevant benefits in this specific population beyond their effects on glucose homeostasis.

To date, GLP-1RAs can be used as first-line drugs in diabetic patients with high cardiovascular risk. Their ability to improve glycemic control with very low risk of hypoglycemia make them very handy drugs, with few side effects compared to the expected benefits. However, these drugs exert their positive effects beyond the reduction of HbA1c or weight loss. Preclinical studies showed as GLP-1RAs have inhibitory effects on many inflammatory pathways and these properties make them amazing candidate for therapy in T2D. Such effects have been confirmed both in in vivo studies exploring tissue and organs changes but also in human clinical trials. Better mechanistic understanding of the action of these drugs could highlight their pleiotropic effects resulting in protec­ tion from chronic complications. Further investigations correlating major cardiovascular and renal outcome to biomarkers of low-grade inflammation are also needed. Besides, future studies could allow to individualize new GLP-1RAs with definite therapeutic targets in order to personalize therapy based on patient comorbidities. The glucose- dependent insulinotropic polypeptide/GLP-1 treatment will be avail­ able soon. It has proved to impact substantially on reduction of glucose levels and body weight. However, given the conflicting results on the pro and anti-inflammatory effects of glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide in atherosclerotic plaque formation, it could be also inter­ esting to examine how the glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide/GLP-1 association exert its potential immunological effects in the other tissues and organs. This evidence could strengthen their clinical use but also open new perspective on long-term beneficial effects of those therapies.
