## Abstract

**Objective:** Hydroxymethylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase inhibitors (statins) are widely used lipid-lowering agents. In addition to their well-known effect on cholesterol levels, statins have been reported to display antiinflammatory activities both in vitro and in vivo. In this context, in vivo prophylactic and therapeutic effects of simvastatin were recently demonstrated in mouse collagen-induced arthritis, a well-described experimental model for human rheumatoid arthritis (RA). The aim of this study was to further investigate in vivo effects of 3 different statins, atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, and simvastatin, using the same experimental model.

**Methods:** Different doses and routes of administration were used for the various statins in an attempt to elicit antiarthritic activity in preventive and curative treatment protocols.

**Results:** ==Atorvastatin and rosuvastatin had no in vivo efficacy, as indicated by clinical, histologic (synovial hyperplasia, exudate, and cartilage damage), immunologic (anti-type II collagen IgG production), and biochemical (interleukin-6, serum amyloid A, and glucocorticoid production) parameters of inflammation and autoimmunity.== ==The previously described beneficial effects of administration of intraperitoneal simvastatin were reproduced in our experiments, but could be accounted for by very severe side effects of the treatment, leading to increased glucocorticoid levels.==

**Conclusion:** ==This work shows that different statins have no effect in a murine model of arthritis, an unexpected observation given the previously described therapeutic effect of statins in immune-mediated inflammatory diseases.== It is still unclear whether statins will have benefit in the treatment of RA.

The hydroxymethylglutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors (statins) constitute a family of chemically related molecules, selected for their lipidlowering effects. Statins are extensively used in medical practice, and large clinical trials have demonstrated their efficacy in reducing cardiovascular-related morbidity and mortality (1,2). Increasing clinical and experimental evidence indicates that some beneficial effects of statins may result from antiinflammatory mechanisms independent of their lipid-lowering effects (3–5). Such mechanisms include the inhibition of proinflammatory cytokine and chemokine production, adhesion molecule expression, and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) secretion (6–11). In addition, statins have been reported to suppress interferon- (IFN)–inducible expression of class II major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules on nonprofessional antigen-presenting cells, as well as class II MHC–dependent activation of T lymphocytes in vitro (12,13). Statins also decrease CD40 and CD40 ligand expression in various cell types, such as endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and macrophages (14–16). Finally, some of the statins, which are commonly used for the treatment of hypercholesterolemia, block lymphocyte function–associated antigen 1–mediated cell adhesion and costimulation of T lymphocytes (17).

In experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), a CD4 T cell–mediated disease model of multiple sclerosis, atorvastatin was recently shown to prevent or reverse chronic and relapsing paralysis (19,20). Similarly, lovastatin improved the clinical symptoms of EAE in rats (21).

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic, chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease that affects 1% of the population and is associated with considerable morbidity and mortality (22,23). RA is characterized by symmetric polyarticular inflammation that is frequently accompanied by joint destruction. Although the pathogenesis of RA is not completely understood, much insight into the cellular and molecular mechanisms involved has been gained in recent years (24). Predominant cell types involved in synovial inflammation include activated T cells, monocyte/macrophages, and neutrophils. Concurrent with the increased cellularity is an increased expression of proinflammatory mediators and MMPs (24). The antiinflammatory properties of statins already described thus suggest that the use of these compounds might be beneficial in the treatment of RA.

Simvastatin treatment has recently been reported to improve the course of collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) in mice (25). It was shown that simvastatin, administered intraperitoneally (IP) at a dosage of 40 mg/kg/day, effectively suppressed CIA, both prophylactically and if administered after clinically evident onset of the disease. Lower doses of simvastatin (10 or 20 mg/kg), however, had no significant effect. To further explore the effects of HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors on disease activity in a mouse model of CIA, we have attempted to reproduce the observations of Leung et al (25) concerning the antiarthritic effect of simvastatin. We also investigated whether this property extended to other statins. In this study, the potential prophylactic and therapeutic effects of simvastatin and atorvastatin (effective lipophilic statins) and rosuvastatin (a hydrophilic statin) were compared in CIA. The results reflect the summation of a number of independent experiments, and different doses and routes of administration of the statins were used in an attempt to elicit antiarthritic activity.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

Induction of CIA. Male DBA/1 mice, 8–10 weeks of age, were obtained from Harlan (Horst, The Netherlands).

Native chicken or bovine type II collagen (CII) was purchased from Morwell Diagnostics (Zurich, Switzerland) and was dissolved at 2 mg/ml in 0.1M acetic acid. For the induction of CIA, mice were immunized with 100 g of native CII (emulsified in Freund’s complete adjuvant containing 5 mg/ml Mycobacterium tuberculosis) by intradermal injection at the base of the tail. On day 21, a booster injection of 100 g CII in Freund’s incomplete adjuvant was given at the base of the tail. All immunization reagents were purchased from Difco (Basel, Switzerland). Institutional approval was obtained for these experiments.

Clinical assessment of disease. From day 15 after the first immunization onward, mice were examined daily for the onset of clinical arthritis. The severity of arthritis was scored on a 3-point scale, in which 0  normal appearance, 1  mild swelling and/or erythema, 2  pronounced swelling and erythema, and 3  joint rigidity. Each limb was graded, resulting in a maximum possible clinical score of 12 per animal.

Preparation of statins and treatment protocols. Atorvastatin (Pfizer, New York, NY) was prepared as a suspension in water and administered at 1 mg/kg (n  10) or 100 mg/kg (n  10) by oral gavage. Control mice (n  9) received the same amount of water. In the preventive protocol, the treatment was administered daily from the day of the first immunization. In the curative protocol, treatment was administered daily for 7–13 days from the day CIA became clinically detectable (clinical scoring 1).

Rosuvastatin, provided by AstraZeneca Pharmaceuticals (London, UK), was dissolved in phosphate buffered saline (PBS). In the preventive protocol, rosuvastatin was administered subcutaneously (SC) at 0.2 mg/kg/day (n  10) or 2 mg/kg/day (n  10) from the day of the first immunization. Control mice (n  10) received the same amounts of PBS SC. In the curative protocol, IP rosuvastatin was administered at 40 mg/kg/day (n  8) for 14 days and treatment was started on the day after the appearance of the first clinical symptoms of arthritis (clinical scoring 1). Control mice (n  8) received the same amount of PBS.

Simvastatin, provided by Merck (Rahway, NJ), was dissolved in 10% ethanol–0.1N NaCl (pH 7), according to the protocol recently described by Leung et al (25). Simvastatin was administered daily at a dosage of 40 mg/kg, either IP (n  18) or by mouth (n  13). The IP control mice (n  7) were injected daily with the same volume of vehicle. For oral administration, simvastatin was added to the drinking water and the control mice (n  11) received the same volume of vehicle. The treatment was administered for 14 days, starting on the day following the appearance of the first clinical symptoms of arthritis (clinical scoring 1). Mice were killed 48 days after the first immunization (for the preventive treatment protocols) or 7–15 days after CIA became clinically detectable (for the curative treatment protocols).

Histologic analysis. Knees were dissected, fixed in 10% buffered formalin, decalcified in 15% EDTA, and embedded in paraffin. Sagittal sections (8 m) of the whole knee joint were stained with Safranin O and counterstained with fast green–iron hematoxylin. Histologic sections were graded independently by 2 observers (VC and NB) who were unaware of animal treatments, by using an established scoring system for synovial hyperplasia (from 0  no hyperplasia to 3  most severe hyperplasia) and inflammatory cells in synovial fluid (from 0  no inflammation to 3  severely inflamed joint

fluid). Cartilage damage was determined by Safranin O staining (from 0  no change from a normal knee joint, fully stained cartilage to 3  total loss of Safranin O staining). The abdominal wall, liver, and spleen from mice treated IP and by mouth (controls and simvastatin treated) were fixed in 10% buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histopathologic examination.

Measurement of serum levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6), serum amyloid A (SAA), corticosterone, and CII-reactive antibodies. At the end of the experiments, blood was collected by cardiac puncture. All blood samples were collected in the morning to minimize diurnal variations in hormone levels. Serum levels of IL-6 were measured with a DuoSet enzymelinked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) development system from R&D Systems (Abingdon, UK). The detection limit for this test is 16 pg/ml. Serum levels of SAA were determined by direct ELISA, as previously described (26). The detection limit for this ELISA is 9 g/ml. Serum corticosterone levels were determined by radioimmunoassay (Diagnostic Systems Laboratories, Webster, TX), as previously described (27). Serum levels of total anti-mouse CII antibodies were determined using a commercial ELISA (Chondrex, Morwell Diagnostics, Redmond, WA). For determination of isotype-specific IgG1 and IgG2a anti-CII antibodies, 96-well plates were coated with bovine CII, which was diluted at 10 g/ml in coating buffer (PBS, 7 mM acetic acid, 7 mM K2HPO4, 0.17M NaCl, 0.02% NaN3, pH 7.5). Wells were then washed with PBS–0.05% Tween 20 (PBST) and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBST. Diluted sera (1:1,000 in PBS–1% BSA) were applied to the wells for 2 hours at 37°C. After washing, sheep anti-mouse IgG1 or IgG2a antibodies linked to alkaline phosphatase (The Binding Site, Birmingham, UK) were added at a dilution of 1:1,000 in PBS–1% BSA, and incubated for 1 hour at 37°C. Color was developed using p-nitrophenyl phosphate as a substrate, and optical density was assessed at 405 nm.

Draining lymph node cell cultures. Lymph node cells of statin-treated or control mice were seeded at 4  105

cells/well in 96-well plates in 200 l RPMI 1640 containing 100 IU/ml penicillin, 100 g/ml streptomycin, 5  105M -mercaptoethanol, and 1% autologous mouse serum. Cells were incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2 for 72 hours without or with 100 g/ml heat-denatured bovine CII. During the final 18 hours of incubation, tritiated thymidine was added at 1 Ci/ well. Cells were harvested, and radioactivity was measured to determine the amount of tritiated thymidine incorporation into DNA as a measure of cell proliferation.

Real-time reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) for class II MHC transcription activator (CIITA). Total RNA was isolated from draining lymph nodes and spleens of statin-treated or control mice using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). RNA (1 g) was digested with DNase I and reverse transcribed with avian myeloblastosis virus–reverse transcriptase and random hexamer primers (Promega, Madison, WI). Expression levels of the 3 CIITA transcripts (types I, III, and IV) were assessed by TaqMan real-time RT-PCR (Applied Biosystems, Rotkreuz, Switzerland) using previously described primers and conditions (28).

Lipid level measurements. Concentrations of total cholesterol and triglycerides in serum samples were measured using the Unimate 5 and Unimate 7 kits (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland), respectively, as previously described (29).

## RESULTS

Preventive effects of atorvastatin and rosuvastatin on CIA. Preventive treatment with orally administered atorvastatin (1 or 100 mg/kg/day) did not reduce the incidence of CIA (Figure 1A) or its severity, as indicated by the clinical (Figure 1B) and histologic (data not shown) arthritis scores, which were similar in treated and untreated mice. Similar results were seen with the hydrophilic statin rosuvastatin. Again, preventive treatment with SC rosuvastatin (0.2 or 2 mg/kg/day) did not protect mice from the development of CIA: the incidence (Figure 1C) and the mean clinical scores (Figure 1D) were not statistically different compared with control animals, although a favorable trend was observed at the higher dosage. However, in another series of experiments, mice receiving 20 mg/kg/day of rosuvastatin did not differ from control mice (data not shown). In accordance with these clinical observations, histopathologic scores of knee joints from rosuvastatin- and placebo-treated mice did not differ significantly between groups (data not shown). Preventive studies were not performed with simvastatin treatment.

Effects of statins on disease severity in established CIA. Simvastatin, atorvastatin, or rosuvastatin treatment was begun on the day following the appearance of the first clinical symptoms of arthritis and continued for 7–14 days. When rosuvastatin (40 mg/kg/ day) was administered IP and simvastatin (40 mg/kg/day) or atorvastatin (up to 100 mg/kg/day, data not shown) was administered orally, there was no protective effect observed on the severity of the disease (Figure 2A) or on the number of affected paws (Figure 2B). However, under the same protocol as previously reported by Leung et al (25), IP simvastatin had a marked protective effect on CIA when administered at the same dosage (40 mg/kg/day). This effect is consistent with the observations of Leung et al. The beneficial effect was apparent within 3 days after the commencement of treatment, and was evidenced by a reduced mean arthritis score (Figure 2A) as well as by a lower mean number of affected paws (Figure 2B).

Major adverse effects with IP simvastatin treatment. Injection of IP simvastatin at a dosage of 40 mg/kg/day (the only effective dose and route of administration to elicit a beneficial effect in CIA) was associated with severe toxicity. Mice receiving simvastatin by this route exhibited signs of toxicity shortly after the beginning of treatment, and progressive mortality was observed in this group over the 14-day treatment period, with the first deaths occurring after only 3 days (Figure 3A). The effect of simvastatin on survival was not dependent on immunization of the mice with CII, because IP simvastatin at a dosage of 40 mg/kg/day was also toxic in nonimmunized naive mice (3 of 6 naive mice died within 2 weeks of treatment).

In the CIA model, moribund mice that received IP simvastatin showed a decrease in clinical severity of arthritis prior to their death. Thus, the favorable effect on disease activity observed in the mice treated with IP simvastatin (Figure 2) may be attributed to the poor health of the surviving animals. At autopsy, mice treated with IP simvastatin showed macroscopic signs of severe peritonitis and an enlarged gastrointestinal tract (Figure 3B). Histologic examination of the abdominal wall, liver, and spleen revealed severe subacute inflammation of the peritoneum and infiltration of inflammatory cells into the parenchyma of the liver and spleen (Figure 3C). In contrast, no mouse died in any of the other groups (receiving placebo or IP rosuvastatin at the same dosage). Furthermore, oral simvastatin, administered at the same dosage, was well tolerated, and the corresponding histopathology in this group and that in rosuvastatintreated mice was no different from that in control animals.

Effects of statin treatment on immune responses. Because statins were previously reported to have an impact on immune responses (25), we analyzed the humoral and cellular components of the immune response to CII in statin-treated mice. In the curative protocol, the serum total IgG anti-CII titers were not significantly different in CII-immunized mice treated with IP or oral simvastatin when compared with control mice (Table 1), although there was a trend toward decreased levels in both simvastatin-treated groups. As reported by Leung et al (25), anti-CII IgG2a antibodies were decreased in mice treated with IP simvastatin, and oral simvastatin treatment achieved a similar effect. No difference in anti-CII IgG1 antibody levels was observed. Rosuvastatin (Table 1) and atorvastatin (data not shown) had no significant effect on anti-CII antibodies. In addition, we found that, upon stimulation with CII, the proliferation of lymph node cells isolated from simvastatin-treated mice was not lower than that of lymph node cells isolated from control mice (Table 1).

## DISCUSSION

Various clinical and experimental observations suggesting that statins display antiinflammatory and immunosuppressive effects have led to the hypothesis that these compounds might represent interesting new therapeutic agents for the treatment of chronic immunemediated inflammatory diseases. However, in the present study, we were unable to substantiate any beneficial effects of 2 lipophilic statins, atorvastatin and simvastatin, administered orally, and of 1 hydrophilic statin, rosuvastatin, administered SC and IP, on disease activity in murine CIA. In addition, although we confirmed that IP simvastatin at high doses reduces CIA, as recently described (25), in our study this effect appeared to be a consequence of severe toxic side effects of the treatment.

To confirm that the observed lack of effect of statins on CIA was not due to the administration of biologically inactive compounds, we tested the activity of our simvastatin and rosuvastatin preparations in an in vitro assay. In accordance with previously published observations (12,13), both statins inhibited IFN- induced class II MHC expression in microvascular endothelial cells, as determined by flow cytometry (data not shown). This inhibitory effect of statins was reversed by the addition of mevalonate, the immediate downstream metabolite of HMG-CoA. These findings indicate that the statins used in our in vivo experiments indeed display adequate biologic activity.

The high mortality observed in mice treated with IP simvastatin was due to massive peritoneal inflammation, leading to mechanical inhibition of intestinal function, intestinal occlusion, and IP hemorrhage. Although the exact causes of this impressive peritonitis are not clear, our observations suggest that simvastatin injections triggered local irritation of the peritoneal cavity. Some lipophilic statins, including simvastatin, have been reported to increase proinflammatory cytokine production by monocytes and dendritic cells (32,33). Furthermore, pretreatment of mice with simvastatin and atorvastatin was shown to increase the influx of leukocytes into the peritoneal cavity following subsequent inflammatory challenge with thioglycollate (32). Such mechanisms may have contributed to amplification of local inflammation occurring in the peritoneal cavity of simvastatin-injected mice. Histologic examination confirmed massive subacute peritonitis and, consistently, serum levels of acute-phase response markers, such as IL-6 and SAA, were increased. Circulating levels of corticosterone were also elevated in mice treated with IP simvastatin, and the antiinflammatory properties of these steroids may have contributed to decreasing arthritis in this group of mice. Disease activity in the CIA model is indeed known to be efficiently suppressed by glucocorticoids (34,35).

Studies in the EAE model of multiple sclerosis showed beneficial effects of atorvastatin and lovastatin on disease activity, both in preventive and in therapeutic protocols (19–21). Subsequently, 2 open-label clinical trials examined potential effects of lovastatin (20 mg daily over 12 months) and simvastatin (80 mg daily over 6 months) on multiple sclerosis (36,37). Furthermore, immunologic data obtained in the second trial showed no effect of simvastatin on relative numbers of monocyte and lymphocyte subsets or on the secretion of Th1 and Th2 cytokines, challenging the notion that meaningful antiinflammatory effects can be obtained with simvastatin at this dosage (37). Recently, a controlled clinical trial evaluated for the first time a potential effect of atorvastatin on human RA (38). That study showed a modest effect of atorvastatin treatment (40 mg daily over 6 months) on the Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS28 reduction of 0.5). In addition, atorvastatin treatment had beneficial effects on various markers of inflammation. However, the reported clinical effect was very weak compared with that of conventional disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs or novel biologic compounds. Additional studies are clearly required to determine whether statins, alone or combined with an established disease-modifying drug, may be beneficial in the treatment of RA.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that 3 different statins have no preventive or curative effects in the attenuation of autoimmunity in murine experimental arthritis, an observation that was unexpected given the previously described therapeutic effect of statins in immune-mediated inflammatory diseases.
