# Association between thiazolidinedione use and rheumatoid arthritis risk in patients with type II diabetes, a population-based, case–control study

**Authors:** Hsieh Ming-Shun, Hung Pin-Shun, Hsieh Vivian Chia-Rong, Liao Shu-Hui, How Chorng-Kuang  
**Journal:** International Journal of Clinical Practice (2021)  
**DOI:** 10.1111/ijcp.13804  
**PDF:** pdf/Hsieh2021.pdf

## Abstract

### Aim

A previous study revealed that PPARγ agonists have anti-inflammatory effects in rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Furthermore, some studies have shown that type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) may elicit the development of RA. In this study, we aimed to investigate whether the use of thiazolidinediones (TZDs) is associated with a lower risk of developing RA in patients with T2DM.

### Methods

Based on the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database, we conducted a nationwide case–control study. The selected cases were patients with T2DM who were diagnosed with RA between 2000 and 2013. The controls were retrieved at a ratio of 1:4 by propensity score matching.

Logistic regression was conducted to evaluate whether TZD use lowers the risk of RA in patients with T2DM. The dose–response effect was examined according to the total TZD dose, within 2 years before the index date (the first diagnosis date of RA), and TZD doses were divided into four groups by cumulative Defined Daily Dose (cDDD): <30, 31–90, 91–365, and >365 cDDDs.

### Results

A total of 3,605 cases and 14,420 controls were included in this study. After adjusting for age, sex, baseline comorbidities, the results demonstrated that TZD use did not significantly reduce the risk of RA in patients with T2DM (adjusted OR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.81–1.02).

In the subgroup analysis by total TZD exposure dose within 2 years, 91–365 cDDDs of TZD had a lower risk of RA development, aOR = 0.87 (95% CI 0.71–1.06) and >365 cDDDs of TZD, aOR = 0.85 (95% CI 0.73–1.01). In the trend test, P was <0.05.

### Conclusions

TZD use might reduce the risk of RA in patients with T2DM, but it was non-statistically significant. Further research is necessary to assess this association.

**Key words:** inflammation, PPARγ, rheumatoid arthritis (RA), thiazolidinedione, type II DM

## Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic systemic inflammatory disease that is triggered by unknown causes. External triggers such as cigarette smoking, infection, and trauma cause an autoimmune reaction, leading to synovial hypertrophy and chronic joint inflammation with extra-articular manifestations, and this is theorized to occur in genetically susceptible individuals.[1] The pathogenesis of RA is complex and involves T cells, B cells, and the interaction of many proinflammatory cytokines, mainly belonging to the Th1 and Th17 pathways.[2]

Patients with RA are at a higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) than subjects without diabetes.[3-8] Similarly, a higher risk of diabetes development has been reported in patients with other inflammatory diseases, such as psoriasis,[9, 10] atopic dermatitis,[11] hidradenitis suppurativa,[7, 12] periodontal disease,[8, 13] and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.[14, 15] The association between diabetes and inflammatory diseases is mainly due to a common pathogenic background.[16-25] It has also been reported that chronic low-grade inflammation in T2DM may elicit the development of RA in genetically susceptible individuals.[15, 26]

Peroxisome proliferator activated receptors (PPARs) are ligand-activated transcription factors that belong to the nuclear receptor family and comprise the following three subtypes: PPARα, PPARγ, and PPARβ/δ. Activation of PPAR-α reduces triglyceride level, and it is involved in the regulation of energy homeostasis. Activation of PPARγ causes insulin sensitization and enhances glucose metabolism, whereas activation of PPARβ/δ enhances fatty acid metabolism. In addition, it has been reported that PPARγ acts as a regulator of inflammatory response.[27, 28]

Thiazolidinediones (TZDs), PPAR-γ agonists, were first introduced for the treatment of T2D. Emerging evidence suggests that TZDs play important roles in regulating inflammation and joint destruction.[23, 29-33] There is no clinical research to prove whether the use of TZDs can effectively reduce the risk of RA development in patients with T2DM. Hence, in the present study, we assessed whether the use of TZDs can decrease the risk of RA development in patients with T2DM.

## Methods

### Data source and disease definition

We conducted a nationwide case–control study, using the Taiwan National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), which consists of standardized documents submitted by medical institutions through the National Health Insurance (NHI) program. The NHIRD has been well described in a previous study.[34] The NHI program covers the medical needs of more than 23 million individuals, representing more than 99% of the population of Taiwan. All clinical diagnoses were recorded according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) codes.

We identified the patients with T2DM aged 20–100 years using the ICD-9 codes (250.00, 250.02, 250.12, 250.20, 250.22, 250.30, 250.32, 250.40, 250.42, 250.50, 250.52, 250.60, 250.62, 250.70, 250.72, 250.80, 250.82, 250.90, and 250.92) from January 1, 2000 to December 31, 2013. The diagnosis of T2DM was confirmed if a patient was coded with T2DM in one of the following conditions: (1) being discharged from a hospital or (2) at least two or more outpatient visits within a 1-year period. The accuracy of T2DM diagnosis had been validated in a previous study.[35] The above validation study showed that the accuracy of T2DM diagnosis in the NHIRD in Taiwan was 74.6%. The probability of accurate diagnosis of T2DM among patients with ≥4 outpatient visits was 99.16 times greater than that of patients with ≤1 outpatient visit. The probability of accurate diagnosis in patients with ≥1 hospitalization was 5.26 times greater than that of patients who had not been hospitalized.

We retrieved and confirmed the diagnosis of RA (ICD-9-CM code: 714.0) by using the catastrophic card of RA of patients.[36] The following is a description on the catastrophic card from the National Health Insurance (NHI) Administration, Ministry of Health and Welfare of Taiwan: "Patients with Catastrophic Illnesses. If you are diagnosed by a physician as having a condition classified as a catastrophic illness by the Ministry of Health and Welfare, you can submit relevant information and apply for a catastrophic illness certificate. The application will be formally reviewed by specialists according to the latest diagnostic criteria, and if approved, the information is entered into your NHI Card. Patients with catastrophic illness certification who get care for the illness or related conditions within the certificate's validity period do not need to pay a co-payment for outpatient or inpatient care."

### Definition of the index date (year)

In this study, the cases were all patients with T2DM, who were diagnosed with RA after the diagnosis of T2DM. The index date was defined as the date of new diagnosis of RA in the patients, whereas, a random date (i.e., the calendar year) was assigned to the control subjects. We only included patients who had been diagnosed with RA and were diagnosed with T2DM for more than 2 years before the index date to allow further evaluation. We estimated the accumulative dose of TZD use between the index date and a 2-year period before the index date in the case and control subjects, respectively.

### Exclusion criteria

Patients who were already diagnosed with autoimmune diseases including systemic lupus nephritis (SLE) (ICD-9-CM: 710.0), psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis (ICD-9-CM: 696.0, 696.1, 696.8), multiple sclerosis (ICD-9-CM: 340), and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (ICD-9-CM: 556) during the study period were excluded from this study. Subjects who were younger than 20 years or older than 100 years diagnosed with type I diabetes mellitus and were diagnosed with T2DM after the index date were excluded from this study.

The case subjects who received TZDs after the index date (i.e., the date of RA diagnosis) were not excluded from this study. Instead, these patients were classified as unexposed case subjects. Therefore, the unexposed case subjects could not have led to an overestimation of the effect.

### Baseline comorbidities

In the present study, comorbidities were defined using the ICD-9-CM codes: hypertension (ICD-9-CM: 401-405), hyperlipidemia (ICD-9-CM: 272), chronic kidney disease (CKD) (ICD-9-CM: 581-588, 403-404, 285.21), congestive heart failure (CHF) (ICD-9-CM: 428), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (ICD-9-CM: 491, 492, 494, 496), ischemic heart disease (IHD) (ICD-9-CM: 410-414), cerebral vascular disease (CVA) (ICD-9-CM: 430-438), malignancy (ICD-9-CM: 140-208), and thyroid disorders (ICD-9-CM: 240–246). Figure 1 shows the selection algorithm of this study.

### T2DM severity

Considering the severity of T2DM, which plays an important role in the association between TZD use and RA risk, we classified T2DM severity using the adapted diabetes complication severity index score (aDCSI score).[37-39] The aDCSI score has been validated in the NHIRD.[37] The aDCSI score comprises seven categories of complications: cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, neuropathy, retinopathy, peripheral vascular disease, stroke, and metabolic emergency. Based on the aDCSI score, we divided the patients into two groups (aDCSI scores ≥3 and <3). The aDCSI score helped understand the difference between the case and control subjects. Also, in the main logistic regression model, we conducted a sensitivity test. We adopted the aDCSI score to substitute the selected baseline comorbidities to examine model stability. In this manner, we also prevented the multicollinearity of included variables.

### Propensity score matching

Propensity score (PS) matching can reduce selection bias or indication bias because it allows bundling of many confounding covariates that might be present in an observation study.[40-42] In our study, multiple basic or risk factors might have affected the clinical decision whether to use TZDs for T2DM treatment; for example, the physicians' preference or DM severity. The PSs indicated the possibility that TZDs might be administered if the covariates were present. For each patient, we calculated the PSs using multivariate logistic regression by entering the baseline covariates. We matched 1 case subject (RA case) with 4 control subjects (non-RA cases) according to the PSs and obtained a dataset composed of matched patients who had a statistically identical likelihood of TZDs use for T2DM control.

Furthermore, we matched the case and control subjects for all baseline characteristics. The baseline characteristics of both groups were compared, and then further matched using standardized mean differences, calculated as the difference in means or proportions of a variable divided by a pooled estimate of the standard deviation of the variable. A value of standardized mean difference equal to ≤0.05 indicated a negligible difference in means between the case and control subjects.

We randomly selected patients with T2DM who were not diagnosed with RA as controls. The cases and controls were matched at a ratio of 1:4 based on the propensity score including age, sex, baseline comorbidities, and the index year.

### Exposure assessment

The TZD dose was calculated between the index date and a 2-year period before the index date. To evaluate whether the risk of developing RA was associated with TZD dose, we classified TZD dose into four categories (<30, 31–90, 91–365, and >365 cDDDs). We conducted a trend analysis to evaluate the existence of dose–response effect between TZD dose and RA risk in patients with T2DM.

### Lag period of RA

RA was frequently delayed in early diagnosis, and it ranged from weeks to months. To address this lag period, we conducted a sensitivity analysis that excluded patients with T2DM in the case group who used TZDs within a 90-day period before RA diagnosis.

### Statistical analyses

A logistic regression model was used to conduct the univariate and multivariate analyses to estimate the crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of RA in the study subjects. The covariates included age, sex, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, CHF, IHD, CKD, COPD, malignancy, stroke, and thyroid disorders. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.3 software package (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

## Results

In this study, 3,605 cases were enrolled between January 1, 2000 and December 31, 2013. A total of 14,420 controls were retrieved according to propensity score matching with the case subjects. Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics, baseline comorbidities, DM severity, and medications of the case and control subjects. The average age at RA diagnosis was 62.6 years, and 75.15% of patients with RA were female. There were more TZD users in the control group than in the case group (12.48% vs. 11.57%). The case subjects were more likely to have CKD, malignancy, and thyroid disorders than the controls.

The proportion of antidiabetic drug use, including biguanides and sulfonylureas, was lower in the case subjects than in the controls. The severity of T2DM was higher (aDSCI score ≥ 3) in the case group (12.65%) than in the control group (8.02%).

Figure 2 shows the OR of developing RA between TZD users and non-users using the multivariate analysis. The risk of developing RA in patients with T2DM using TZDs was lower than that in patients not using TZDs, with adjusted OR = 0.91 (95% CI 0.81–1.02); however, the difference was not significant.

In the subgroup analysis by total TZD dose within 2 years, 91–365 cDDDs of TZDs had a lower risk of developing RA, with the aOR = 0.87 (95% CI 0.71–1.06) and for >365 cDDDs of TZDs, the aOR = 0.85 (95% CI 0.73–1.01). Overall, the higher the TZD exposure dose, the lower the risk of RA development. Further trend analysis also supported this observation (P for trend <0.05) (Table 2).

Considering the DM severity as an important confounding factor, we conducted the other sensitivity test, in which the selected baseline comorbidities were substituted with the aDCSI score, to examine model stability in this case–control study. And TZDs exerted protective effects against RA development in patients with T2DM of different severity (Table 3).

In the sensitivity test to address the bias of the lag time period, the results showed that after excluding the patients in the case group who took TZDs within a 90-day period before the index date, the TZD use still significantly affected RA risk (Table 4).

## Discussion

The results of large population-based studies have showed that TZD use has the potential to reduce the risk of developing RA in patients with T2DM. According to a previous study, PPARγ agonists have anti-inflammatory effects in patients with RA,[43] including regulating immune cells and reducing inflammatory cytokine secretion.[44, 45] Our study results were consistent to some extent with these previous study findings.

Currently, to the best of our knowledge, there is no large cohort study on the association between the risk of developing RA and TZD use in patients with T2DM. Although our study did not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in RA risk in patients with T2DM using TZDs (adjusted OR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.81–1.02). However, in the dose–response effect analysis, there was an obvious association between TZD use and RA risk reduction in patients with T2DM, especially in those who used >365 cDDDs of TZDs within 2 years.

It has been proposed that TZDs could exert their protective effect against inflammation shortly after their administration. In studies on the association between anti-diabetic agents and sepsis, TZDs were found to be associated with a modest reduction in mortality, and the effect was immediate.[46-48]

TZDs are selective ligands of PPARγ, used in the treatment of T2DM. Moreover, several studies have demonstrated that TZDs can exert anti-inflammatory effect.[43] For example, TZDs can inhibit macrophage activation, reduce inflammatory cytokine expression, and macrophage release.[49] In in-vivo studies, TZDs reduced NF-κB expression in the circulating mononuclear cells and increased IκB expression.[50-52] Furthermore, TZDs decreased the plasma levels of inflammation and cardiovascular risk markers, such as MMP9, CRP, PAI-1, and sCD40 in patients with T2DM. [53] TZDs can also induce the apoptosis of synoviocytes and reduce the secretion of IL-6, IL-8, and TNF in synoviocytes.[53, 54] The multiple mechanisms may explain the findings of our study.

There were some limitations to our study. Several variables that have been related to RA development were not included, such as smoking habit. However, we used COPD as a proxy to represent the smoking behavior. Furthermore, we used hyperlipidemia to partially represent the obesity condition. The results of our study will help understand the effect of TZD use on RA risk in patients with T2DM.

## Conclusion

TZD use might reduce the risk of RA in patients with T2DM, but it was non-statistically significant. Further research is necessary to assess this association.
