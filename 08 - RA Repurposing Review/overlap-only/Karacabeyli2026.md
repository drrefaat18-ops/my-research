# Cardioprotective mechanisms and effects of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists in autoimmune rheumatic diseases

**Authors:** Karacabeyli Derin, Lacaille Diane  
**Journal:** Rheumatology (Oxford, England) (2026)  
**DOI:** 10.1093/rheumatology/keag147  
**PDF:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13105841/


## Abstract


### Abstract

Cardiovascular diseases are the leading cause of premature death in many autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs). Landmark randomized controlled trials in diabetes and obesity have demonstrated that glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) have cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic benefits. Observational studies assessing GLP-1RAs in ARDs, including RA and SLE, have found reductions in risk of cardiovascular events similar to those reported in broader populations. GLP-1RAs likely exert their cardioprotective effects through direct and indirect mechanisms. GLP-1RAs treat type 2 diabetes and obesity, two important cardiovascular risk factors. They lower lipid levels by mitigating post-prandial hyperlipidaemia and reduce blood pressure by dampening carotid body–mediated sympathetic excitation. GLP-1RAs have anti-atherogenic and anti-inflammatory effects in mice. They attenuate T cell–mediated inflammation directly by activating GLP-1 receptors on gut intraepithelial lymphocytes, and myeloid cell-mediated inflammation indirectly by activating central neuronal GLP-1 receptors. While knowledge gaps remain, existing evidence supports a cardioprotective role for GLP-1RAs in ARDs.

Keywords: glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist, cardiovascular diseases, autoimmune rheumatic diseases


## Introduction

Cardiovascular (CV) diseases are the leading cause of premature death for people with common autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs), including RA, psoriatic diseases (psoriasis and PsA), axial SpA and SLE [1–3]. This heightened CV risk is multifactorial. Patients with ARDs live with chronic systemic inflammation that accelerates atherogenesis, commonly use medications like glucocorticoids and NSAIDs that can increase CV risk, and often have higher prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities [obesity, type 2 diabetes (T2D), hypertension, etc.] [4–8]. Managing these factors non-pharmacologically and pharmacologically is critical when providing comprehensive care for patients with ARDs.

Non-pharmacological interventions, such as optimizing nutrition and physical activity, are foundational for managing CV risk and improving the overall health of people living with ARDs. They are recommended by both the ACR and the EULAR [9–11]. The arsenal of medications to complement behavioural strategies and treat cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic conditions has continued to expand, and drug classes like sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) have emerged as promising therapeutic options. In people with T2D and/or obesity, GLP-1RAs facilitate weight loss, improve glycaemic control and inflammatory markers, and reduce risk of major adverse CV events (MACE) and death from any cause [12–16]. In people with T2D, they have also been shown to slow progression of kidney disease and reduce risk of renal failure [12, 17]. However, landmark randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that demonstrated these effects did not describe (or, in some cases, excluded) immunocompromised individuals [18]. As such, there has been growing interest in understanding how these CV and renal benefits apply to people with ARDs.

In this Review, we describe the mechanisms of action of GLP-1RAs that might explain their cardioprotective effects, summarize their established benefits in people with T2D and/or obesity, discuss real-world evidence investigating their CV and renal effects in adults with ARDs, and highlight opportunities for future research. Practical considerations pertaining to dosing, safety, tolerability and cost are discussed in separate review articles [18, 19] and are beyond the scope of this review.


## Cardioprotective mechanisms of GLP-1RAs

Native GLP-1 is produced and secreted by enteroendocrine L cells, predominantly located in the ileum and colon [20]. It is secreted in response to food intake and facilitates glucose-dependent insulin secretion by the pancreas. Native GLP-1, however, is rapidly degraded by dipeptidyl peptidase-4 [21], limiting its clinical utility. Interestingly, it was the isolation of Exendin-4 from Gila monster venom [22] that sparked the development of exenatide, the first GLP-1RA for clinical use. Exendin-4 is structurally similar to human native GLP-1 but resistant to enzymatic breakdown [23, 24]. Exenatide was approved by the Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of T2D in 2005 [20], 14 years after the isolation of Exendin-4 [22]. Since then, the drug class has expanded to include long-acting GLP-1RAs (e.g. semaglutide), oral small molecules (e.g. orforglipron) and agents with broader mechanisms of action such as dual agonists [e.g. tirzepatide, a co-agonist targeting glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and GLP-1 receptors], triple agonists (e.g. retatrutide, targeting glucagon, GIP and GLP-1 receptors) and combination therapies (e.g. cagrilintide–semaglutide) [25]. Our mechanistic understanding of GLP-1RAs has deepened as the drug class has evolved and multiple pleiotropic effects extending beyond glycaemic control have been discovered.

The GLP-1 receptor is a G protein-coupled receptor, and it is expressed on cells throughout the body [26]. When this receptor is stimulated by native GLP-1 or GLP-1RAs, various pancreatic and extra-pancreatic effects ensue, including (but not limited to) reductions in blood glucose, appetite, blood pressure, serum lipids and systemic inflammation [27]. GLP-1RAs likely exert their cardioprotective effects through a combination of direct and indirect mechanisms.


### Treating cardiovascular comorbidities

GLP-1RAs effectively treat two key CV risk factors: obesity and T2D. In the general population, every five-unit increase in BMI above 25 kg/m2 is associated with a 40% increase in risk of CV mortality [28]. In people with T2D, every 1% rise in serum haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) is associated with a 14% increase in risk of CV death [29]. GLP-1RAs, by facilitating weight loss and glycaemic control, mitigate these risks. In T2D, GLP-1RAs have also been shown to slow progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD) [17] and reduce risk of kidney failure [12]. CKD is one of the strongest CV risk factors [30], and risk of CV death increases exponentially as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) drops below 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 [31].

Beyond their effects on body weight, glycaemia and kidney health, mechanistic studies suggest that GLP-1RAs also directly reduce lipid levels, lower blood pressure, stabilize atherosclerotic plaques and prevent atherogenesis (potentially mediated by weight loss-independent anti-inflammatory effects).


### Lowering lipids and blood pressure

GLP-1RAs mitigate post-prandial hyperlipidaemia by suppressing production and increasing catabolism of apolipoprotein B-48(32), the protein component of chylomicrons (lipoproteins that transport dietary fats). In turn, GLP-1RAs reduce intestinal chylomicron production while increasing chylomicron lipolysis [33–35]. This minimizes post-prandial spikes in triglycerides [36], an independent risk factor for myocardial infarction, ischaemic heart disease, stroke and death [37, 38]. GLP-1RAs also reduce hepatic steatosis and serum triglyceride and low-density lipoprotein levels [27]. The GLP-1 receptor is also expressed on carotid body chemosensory cells in rats and humans, and, in hypertensive rats, Pauza and colleagues showed that administering a GLP-1RA attenuated carotid body–mediated sympathetic responses and blood pressure rises in both euglycaemic and hyperglycaemic conditions [39]. The authors postulated that under normal physiological conditions, post-prandial rises in blood glucose and insulin stimulate the carotid body, triggering sympathetic excitation. Meanwhile, GLP-1 (released in response to food intake) inhibits the carotid body, preventing excess sympathetic nerve activity. This balance can be lost in states of metabolic dysfunction (e.g. hyperglycaemia), leading to sympathetic overactivity and hypertension. GLP-1RAs may exert their blood pressure–lowering effects by restoring this balance in autonomic tone. However, reductions in blood pressure have been modest in many CV outcome trials, so the anti-hypertensive properties of GLP-1RAs are unlikely to play a major role in mediating their cardioprotective effects [27].


### Atherosclerotic plaque stabilization and regression

In mice, dulaglutide, liraglutide, lixisenatide and semaglutide treatment have all been shown to reduce atherosclerotic plaque area [40–43]. This was not seen in weight loss–matched control mice, suggesting a weight loss-independent anti-atherogenic effect [40]. However, this benefit was also seen with targeted inactivation of GLP-1 receptors on endothelial and haematopoietic lineage cells, suggesting that GLP-1RAs do not need to act directly on GLP-1 receptors on these cells to attenuate atherosclerosis [42]. In vitro and in vivo studies indicate that GLP-1RAs modulate macrophage polarization towards an anti-inflammatory M2 subtype [43]. Mice treated with lixisenatide for 4 weeks had more stable plaques with fewer inflammatory infiltrates, smaller necrotic cores and thicker fibrous caps than controls [43]. GLP-1RAs might stabilize existing plaques by reducing expression of matrix metalloproteinases, enzymes that can weaken fibrous caps and increase the risk of plaque rupture [40, 41].


### Anti-inflammatory effects

The systemic anti-inflammatory effects of GLP-1RAs likely also contribute to their anti-atherogenic properties. Intestinal L cells, which produce GLP-1, express toll-like receptor 4 and secrete GLP-1 in response to inflammatory triggers [e.g. lipopolysaccharide (LPS)] [44]. The L cell can therefore be thought of as an inflammation sensor, releasing GLP-1 to counterbalance gut and systemic inflammation stemming from intestinal barrier dysfunction [45]. While the anti-inflammatory effects of GLP-1RAs have been consistently demonstrated in both preclinical [45, 46] and clinical studies [14–16], underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood.

GLP-1 receptor expression is low in most peripheral organs and immune cells [45]. Myeloid cells express the GLP-1 receptor less than lymphoid cells, and GLP-1 receptor expression on peripheral blood mononuclear cells is low overall [45]. High levels of the GLP-1 receptor, however, are expressed by intestinal intraepithelial lymphocytes [47], and GLP-1RAs dampened T cell–mediated intestinal and systemic inflammation in mice by activating GLP-1 receptors on these cells [48]. These studies also showed that GLP-1RAs attenuated LPS-induced systemic inflammation independent of the intraepithelial lymphocyte GLP-1 receptor, suggesting that GLP-1RAs must act on other cell types as well. LPS stimulates myeloid cells via toll-like receptors, generating a pro-inflammatory state characterized by increases in TNF, IL-1β and IL-6 [49]. Myeloid cells, however, express low levels of the GLP-1 receptor and GLP-1RAs still exert anti-atherogenic effects in mice lacking GLP-1 receptors on haematopoietic cells [42]. Hence, Wong and colleagues asked whether GLP-1RAs mitigate myeloid cell-driven (LPS-induced and toll-like receptor–mediated) inflammation indirectly, through non-myeloid cells [50]. They discovered that GLP-1RAs attenuated LPS-induced inflammation by acting centrally, on neuronal GLP-1 receptors. GLP-1 receptor activation in the brain appeared to recruit α1-adrenergic and δ- and κ-opioid receptor signalling pathways to reduce plasma TNF levels. This anti-inflammatory effect was not seen in mouse models with knockdown of GLP-1 receptors in the central nervous system, or when these adrenergic or opioid signalling pathways were blocked, supporting that this mechanism is centrally mediated. Notably, tirzepatide, a dual agonist of GLP-1 and GIP receptors, decreased plasma TNF in mice lacking CNS GLP-1 receptors, suggesting that GIP might additionally act peripherally to dampen inflammation. The GIP receptor is expressed on myeloid cells, including monocytes and macrophages, and, unlike GLP-1, inflammatory signals do not stimulate GIP secretion in mice or humans [26]. The anti-inflammatory mechanisms of GLP-1/GIP co-agonism warrant further study.


### Direct actions on the heart and vascular cells

GLP-1 receptor expression is very low in the heart, yet preclinical studies show that GLP-1RAs improve myocardial glucose metabolism, reduce cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and protect against myocardial ischaemia–reperfusion injury [27]. GLP-1 receptors are expressed in some vascular cells and their activation triggers cAMP and PI3K/Akt signalling pathways [51]. This leads to improved blood flow (via increased nitric oxide production causing vasodilation) and reduced oxidative stress and vascular inflammation.

In summary, GLP-1RAs likely act via many mechanisms to exert their clinically relevant cardioprotective effects (Fig. 1). Current understanding of these mechanisms, however, is based on preclinical data and clinical data in people without ARDs. Questions relevant to individuals with ARDs therefore remain unstudied, including whether ARD-specific pathophysiology (different inflammatory profiles and gut microbial composition [52]) or ARD treatments (e.g. glucocorticoids, TNF inhibitors, IL-6 inhibitors) might modify the immunomodulatory effects of GLP-1RAs.

## Cardioprotective effects in diabetes and obesity

Several landmark RCTs have showcased the CV benefits of GLP-1RAs in populations with T2D and/or obesity (Table 1) [13, 16, 17, 53–56]. A 2025 meta-analysis of 10 RCTs including 67 769 participants with T2D found that risk of MACE was 13% lower with GLP-1RA treatment compared with placebo [12]. A 2021 meta-analysis including eight RCTs and 60 080 participants with T2D demonstrated an 11% reduction in risk of hospitalization for heart failure with GLP-1RAs relative to placebo [57]. The SELECT (Semaglutide Effects on Cardiovascular Outcomes in People with Overweight or Obesity) trial, an international double-blind RCT of 17 604 adults with pre-existing CV disease, BMI ≥27 kg/m2 and no diabetes, found that the absolute difference in rate of MACE (i.e. myocardial infarction, stroke or CV death) was 1.5% lower with semaglutide relative to placebo [8.0% vs 6.5%; hazard ratio (HR) of 0.8], yielding a number needed to treat of 67 [16].

With regard to heart failure, RCTs suggest that GLP-1RAs are efficacious in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) but not heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) (Table 2) [53, 58–61]. Early trials assessing liraglutide in adults with HFrEF were negative [58, 59], and safety concerns were raised when the LIVE trial found that serious cardiac events were more common in the liraglutide group than the placebo group [58]. Subsequently, the STEP (Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with Obesity)-HFpEF, STEP-HFpEF DM and SUMMIT trials evaluated the effects of semaglutide and tirzepatide, respectively, in adults with obesity and HFpEF [53, 60, 61]. The STEP-HFpEF trial showed that in adults with HFpEF and obesity without diabetes, heart failure–related symptoms and physical limitations (measured using the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire clinical summary score) improved significantly more with semaglutide than with placebo [60]. Moreover, participants receiving semaglutide improved their 6-min walk distance by 20 m more than the placebo group. The STEP-HFpEF DM trial then tested the same hypothesis in adults with HFpEF, obesity and T2D, and yielded similar findings (Table 2) [61]. In SUMMIT, participants randomized to receive tirzepatide had a 38% lower risk of death from CV causes or of worsening heart-failure events than participants randomized to the placebo group [53]. Over a median of 104 weeks, this composite primary outcome occurred in 9.9% of people in the tirzepatide group vs 15.3% in the control group, yielding a number needed to treat of 19. This difference was primarily driven by worsening heart-failure events, as deaths from CV causes were uncommon. Using the reported incidence rates (4.5 vs 8.2 events/100 person-years with vs without tirzepatide), one can calculate a number needed to treat of 27 for 1 year to prevent one worsening heart-failure event resulting in hospitalization, i.v. therapy in an urgent care setting or intensification of oral diuretic treatment. Furthermore, similar to the semaglutide group in STEP-HFpEF, the tirzepatide group in the SUMMIT trial experienced significantly greater improvements in heart failure–related symptoms and physical limitations relative to the placebo group.

In these trials, people with ARDs were not described or, in some cases, excluded from participation. For example, in the SUMMIT trial, people with significant musculoskeletal disease (e.g. arthritis) impairing exercise or walking capacity were excluded, as were people using variable doses of medications associated with weight gain (e.g. prednisone) [53]. In the FREEDOM CVO trial, individuals who were immunocompromised and/or using common immunomodulatory drugs (including, but not limited to, HCQ, MTX, LEF, AZA, tacrolimus, ciclosporin, MMF, infliximab or etanercept) were excluded [62]. Real-world evidence in adults with ARDs is emerging, and studies to date suggest that the cardioprotective effects of GLP-1RAs in individuals with ARDs [63] (including RA [64] and SLE [65]) are similar to those reported in broader populations with T2D or obesity.


## Real-world evidence supporting cardiovascular benefits in people with ARDs

In August 2024, we published a study showing that in adults with T2D and immune-mediated inflammatory diseases (IMIDs), GLP-1RA use was associated with lower risk of all-cause mortality and MACE (i.e. myocardial infarction, ischaemic stroke or CV death) relative to dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor (DPP4i) use [63]. We included RA, psoriatic disease (encompassing both psoriasis and PsA), axial SpA, SLE, idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, SSc, Sjögren’s disease, systemic vasculitides and IBD in our definition of IMIDs. Using administrative health data from a Canadian province, we assembled a population-based cohort of nearly 11 000 adults with T2D and IMIDs who were incident users of either GLP-1RAs or DPP4is. DPP4is were selected as active comparators given their similar indication as second-line therapy for T2D and their weight- and cardio-neutral properties [66]. Groups were balanced at baseline after applying propensity score (PS) overlap weighting. Using Cox proportional hazards models, we estimated a 34% lower risk of MACE with GLP-1RA treatment compared with DPP4i treatment, independent of baseline sociodemographic factors, comorbidities, T2D duration and complications, IMID duration, use of other relevant medications and healthcare utilization. Risk of death from any cause was 52% lower with GLP-1RA than with DPP4i use. We calculated that 96 (95% CI 49–1250) and 107 (95% CI 63–371) patients would need to be treated with GLP-1RAs instead of DPP4is for 1 year to prevent one MACE and one death, respectively. We then investigated whether people with IMIDs derived greater benefit from GLP-1RAs than people without IMIDs. We repeated analyses in age- and sex-matched adults with T2D without IMIDs and found that risk reductions were similar. This argued against the hypothesis that people with IMIDs might benefit more than people without IMIDs given their higher baseline CV risk or their ongoing systemic inflammation that could be improved by GLP-1RAs.

Subgroup analyses revealed that incident GLP-1RA use, relative to DPP4i use, was associated with significantly lower risk of all-cause mortality and MACE in RA but not in psoriatic disease. Adults with RA and psoriatic disease comprised about 80% of the final weighted sample (38% with RA, 42% with psoriatic disease). Events, however, were uncommon, particularly for psoriatic disease, limiting power. We also did not estimate the effects of GLP-1RAs in other subgroups such as SLE due to low numbers. Studies that ensued have replicated our findings in different settings and have filled gaps that remained after our study [64, 65].

In September 2025, Wang and Anthony published a brief report that provided additional evidence supporting the CV benefits of GLP-1RAs in RA [64]. They performed a retrospective cohort study using data from TriNetX, a global network of electronic health records data, comparing risk of arterial and venous thrombotic events among adults with T2D and RA who were new users of GLP-1RAs or DPP4is. After 1:1 PS matching, their cohort included 17 394 individuals who were balanced at baseline on age, sex, race, comorbidities, HbA1c, tobacco use and use of various medications (for T2D, CV disease, anticoagulation or RA disease control). BMI was included in the model for calculating PS but was not balanced between groups after PS matching. Mean BMI was 35 kg/m2 in new users of GLP-1RAs vs 32 kg/m2 in new users of DPP4is. Using Cox proportional hazards models, they found that GLP-1RA initiation, relative to DPP4i initiation, was associated with significantly lower risks of thrombotic events and all-cause mortality, including a 26% lower risk of arterial thromboses (i.e. myocardial infarction or cerebral infarction), a 20% lower risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE, i.e. deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism) and a 44% lower risk of death from any cause. Bias resulting from between-group differences in BMI would likely have blunted, rather than amplified, the protective associations seen since obesity is a risk factor for thrombosis [67, 68] and was more severe in the group receiving GLP-1RAs. Overall, this study added additional evidence to support a cardioprotective role for GLP-1RAs in people with RA and T2D, and suggested that these agents might also reduce VTE risk in this group. The latter, however, requires replication.

Another brief report was published in September 2025, expanding what is known about GLP-1RAs across the spectrum of ARDs [65]. Jorge and colleagues performed a target trial emulation using data from TriNetX, comparing the risks of MACE, VTE, kidney progression (new onset of end-stage renal disease or drop in eGFR by >30%) and all-cause mortality in adults with SLE and T2D who were initiating a new treatment course of GLP-1RAs or DPP4is. They applied PS overlap weighting to create two groups that were well-balanced across all baseline covariates, which included demographic variables, geographic region, comorbidities, tobacco use, SLE and T2D medication use, healthcare utilization, presence of LN, SLE severity, T2D complications and HbA1c. They identified 910 new users of GLP-1RAs (267 with LN) and 1004 new users of DPP4is (324 with LN). Using Cox proportional hazards models, they observed 34% lower risk of MACE with GLP-1RAs (vs DPP4is), 51% lower risk of VTE, 23% lower risk of kidney progression and 74% lower risk of death from any cause, all significant differences. In subgroups with LN, possibly due to reduced power from smaller sample sizes, GLP-1RA initiation was only associated with a significantly lower risk of MACE [HR 0.64 (95% CI 0.41–0.98)].

Their definition of MACE included myocardial infarction, ischaemic stroke, CV death and heart failure hospitalization. Most MACE observed were heart failure hospitalizations, and heart failure hospitalization is not included in the traditional 3-point MACE definition (myocardial infarction, stroke and CV death) or 4-point definition (myocardial infarction, stroke, CV death, and either urgent revascularization or hospitalization for acute coronary syndrome as the fourth component) [69]. When MACE definitions differ, comparing results across studies becomes more challenging. High rates of MACE (particularly in the LN subgroups, where incidence rates were a concerning 122 and 193 events per 1000 person-years with GLP-1RAs and DPP4is, respectively) suggest that the sample studied had high baseline CV risk. This may limit generalizability to healthier subpopulations with SLE. These data suggest that to prevent one MACE in adults with SLE, T2D and similarly high baseline CV risk, 32 people would need to be treated with a GLP-1RA instead of a DPP4i for 1 year. In LN, the number needed to treat drops to 15. However, it appears that the benefit was largely driven by reductions in heart failure hospitalizations rather than in traditional MACE (i.e. myocardial infarctions, strokes or CV deaths). Neither myocardial infarction nor stroke risk was significantly reduced with GLP-1RA exposure, although these outcomes were rare, estimates were imprecise and a type II error is possible. In this study, patients who used SGLT2is in the year prior to initiating GLP-1RAs or DPP4is were excluded. Evidence for the benefits of SGLT2is in the prevention and management of systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases is mounting [19, 70] and recent international guidelines suggest considering their use in LN [71]. SGLT2is have distinct immunomodulatory and cardioprotective properties, and studying whether concomitant SGLT2i use modifies the CV effects of GLP-1RAs in patients with ARDs is one of many existing opportunities for future research.

In all three studies, observed reductions in relative risk were larger than what has been reported in RCTs. Several factors may have contributed. First, the observational studies included high-risk patients who were excluded from RCTs. These individuals may have derived greater benefit from GLP-1RAs owing to their higher baseline risk profile. This might partially explain the larger effect sizes. Second, various forms of confounding can affect risk estimates from observational studies. While the inclusion of an active comparator and the use of PS-based methods can minimize confounding, residual confounding (including confounding by indication) may persist. Certain potentially relevant variables (e.g. socioeconomic status, disease activity, prescriber behaviour) are often inadequately captured or not measured in administrative and electronic health records data. This can lead to residual confounding (when the covariate is incompletely captured) and unmeasured confounding (when the variable is not available). Finally, certain covariates, like SGLT2i use, might change with time and can influence the apparent relationship between GLP-1RA exposure and CV outcomes. The three observational studies described did not account for time-varying confounding.


## Future directions

There is a paucity of real-world data on the cardioprotective effects of GLP-1RAs in ARDs aside from RA and SLE. Both RCTs and observational studies with extended follow-up are needed to better understand the efficacy and long-term safety of GLP-1RAs in populations with ARDs. The expansion of the drug class to include dual agonists, triple agonists and combination therapies, along with the increasing use of SGLT2is, present opportunities to assess for synergy. For example, can the concurrent use of GLP-1RAs and SGLT2is reduce CV risk more than either agent alone in people with ARDs, and to what degree? How does the co-activation of GIP, glucagon or amylin signalling pathways modify the effects of GLP-1RAs? Notably, the GIP receptor is expressed at low levels in the human heart [72], and, in mice, GIP receptor agonism reduces vascular inflammation and aortic atherosclerosis [73, 74]. In contrast, loss of GIP receptor signalling has the opposite effect [75]. Interest in the CV effects of GIP is growing, and more studies are needed in humans. A recent study of 64 patients with inflammatory arthritis and no diabetes found that fasting levels of GIP (as well as amylin and pancreatic polypeptide) increased following TNF inhibition. These changes were still present 1 year after treatment initiation and remained significant after adjustment for CRP measured during follow-up [76]. Individuals taking GLP-1RAs or SGLT2is, however, were excluded.

More research is also needed to clarify the degree to which cardioprotective and anti-inflammatory effects of GLP-1RAs are independent of weight loss and improved glycaemic control [18, 46]. Could these anti-inflammatory properties translate to clinically relevant improvements in disease activity for people with ARDs? While several questions remain, tremendous progress has been made in understanding the extra-pancreatic effects of GLP-1RAs.


## Conclusion

The cardioprotective effects of GLP-1RAs in adults with T2D and/or obesity are well-established. Based on large observational studies, people with ARDs (including RA and SLE) appear to derive similar CV benefits from these medications to those reported in broader populations. Several direct and indirect mechanisms likely play a role in explaining this drug class’s cardioprotective properties. GLP-1RAs effectively manage important CV risk factors, like T2D and obesity, and reduce CKD progression in people with T2D. They directly lower lipid levels by mitigating post-prandial hyperlipidaemia and reduce blood pressure by dampening carotid body–mediated sympathetic excitation triggered by glucose and insulin rises that follow food intake. In mice, GLP-1RAs stabilize and shrink atherosclerotic plaques, and their anti-inflammatory properties likely contribute to their anti-atherogenic effects. They attenuate T cell–mediated inflammation directly by activating GLP-1 receptors on gut intraepithelial lymphocytes, and myeloid cell-mediated inflammation indirectly by activating central neuronal GLP-1 receptors. Finally, GLP-1 receptors on heart tissue and certain vascular cells might act as additional relevant targets for GLP-1RAs. The effects of GIP receptor signalling on systemic inflammation and CV health are promising yet incompletely understood. While knowledge gaps remain, GLP-1RAs represent a potentially beneficial option for people with ARDs and concurrent cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic conditions.
