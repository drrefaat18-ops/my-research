ABSTRACT Peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor a (PPARa) is a nuclear receptor of clinical interest as a drug target in various
metabolic disorders. PPARa also exhibits marked anti-inﬂammatory capacities. The ﬁrst-generation PPARa agonists, the ﬁbrates, have
however been hampered by drug–drug interaction issues, statin drop-in, and ill-designed cardiovascular intervention trials. Notwithstanding,
understanding the molecular mechanisms by which PPARa works will enable control of its activities as a drug target for metabolic diseases
with an underlying inﬂammatory component. Given its role in reshaping the immune system, the full potential of this nuclear receptor
subtype as a versatile drug target with high plasticity becomes increasingly clear, and a novel generation of agonists may pave the way for
novel ﬁelds of applications. (Endocrine Reviews 39: 760 – 802, 2018)

he peroxisome proliferator–activated receptors
(PPARs) are ligand-inducible
transcription
factors that belong to the nuclear receptor (NR) su-
perfamily. Three PPAR subtypes have been identiﬁed:
PPARa (NRC), PPARb/d (NRC), and PPARg
(NRC) (–).
The ﬁrst member of the PPAR family, PPARa, was
identiﬁed in and named by its ability to become
activated by chemicals known to induce peroxisome
proliferation in rodents (). Peroxisomes are sub-
cellular organelles found in most plant and animal
cells and harbor a complex set of enzymes acting on
various metabolic functions, including b-oxidation of
fatty acids (FAs), bile acid, and cholesterol metabolism
().
Peroxisome proliferators cover a various set of
synthetic and structurally diverse industrial and
pharmaceutical chemicals, including herbicides, in-
dustrial solvents, and hypolipidemic drugs, that cause
signiﬁcant increases in the size and number of
peroxisomes in liver, liver hypertrophy, liver hy-
perplasia, and hepatocarcinogenesis in rodents ().
Following the initial discovery of mouse PPARa, the
two other PPAR subtypes, PPARb/d and PPARg,
were identiﬁed by homology screens (, ). Con-
tradicting their name, the latter two subtypes do not
cause peroxisome proliferation in mice (). The
PPAR subtypes are encoded by distinct genes located
on diﬀerent chromosomes [reviewed in Ref. ()] and
share a high degree of structural and sequence ho-
mology, but each subtype displays a divergent pattern
of tissue-speciﬁc expression and ligand-binding
speciﬁcity (, ).
PPARs were originally described as orphan NRs,
but shortly thereafter a plethora of potential endog-
enous ligands were described (), acknowledging
PPARs to be activated by a wide range of endogenous
ligands including FAs and FA metabolites, such as
hydroxylated eicosanoids, prostaglandins, and leuko-
trienes, as well as by many synthetic compounds,
including ﬁbrates and antidiabetic thiazolidinedione
drugs (, ).
Most of the physiological functions of PPARs can
be explained by their activity as transcription factors

PPARa Expression Proﬁle, Gene, and
Protein Structure
PPARa controls the expression of numerous genes
involved in a plethora of lipid metabolic pathways,
including microsomal, peroxisomal, and mitochon-
drial FA oxidation (FAO), FA binding and activa-
tion, FA elongation and desaturation, synthesis and
breakdown of triglycerides (TGs) and lipid droplets,
lipoprotein metabolism, gluconeogenesis, bile acid
metabolism, and various other metabolic pathways
and genes (). Consistent herewith, PPARa is widely
expressed in tissues with high FAO rates, such as heart,
liver and skeletal muscle, and functions as a major
regulator of FA homeostasis (–). PPARa ex-
pression is also signiﬁcant in brown adipose, adrenal,
and kidney tissue, as well as in several inﬂammatory/
immune cell types such as monocytes/macrophages
(, , ).
It is generally agreed that active PPARa is pre-
dominantly localized in the nucleus (). However,
PPARa was reported to be localized in the cytoplasm
of rat chondrocyte primary cultures (). Similarly, in
diﬀerentiated human macrophages, PPARa was also
much more localized in the cytoplasm (). Hence, as
with other NRs, PPARa displays a dynamic shuttling
between the cytosol and the nucleus. This shuttling is
inﬂuenced by Ca+ signaling and ligand binding, with
the latter enhancing nuclear import of PPARa (). In
this way, internal and/or external cell signals can be
translated into changes in subcellular localization and
turnover/stability of PPARa and, as a consequence,
aﬀect its regulation of cellular functions (). The
observation of a nucleocytoplasmic shuttling is not
only restricted to PPARa, but also applies to PPARg.
MAPK kinase–dependent export of PPARg from
the nucleus reduces its transcriptional activity and
therefore represents a mechanism by which nuclear
activities of PPARg and possibly also other NRs are
downregulated (). Further investigations are re-
quired to fully elucidate the underlying molecular
mechanism of nucleocytoplasmic shuttling as well as
the subcellular functions of this receptor. Moreover, a
better understanding of where and how PPARs are
activated, turned over, and regulated is of the utmost
importance.
ESSENTIAL POINTS
· Peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor (PPAR)a is a transcription factor whose levels and activity are controlled at
many diﬀerent steps (e.g., gene and protein expression and protein posttranslational modiﬁcations) and by many diﬀerent
factors (e.g., nutritional/hormonal cues, aging, cytokines, growth factors)
· Natural ligands exist and many diﬀerent synthetic ligands have been synthesized, some only for basic research but some
currently in clinical phase III trials for human disease
· PPARa drives PPAR response element–dependent and –independent gene transcription through various molecular
mechanisms, in support of the versatility of PPARa and expanding its target gene repertoire
· PPARa target genes are not only involved in glucose and lipid metabolism but also in inﬂammation-modulating pathways
· A most recently developed high-aﬃnity PPARa agonist is currently tested as a potential treatment of the inﬂammatory
state of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease in humans, that is, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis
· Although the vast majority of PPARa’s activities yield an anti-inﬂammatory proﬁle, inﬂammation-priming eﬀects have
also been documented; the circumstances in which the latter events occur and the physiological relevance hereof remain
to be further clariﬁed
· Future therapeutics relying on PPARs in a context of inﬂammation will most probably have to take “the clock” into

PPAR activity.

Similar to PPARs, many other NR members also
need RXRs as their obligatory heterodimerization
partner, raising therefore the possibility of a compe-
tition between particular NRs for their common
binding partner (). This competition may have
important biological outcomes. For instance, liver X
receptor (LXR)a activation by either overexpression of
LXRa or ligand activation suppresses PPARa sig-
naling through reduction of active PPARa–RXR
heterodimers in the liver and thus diminished PPRE
occupancy. RXRa supplementation restored the
LXR-mediated inhibition of PPARa activity ().
Conversely, excess PPARa in the presence of its li-
gands suppresses the sterol regulatory element–
binding protein (SREBP)-c promoter that contains
two LXR response elements (), indicating that the
mechanism of competition for RXRa between PPARa
and LXR may be reciprocal (). This relationship
between PPARa, LXR, and RXR could be crucial for a
mutual regulation of both LXR and PPARa activities,
and thus for the nutritional regulation of their
downstream target genes. However, the physiological
validation of these mechanisms still remains to be
demonstrated. Another example of competition for
RXRa between RXR-partnering NRs was described
for the thyroid hormone receptor with LXR ().
Of note, silent information regulator (SIRT) was
also shown to bind PPARa through a region ho-
mologous to the PPARa binding domain in RXRa.
Interestingly, the balance between SIRTand RXR
appears to determine whether PPARa is a positive or
negative regulator of genes involved in FA metabolism
(). Additionally, the PPARa–SIRTcomplex would

fat diet (HFD) also induces the formation of a
PPARa–SIRTcomplex, impairing an association of
PPARa with the proinﬂammatory transcription factor
p. Collectively, this shift is expected to lead to the
repression of PPARa-target genes involved in FAO
and enhancement of proinﬂammatory pathways in the
heart (). Albeit intriguing, more experimental evi-
dence is necessary to prove that PPARa can bind
DNA as an RXR-independent monomer and whether
this mechanism is also operative in other tissues such
as the liver.

Modulating Factors of PPARa Activity
PPARa gene and protein expression
The expression of the PPARa gene is regulated
by various physiological conditions such as stress,
glucocorticoids (GCs), growth hormones, insulin,
and leptin. As an important physiological response,
PPARa expression is induced during fasting, resulting
in an enhanced expression of PPARa target genes in
liver to operate FAO and to form substrates that can be
metabolized by other tissues. Oppositely, PPARa also
controls, directly or indirectly, lipogenic pathways
(which is further discussed in detail in “PPARa Target
Genes and Their Function”). Liver lipogenesis par-
ticularly allows FA synthesis in a fed state when dietary
carbohydrates are abundant (, ). Adipocyte-
derived factors may further inﬂuence the expression
level and subsequent PPARa activity. Consistent
with this, administering leptin, considered proin-
ﬂammatory, correlated with an increased PPARa
hepatic expression in ob/ob mice (). The mecha-
nisms underlying the induction of PPARa tran-
scription by leptin may be connected to changes in FA
ﬂux mediated by leptin signaling (). Leptin-
stimulated PPARa gene expression was also ob-
served in muscle cells, through the activation of and
changes in the subcellular localization of AMP-
activated protein kinase (AMPK)-a(). Addi-
tionally, leptin was found to enhance PPARa gene
transcription in pancreatic b-cells () but not in
adipose tissue (). The basis for this discrepancy is
not known, but ﬁndings generated using isolated
model systems will deﬁnitely require further conﬁr-
mation in vivo.
Alternatively, the anti-inﬂammatory adipokine
adiponectin also increased PPARa-responsive pro-
moter activity in HIIECcells, an eﬀect that required
coexpression of PPARg coactivator (PGC)-a, sug-
gesting that adiponectin-induced PPARa activity may
be mediated through upregulation of PGC-a
expression (). In contrast, GH has been shown to
rather decrease PPARa mRNA in cultured rat he-
patocytes (, ). A similar eﬀect was shown in
hypophysectomized rats (). Additionally, GH also
decreases PPARa transcriptional activity () as well
as the expression level of PPARa (). Similarly,
treatment of primary hepatocytes for days with
another anabolic signal, insulin, also led to reduced
PPARa levels (), whereas short-term exposure to
this hormone increased PPARa transactivation via
phosphorylation of the ligand-independent AF-
domain ().
Next to tissue-speciﬁc diﬀerences in PPARa levels,
the expression of PPARa in a given tissue further
oscillates under the control of the circadian rhythm.
For example, PPARa expression peaks during daytime
in mice, which correlates with the timing of fasting in
these nocturnal rodents (). These diurnal patterns
thus correspond well with known functions of PPARa
in physiology but also pathophysiology (). PPARa
is under the transcriptional control of the GC-
activated GC receptor (GR) in rat hepatocytes ().
As such, PPARa expression follows a diurnal rhythm,
which parallels that of circulating corticosterone, and
the levels of PPARa are also upregulated during times
of stress (). Moreover, the circadian expression of
PPARa was reported to be directly regulated by the
core-clock transcription factors brain and muscle
Arnt-like (bmal) protein and circadian locomotor
output cycles kaput (CLOCK) both in vivo and in vitro
(). In another study, the proline- and acidic amino
acid–rich basic leucine zipper (PARbZip) proteins
were also shown to modulate the rhythmic expression
and activity of PPARa. The mechanism was proposed
to be indirect and involved a cyclic generation of
PPARa ligands that initiate a feed-forward loop, in
which PPARa enhances transcription from its own
gene (). Additionally, enhanced PPARa transcript
levels were observed in both B and T cells upon GCs
treatment (), indicative of a function of PPARa in
the immune system (see “PPARa Agonists in a
Clinical Setting”).
Additionally, PPARa expression is also reported to
be related to aging. In this line of reasoning, exami-
nation of mRNA expression in mouse spleens revealed
an age-associated decline in the levels of mRNA
encoding PPARa and its concomitant target genes
ACOX and catalase, which was accompanied by a
constitutive proinﬂammatory cytokine production
(). Similarly, a recent study has shown that aging
inhibits PPARa activation as assessed by diminished
mRNA levels of its target genes ACOX and carnitine
palmitoyl transferase (CPT) in aged rat livers ().

triguingly, glucose and metabolites (glucose--phosphate
and glucose--phosphate) were shown to bind PPARa
with high aﬃnity, resulting in a signiﬁcantly altered
PPARa secondary structure (). In this study, in

prolonged fasting. Activated AMPK was recruited to
promoters of glucose and lipid metabolism genes that
are cocontrolled by PPARa and GRa (). For the
latter example in liver, it also remains to be discovered
which residues of involved transcription factors,
PPARa, GR, or both, may be phosphorylated by
AMPK.
PPARa phosphorylation, besides aﬀecting target
gene regulation, also aﬀects the stability and turnover of
PPARa through ubiquitination (, ). Ubiq-
uitination involves the conjugation of a small protein,
ubiquitin, to the target protein via a sequential action of
particular enzymes, which primarily leads to degrada-
tion of the receptor by the S proteasome. PPARa is
targeted by the Eenzyme murine double minute ,
which leads, depending on the cellular PPARa levels, to
stabilization of receptor levels or degradation via the
ubiquitin–proteasome pathway (). The eﬀect of
degradation on the transcriptional activity is still under
debate; Blanquart et al. () showed an inverse corre-
lation between PPARa degradation and activity, which
is in contrast to results obtained by Burns and Vanden
Heuvel (). Recently, a muscle ring ﬁnger-–mediated
monoubiquitination of cardiomyocyte PPARa was
discovered that enhances nuclear export of the receptor
and consequently inhibits PPARa-induced FAO in
cardiac tissue ().
GSKis a key enzyme of glycogen metabolism and
able to inactivate glycogen synthase. Its activation
phosphorylates PPARa predominantly at Serin the
A/B domain. This modiﬁcation negatively aﬀects
PPARa stability and promotes ubiquitin proteasome-
mediated degradation (). PPARa ubiquitination
may thus constitute a regulatory pathway that may
contribute to the duration and the magnitude of the
response to PPARa activators (, ). Functionally,
when blocking GSKby biliverdin reductase A in the
liver, a protective eﬀect against steatosis is inferred, due
to a preservation of hepatic PPARa activity ().
Next to phosphorylation and ubiquitination, PPARs
can be modiﬁed by SUMOylation. SUMOylation is
a reversible modiﬁcation that entails the cova-
lent attachment of small ubiquitin-related modiﬁer
(SUMO) proteins via speciﬁc enzymes (). Murine
PPARa was shown to be SUMOylated on Lys
(). This particular SUMOylation triggers the in-
teraction with GA-binding protein a bound to the
a-hydroxylase cytochrome Pbpromoter,
resulting in speciﬁc downregulation of this gene.
Hence, SUMO modiﬁcation can be regarded as a
positive regulator of mPPARa transrepressive ac-
tivity (). Likewise, human PPARa undergoes
SUMOylation by SUMO-on Lysin the hinge
region, leading to downregulation of its trans-
activation capacity through the selective recruitment
of NCoR () [Table (, )]. Both SUMOylation
sites are targeted by the Eenzyme protein inhibitor
of activated STATy (, ).
Finally, another means of regulating PPARa ac-
tivity indirectly includes the phosphorylation of RXR
and of various associated cofactors such as PGC-a,
silencing mediator for retinoid and thyroid hormone
receptors, and NCoR [reviewed in Ref. ()].
PTMs play a key role in modulating PPARa ac-
tivity in (patho)physiological contexts. The modifying
enzymes themselves are modulated by the cellular
metabolic status and environment, thereby positioning
PPARa into a very complex regulatory network. The
level of complexity is even intensiﬁed knowing that
PPARa forms part of a physical assembly for variable
corepressor or coactivator complexes, which are also
on their turn subjected to PTMs (). Full un-
derstanding of these complex patterns of regulation
requires more high-throughput technologies to char-
acterize the PPARa interactome, its dynamic inter-
actions with modifying enzymes, as well as the
mechanisms by which these interactions modulate
PPARa activity. These might point to drugs that can
modulate the PPARa PTMs status with regard to
metabolic disorders and inﬂammation.

of long-chain fatty acyl-CoA coinciding with an in-
creased peroxisome proliferation, hepatocarcinoma, and
profound activation of PPARa in rodents (, ).

enzyme may possibly be PPARa agonists. Numerous

putative PPARa ligand, which connects the receptor
to inﬂammation and immune responses. Its ability to
act as a PPARa agonist has been reported in several
studies (, , ), and the binding afﬁnity is
found to be within the nanomolar range (). Stim-

A–dependent manner, suggesting that phospholipids

Mice with liver-speciﬁc inactivation of FA synthase
(FAS), a key lipogenic enzyme, developed hypogly-
cemia, fatty liver, and defects in expression of PPARa
target genes when subject to a fat-depleted diet. These
eﬀects were reversed by a synthetic PPARa agonist,
identifying thereby the de novo synthesized end
products of FAS as endogenous activators of PPARa
in adult liver (). This phenomenon is not limited to
the liver. Indeed, selective inactivation of FAS in the
hypothalamus is found to aﬀect PPARa-dependent
gene expression and feeding behavior, which are
corrected after hypothalamic infusion of a PPARa
activator (). These observations suggest that FAS,
which is known to be regulated by nutrition, is involved
in the generation of endogenous PPARa ligands. The
same research group demonstrated that FAS generates
-palmitoyl--oleoyl-sn-glycerol--phosphocholine,
which seems to be a putative PPARa ligand ().
Other natural compounds such as polyphenols
have been identiﬁed as ligands of PPARa (, ).
PPARa activation with resveratrol, a natural poly-
phenol, and some of its derivatives and analogs re-
sults in brain protection against stroke (, ).
Resveratrol was identiﬁed as an AMPK activator,
linking this sensor of the nutritional status again to
PPARa functionality () (see “Modulating Factors
of PPARa Activity”). Because PPARa acts as an in-
tracellular sensor of FAs levels, of which an alteration
is associated with the development of several diseases
such as diabetes, obesity, atherosclerosis, and in-
ﬂammation, ﬁnding a way of regulating the receptor
activity through modulating (levels of) endogenous
ligands may have the potential to generate new
therapies.

PPARa Target Genes and Their Function
Numerous studies with PPARa-deﬁcient mice during
the past years have demonstrated the critical roles
played by this receptor in energy metabolism, hepatic
functions, inﬂammation, cardiac pathophysiology, cell
cycle alterations, and rodent hepatocarcinogenesis
(). We next discuss functionalities of PPARa target
genes and proteins per tissue type. Table (, , ,
–) summarizes the many genes that have been
identiﬁed as PPARa target genes in diﬀerent organs
since the end of the s. We have indicated whether
the regulation was found to be direct or indirect.
PPARa target genes in liver
The PPARa subtype exerts its main functions in the
liver, where the receptor is involved in various aspects
of lipid metabolism. These functions range from
FA transport, binding, uptake, synthesis, degradation
through mitochondrial or peroxisomal oxidation,
storage, lipoprotein metabolism, and ketogenesis
under fasting conditions (). Hence, although the
phenotype of PPARa-deﬁcient mice is normal, fasting
results in increased plasma-free FA () levels, ac-
cumulation of lipids in the liver, hypoketonemia,
hypoglycemia, and hypothermia (, , , ).
The wide range of genes that are expressed after
PPARa activation indicates that this receptor has a
central regulatory role in lipid metabolism ().
Recently, in a hepatocyte-speciﬁc PPARa knockout
mouse model impairment in liver and whole-body FA
homeostasis was observed, resulting in hepatic lipid
accumulation and hypercholesterolemia during aging
(). Still today, novel mechanisms are discovered.
The molecular pathway that leads to the upregulation
of PPARa during fasting was recently investigated by
Maus et al. (). They found that FFAs bind to PLCg-
coupled cell surface receptors, such as the FA trans-
porter CD, in the liver and heart. This binding
activates a speciﬁc pathway that leads to Ca+ inﬂux
across the plasma membrane, called store-operated
Ca+ entry. Subsequently, increased intracellular Ca+
levels induce in a cAMP-dependent manner PGC-a
and PPARa expression, ultimately leading to a
transcriptional reprogramming of the cell to FA uti-
lization ().
Collectively, these data demonstrate that PPARa
is a master regulator of FA catabolism by governing
the regulation of key enzymes involved in peroxi-
somal, mitochondrial, and microsomal FAO.

proliferators. For example, in humans, lower expression

peroxisome proliferation upon treatment with ﬁbrate

Overall it is surprising that there are so few therapeutic
applications for ﬁbrates, considering the important role
that PPARa has in regulating hepatic energy meta-
bolism in response to the fasted state. Furthermore,
although we have good evidence for cross-talk between
PPARa and inﬂammatory signaling pathways, in-

being developed to gain greater pharmacological eﬀect
(, ). A restricted activation potential can be
explained at the molecular level by a general decrease
in aﬃnity for cofactors due to partial activation,
binding to diﬀerent target gene promoters, and, but

common feature of atherosclerotic cardiovascular

necessity for complementary therapies. Pemaﬁbrate

Ligand activation of PPARa also antagonizes NF-kB

Epidermal Langerhans cells (LCs) play a pivotal
role in initiating and maintaining primary immune
responses in the skin. PPARa is expressed in im-
mature LCs but downregulated in mature LCs.
Pharmacological PPARa activation inhibits LC mat-
uration, migratory capacity, cytokine expression, and
the ability to drive T cell proliferation. Moreover,
PPARa in LCs also inhibits NF-kB activity. Taken
together, PPARa activation by endogenous ligands
may provide a molecular signal that allows LCs to
remain in an immature state (). The activation of
PPARs by drugs, as well as products of intermediary
energy metabolism within the diverse cells of the
immune system, may reveal new opportunities to
target inﬂammatory process or to advance adjunctive
therapies to enhance anticancer treatment.

PPARa in the metabolic syndrome as an

skin healing via a modulation of the inﬂammatory
phase (). Additionally, PPARa activation is ben-

skin disorders and pave the way for trials of topical
PPARa agonist application.

The paradox of PPARa as a
proinﬂammatory mediator
The above studies (Fig. ) are in line with the well-
accepted notion that PPARa is involved primarily in
anti-inﬂammatory signaling. As stated in the in-
troduction to this section, PPARa has also been as-
cribed potential “pro-inﬂammatory” eﬀects (Fig. ).
Lee et al. () suggested that oxidized phospholipids
induce MCP-and IL-expression through a PPARa-
dependent signaling cascade in human aortic endo-
thelial cells. These data demonstrate that PPARa can
mediate the activation of endothelial cells by stimu-
lating the production of monocyte chemotactic activity
in response to oxidized phospholipids and lipopro-
teins ().
Additionally, PPARa activation by Wy-,
enhances IL-b–induced proinﬂammatory cytokine
release, including IL-, IL-, and TNF-a, in ocular cells
and induces proangiogenic responses (). In con-
trast, in the FIELD and ACCORD studies (, )
ﬁbrates showed protection against diabetic retinopa-
thy. Concordantly, the antiangiogenic activity of
PPARa ligands speciﬁcally depended on the activation
of PPARa because PPARa ligands inhibited FGF
corneal neovascularization in wild-type but not in
PPARa-deﬁcient mice (). Consistent with these
data, fenoﬁbrate was shown to improve diabetic ret-
inopathy in type diabetes animal models and
ischemia-induced retinal neovascularization. Here-
with, PPARa dependency also formed the mechanistic
basis for the therapeutic eﬀects of fenoﬁbrate ().
The anti-inﬂammatory eﬀects of fenoﬁbrate-activated
PPARa were ascribed to the inhibition of NF-kB
signaling in the diabetic retina (). The basis for
these diﬀerences remains unclear but might reﬂect a
reduced complexity of in vitro studies vs in vivo
studies.
Then again, in a mouse model of endotoxemia,
fenoﬁbrate administration strongly increased cir-
culating LPS-induced TNF levels (). In a rat
hypertensive model, bezaﬁbrate was shown to
sensitize for endotoxin eﬀects, concomitant with
low levels of HDL and increased levels of TG ().
In murine macrophage lines and in contrast to
Wy-,, the natural PPARa ligands LTBand

increasing NOS activity (). These observations
are again not restricted to PPARa, because PPARg
activity in T cells and dendritic cells promotes Th
immunity (, ). These are interesting ﬁndings
suggesting that PPARa activation may result in
diﬀerent responses depending on the inﬂammatory
settings, an eﬀect that may involve combinatorial
signaling. Although the mechanisms by which such
potentially “pro-inﬂammatory” eﬀects occur is not
understood, these data point to the need for a
careful evaluation of the eﬀects of PPARa agonists
in the relevant cell types, and in diﬀerent physio-
logical contexts. How to reconcile these seemingly
contradictory ﬁndings with the wealth of data on
anti-inﬂammatory PPAR actions is as of yet unclear.
For instance, reduced PPARa expression has also
been linked to a lower overall survival and an en-
hanced tissue bacterial load in sepsis (). The
timing and nature of the proinﬂammatory stimulus
and/or treatment may play a decisive role. It may
well be that PPARa exerts a context-dependent
immune system priming role, as has been docu-
mented recently also for GCs (). Given that GR
and PPARa are able to physically interact and able
to cocontrol genes involved in both inﬂammatory
and metabolic pathways (, ) increases the
likelihood of this assumption. More research is
clearly necessary to disentangle and explain these
paradoxical observations. This hitherto hidden role
of PPARa not only merits further mechanistic at-
tention but also requires a better understanding at
the level of physiological relevance.
Future Perspectives
On account of their pleiotropic eﬀects, PPARs may be
targets in a number of disease conditions. It is clear
there is still a lot of ground to cover, foremost by
studying novel generations of more potent and better
tolerated single, dual, or pan-PPAR agonists in the
clinic. More basic research involving novel PPARa
agonists is important, as many mechanistic aspects
diﬀer from the ﬁrst generation of PPARa agonists.
Plus, novel ﬁelds of application may still arise, for
which the ﬁrst generation of drugs did not show
therapeutic beneﬁt.
Fibrates might be beneﬁcial in the prevention of
coronary heart disease. The highest beneﬁt is achieved
in cardiovascular disease patients with high TGs and
low HDL-C (). A clinical beneﬁt of PPARa acti-
vation with novel agonists remains to be demonstrated
in patients with high TGs/low HDL and residual
cardiovascular risk. Fibrates might be useful in the
treatment and prevention of atherosclerosis, and es-
pecially the new ﬁbrates with greater potency open
new perspectives. However, it remains unclear whether
ﬁbrates in general are useful in the treatment or
prevention of heart failure.
It is now evident that the eﬀects of PPARa are
much more widespread and cover numerous aspects
