Abstract
Objective and design  Temporomandibular disorder (TMD) is a common painful condition in the temporomandibular joint 
(TMJ). Joint inflammation is believed to be a chief cause of pain in patients with TMD, through the release of pro-inflam-
matory cytokines that induce peripheral sensitization of nerve terminals followed by microglial stimulation.
Materials and subject  TMJ was induced in rats with the injection of complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA) emulsion into the 
left TMJ capsule.
Treatment  The present study would assess the effects of micronized palmitoylethanolamide (m-PEA) on glial activation 
and trigeminal hypersensitivity.
Methods  Ten mg/kg m-PEA or corresponding vehicle was administered 1 h after CFA and mechanical allodynia and edema 
were evaluated at 24 and 72 h after CFA injection.
Results  CFA-injected animals showed TMJ edema and ipsilateral mechanical allodynia accompanied by a robust growth 
in GFAP protein-positive satellite glial cells and activation of resident macrophages in the TG. Moreover, m-PEA adminis-
tration significantly reduced the degree of TMJ damage and pain, macrophage activation in TG and up-regulation of Iba1.
Conclusions  The results confirm that m-PEA could represent a novel approach for monitoring pain during trigeminal nerve 
sensitization.

Introduction
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a complex and highly prevalent dis-
ease affecting about 10% of US adult population [1] and 
a primary reason of disability [2]. An increasing evidence 
indicates a crucial role of inflammatory stimuli in the patho-
genesis and progression of OA. Among the clinical prob-
lems associated to OA [1], pain is markedly important: it 
involves both neuropathic and nociceptive components and 
it is linked to local and central sensitization and therefore, 
also to the possible development of chronic pain [3].
OA commonly affects the temporomandibular joint 
(TMJ), often leading to severe pain and to a limitation 
of the jaw range of motion. Persistent non-resolving-TMJ 
inflammation is consequent to a dysregulation of neu-
ron–non-neuronal cell interaction [4–6]. The satellite glial 
cells surrounding the trigeminal ganglion (TG) neurons 
as well as microglia and astrocytes in trigeminal spinal

Changes in this communication following noxious stimuli

The use of compounds that relieve inflammation process 
might limit the development of peripheral and central sen-
sitization, avoiding the progression from acute to chronic 
pain and the worsening of OA. A new pharmacological 
strategy to restore physiological glia functions includes 
fatty acid ethanolamines (FAEs), a promising family of 
naturally occurring lipid mediators formed on demand to 
decrease inflammation and restore tissue homeostasis. The 
principal members of this family are the endocannabinoid 
N-arachidonoylethanolamine (anandamide) and its conge-
ners N-stearoylethanolamine, N-oleoylethanolamine and 
N-palmitoylethanolamine (PEA). Among these, PEA has 
emerged as an innovative therapeutic strategy to control 
the mast cell–microglia axis, showing anti-inflammatory, 
pain-relieving and neuroprotective properties. In that 
regard, PEA showed great efficacy in the treatment of pain 
and inflammation. Several of previous works demonstrated 
the beneficial effects of PEA alone and in combination 
in different models of inflammation and pain [10, 11], 
and in a mouse model of CIA [12]. The exact mechanism 
of action of PEA is not well known although PEA could 
interact with peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor 
(PPAR)-α, which are involved in its anti-inflammatory 
effects and with the orphan G-protein-coupled receptor, 
GPR55 [13]. Interestingly, previous studies also indicated 
that PPAR-δ and PPAR-γ can contribute to the anti-inflam-
matory activity of PEA [14].
Moreover, a recent study investigated the effects of a 
micronized formulation of PEA (m-PEA, Normast 300) 
vs ibuprofen on temporomandibular joint (TMJ) pain, 
whereby a greater enhancement in pain relief and range 
of motion in the PEA group was associated to long-last-
ing efficacy. In addition, the observed correlation between 
inflammatory pain and activation of glial cells in the TG 
and central nervous system [9] suggests that glial cells 
could be the pharmacological targets of molecules with 
pain-relieving effects, such as m-PEA. m-PEA is obtained 
through a micronization technique to achieve microparti-
cles (< 10 µm), that increased surface area and rate of dis-
solution, together with a reduced variability of absorption. 
Based on these evidence, here we assessed the properties 
of m-PEA on glial cell activation following induction of 
TMJ inflammation and trigeminal hypersensitivity.
Materials and methods
Animals
The study was conducted using Sprague–Dawley male rats 
(200–230 g, Envigo, Italy). The animals were fed standard 
laboratory chow and allowed free access to water in an 
air-conditioned room with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle.
All procedures performed in animals were in accord-
ance with the ethical standards of the Italian regulations 
on the protection of animals used for experimental and 
other scientific purposes (D.M.116192) as well as with 
EEC regulations (O.J. of E.C. L 358/1 12/18/1986).
Induction of TMJ inflammation
To achieve the activation of ganglia satellite glial cells and 
spinal microglia, the induction of TMJ inflammation was 
performed as previously reported [9, 15–17].
Experimental groups
Rats were randomly divided into the following groups:
Sham group Rats received an equal volume of saline 
into the left TMJ capsule using the same technique.
Sham + mPEA group Rats received m-PEA adminis-
tered intraperitoneally (ip) at a dose of 10 mg/kg;
CIA group Rats were subjected to TMJ inflammation by 
administering 50 µl of CFA oil/saline (1:1) emulsion into 
the left TMJ capsule;
CIA + mPEA group Rats were subjected to TMJ as 
described above and were treated by ip administration of 
m-PEA at a dose of 10 mg/kg 1 h post-CFA.
Rats were killed, respectively, at 24 and 72 h after CFA 
or saline injection. Only male rats were used because they 
present a higher nociceptive response to CFA-induced 
inflammation [18], probably due to an anti-inflammatory 
effects of estrogens in female rats.
The minimum number of mice for every technique was 
estimated with the statistical test a priori power studies 
with the G-power software, this statistical test supplies a 
professional method to analyze the sample size required 
to make the experiments.
Micronized PEA preparation and treatment
m-PEA was provided by Epitech Group SpA. m-PEA 
was resuspended in 5% Pluronic F-68 to preserve parti-
cle size distribution of the lipidic molecule. m-PEA was 
administered ip at a dose of 10 mg/kg 1 h post-CFA or

Behavioral test
Mechanical allodynia was measured using a previously

Measurement of Evans blue dye extravasation
Evans blue dye was injected into the tail vein [19] 10 min 
before perfusion (see below). The left TMJ was dissected

temperature in a 7:3 (v/v) mixture of acetone and 35.2 mM

fuged, the supernatant collected and dye absorbance deter-
mined spectrophotometrically at 620 nm.
Tissue processing for histological experiments
Twenty-four and 72 h after CFA injection, rats were killed 
and both brain and TG were excised and fixed in 10% (w/v) 
formaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Eight-
micrometer sections were prepared from paraffin-embedded 
tissues.
Immunofluorescence for GFAP/NeuN, Iba1 and ED1
After deparaffinization and rehydration the detection of 
glial fibrillary acidic protein and neuron-specific nuclear 
protein (GFAP/NeuN), of the number and of the activation 
of macrophages phenotype (Iba1 and ED1) were carried out 
in ophthalmic (V1), maxillary (V2) and mandibular (V3) 
TG divisions after boiling in 0.1 M citrate buffer for one 
minute. Sections were incubated with polyclonal rabbit 
anti-GFAP (1:100, vol/vol Santa Cruz Biotechnology), poly-
clonal mouse anti-Iba1 (1:100, vol/vol Santa Cruz Biotech-
nology), polyclonal mouse anti-ED1 (1:100, vol/vol Santa 
Cruz Biotechnology) and mouse anti-NeuN (1:100; vol/
vol Santa Cruz Biotechnology) antibodies in a humidified 
oxygen and nitrogen chamber over night at 37 °C. Sections 
were incubated with a secondary antibody fluorescein iso-
thiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated anti-mouse Alexa Fluor-488 
antibody (1:2,000 vol/vol Molecular Probes, Monza, Italy) 
and with TEXAS RED-conjugated anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor-
594 antibody (1:1000 in PBS, vol/vol Molecular Probes, 
Monza, Italy) for 1 h at 37 °C. For nuclear staining, 2 µg/
ml 4′,6′-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Hoechst, Frank-
furt, Germany) in PBS was added. Sections were observed 
at 20× magnification using a Leica DM2000 microscope 
(Leica, Milan, Italy).
Quantitative analysis of immunopositive cells in the TG 
was carried out by using the Image-J software. The number 
of positive cells in TG was calculated on sections acquired at 
10× magnification. The perineuronal regions of the V1, V2 
and V3 division were traced, and the number was calculated. 
The number of positive cells has been normalized to the area 
of measurement.
Immunohistochemical localization of GFAP and Iba1
After deparaffinization, endogenous peroxidase was 
quenched with 0.3% (v/v) hydrogen peroxide in 60% (v/v) 
methanol for 30 min. Brainstem and spinal cord sections 
(three sections for each sample) were permeabilized with 
0.1% (w/v) Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min. Non-specific 
adsorption was minimized by incubating the section in 2% 
(v/v) normal goat serum in PBS for 20 min. Endogenous 
biotin or avidin binding sites were blocked by sequential 
incubation for 15 min with biotin and avidin (Santa Cruz, 
Milan, Italy), respectively. Sections were incubated over-
night with rabbit anti-GFAP (1:100; Santa Cruz Biotechnol-
ogy) or rabbit anti-Iba1 (1:100; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). 
Sections were then washed with PBS and incubated with 
secondary antibody. Specific labeling was detected with 
a biotin-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG and avidin–bio-
tin peroxidase complex. The counter stain was developed 
with diaminobenzidine (brown) and nuclear fast red (red 
background). To confirm the specificity of GFAP immuno-
reactivity, some sections were incubated with anti-GFAP 
antibody in the presence of excess GFAP (10 mM). Binding 
specificity for Iba1 was assessed by incubating with primary 
or secondary antibody only. No positive immunostaining 
was seen under these conditions. Acquired immunohis-
tochemical images (n = 5) were assessed by densitometry 
using an imaging densitometer (AxioVision, Zeiss, Milan, 
Italy) and computer program.
Statistical analysis
All values are expressed as mean ± standard error of the 
mean (SEM) of n observations. For in vivo studies “n” rep-
resents the number of animals used.
The results were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed 
by a Bonferroni post hoc test for multiple comparisons. A p 
value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

The present results identify the involvement of differ-
ent non-neuronal cells in m-PEA-induced reduction of 
mechanical allodynia that include peripheral macrophages 
and central microglia. These data confirm that m-PEA may 
also act on central targets to reduce central sensitization 
consequent to peripheral inflammatory stimulation. On the 
other hand, the smaller particle size of m-PEA is asso-
ciated with a higher surface-to-volume ratio which may 
contribute to better solubility of lipophilic molecules like 
PEA [11, 28, 29].
The capacity of PEA to reduce both inflammation and 
neuroinflammation, mainly by regulating peripheral and 
central non-neuronal cells that result in pain relief, is docu-
mented in a growing number of studies. PEA is produced/
hydrolyzed by glial cells and mast cells [30–32]. It down-
modulates mast cell activation [33, 34], and controls glial 
cell behaviors [35]. The administration of PEA in micro-
nized and ultramicronized formulations is reported to reduce 
pain behaviors and to inhibit somatosensory activation in 
different models of acute and chronic pain [6].
The results obtained here demonstrated an attenuation 
of trigeminal peripheral ending sensitization along with 
down-regulation of glial cells and resident macrophage acti-
vation by m-PEA treatment, suggesting a possible option 
for management of TMJ osteoarthritis by modulating the 
active inflammatory process, preserving function and further 
deformity and by relieving pain. However, a long-lasting 
study aimed both at verifying the contribution of the differ-
ent glial and immunocompetent cells in the different phases 
of inflammatory response and the ability of m-PEA to con-
trol the activation of non-neuronal cells also during chronic 
TMJ neuroinflammation.
Furthermore, the results obtained also support a poten-
tial use of m-PEA for management of craniofacial pain and 
headache. This idea is supported by the marked improve-
ment in a female patient affected by occipital neuralgia after 
oral intake of ultramicronized PEA [36], and by a previous 
study demonstrating that m-PEA was more effective than 
ibuprofen in the treatment of TMJ inflammatory pain [37]. 
In this last study, pain intensity progressively decreased in 
the m-PEA treated group, confirming a lack of tolerance 
with chronic treatment. The effect of m-PEA in TMJ pain 
was superior to that achieved with ibuprofen, suggesting that 
m-PEA could act on the neuropathic component of TMJ 
pain, which is mainly mediated by persistent activation of 
non-neuronal cells, in particular brain microglia.
In conclusion, the findings reported here confirm the 
important contribution of non-neuronal cells in TMJ inflam-
mation and pain and the possible application of m-PEA as 
an innovative therapeutic agent to decrease inflammation 
thereby reducing trigeminal hypersensitivity.
